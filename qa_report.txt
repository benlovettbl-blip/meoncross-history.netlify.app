Lesson 1: lesson_1_1 - KT1.1: The Origins of the Republic, 1918–1919
========================================

--- DO NOW (Checks Previous Knowledge) ---
Q1: List the ways in which a major conflict like the First World War might weaken a country's government and economy.
A: A major conflict drains government finances through massive war debts and printing money (causing inflation). It destroys the workforce due to high casualties, and blockades cause civilian starvation. This widespread misery destroys public faith in the government, often triggering strikes, mutinies, and political revolutions.
Q2: Define: What is an 'autocracy', and who held this absolute power in Germany before 1918?
A: An autocracy is a system of government where one person has absolute, unchecked power. Before 1918, this power in Germany was held by Kaiser Wilhelm II, who could make laws and control the army without needing the parliament's approval.
Q3: Recall: What were the typical beliefs and policies of extreme left-wing and right-wing political groups in the early 20th century?
A: Extreme left-wing groups (like Communists) believed in violent revolution to overthrow capitalism and create a society where wealth and property were shared equally. Extreme right-wing groups (like Nationalists/Fascists) believed in strong authoritarian leadership, extreme patriotism, a powerful military, and the destruction of communism.
Q4: 15 Second Challenge: Try to speak for 15 seconds non-stop about what you already know regarding Germany's defeat in the First World War.
A: Students should mention: the Allied naval blockade causing starvation, the failure of the final German spring offensive in 1918, the entry of the USA into the war with fresh troops, the Kiel Mutiny, and the ultimate surrender on 11 November 1918.

Lesson 5: lesson_2_1 - KT2.1: The Early Development of the Nazi Party, 1919–1922
========================================

--- DO NOW (Checks Previous Knowledge) ---
Q1: What was the 'stab-in-the-back' myth (Dolchstoßlegende), and who was blamed for it?
A: The myth that the German army was not defeated in battle but betrayed by politicians on the home front; the 'November Criminals' (socialists, democrats, and Jews) were blamed.
Q2: What is a 'Putsch'?
A: A violent attempt to overthrow a government.
Q3: Who were the Freikorps, and what political ideology did they fiercely oppose?
A: Right-wing, nationalist ex-soldiers who violently opposed Communism.
Q4: 15 Second Challenge: Try to speak for 15 seconds non-stop about the terms of the Treaty of Versailles and why German nationalists hated it.
A: It imposed massive reparations, limited the army to 100,000 men, stripped Germany of its empire, and included the humiliating War Guilt Clause (Article 231).

Lesson 6: lesson_2_2 - KT2.2: The Munich Putsch and the Lean Years, 1923–1929
========================================

--- DO NOW (Checks Previous Knowledge) ---
Q1: What was the name of the elite, highly trusted unit selected from the SA to act as Hitler's personal bodyguard in 1923?
A: The Stosstrupp (Shock Troop).
Q2: What does the German word 'Putsch' mean?
A: A violent attempt to overthrow a government or seize political power.
Q3: Who was the Chancellor of Germany in late 1923 who called off passive resistance in the Ruhr?
A: Gustav Stresemann.
Q4: 15 Second Challenge: Try to speak for 15 seconds non-stop about how hyperinflation created 'winners' and 'losers' in German society.
A: Winners included those with debts (which became worthless) and businessmen who took out loans to buy businesses. Losers included anyone with savings, fixed incomes, or pensions, as their money became completely worthless.

Lesson 7: lesson_2_3 - KT2.3: The Growth of Nazi Support, 1929–1932
========================================

--- DO NOW (Checks Previous Knowledge) ---
Q1: What was Gustav Stresemann's famous quote warning about the fragility of the German economy during the 'Golden Age'?
A: He warned that Germany was 'dancing on a volcano' because its recovery was entirely reliant on short-term American loans.
Q2: What was the central message of the Führerprinzip (Leadership Principle)?
A: The belief that Germany needed a single, strong, undisputed leader (Führer) with absolute power, rather than weak democratic debate.
Q3: How many seats did the Nazi Party win in the 1928 Reichstag elections, proving they were an electoral failure before the Depression?
A: Only 12 seats.
Q4: 15 Second Challenge: Try to speak for 15 seconds non-stop about how Hitler completely reorganised the Nazi Party after he was released from Landsberg Prison.
A: He created national headquarters, divided Germany into regions run by Gauleiters, set up the SS as his personal bodyguard, and created the Hitler Youth to target the young.

Lesson 8: lesson_2_4 - KT2.4: How Hitler Became Chancellor, 1932–1933
========================================

--- DO NOW (Checks Previous Knowledge) ---
Q1: How many votes did Hitler win in the 1932 Presidential election against Hindenburg?
A: 13.4 million votes.
Q2: What is meant by the historical term 'negative cohesion'?
A: Supporting a party because you share the same fears/hatreds (e.g. fear of communism), not necessarily the same beliefs.
Q3: Who was the Weimar Chancellor from 1930–1932 known bitterly as the 'Hunger Chancellor'?
A: Heinrich Brüning.
Q4: 15 Second Challenge: Try to speak for 15 seconds non-stop about how the SA helped the Nazis gain middle-class votes during the Great Depression.
A: The SA deliberately fought the communists in the streets, but then marched in disciplined uniforms, making the Nazis look like the only party organised enough to restore order and crush communism, which the middle classes feared.

Lesson 9: lesson_3_1 - Key Topic 3.1: The Creation of a Dictatorship, 1933–1934
========================================

--- DO NOW (Checks Previous Knowledge) ---
Q1: On what exact date did Paul von Hindenburg appoint Adolf Hitler as Chancellor of Germany?
A: 30 January 1933.
Q2: What is meant by the historical term 'backstairs intrigue'?
A: The secret plotting and political deal-making between elite politicians (like Papen, Schleicher, and Hindenburg) that brought Hitler to power, rather than a democratic election victory.
Q3: How many Nazis were actually allowed in Hitler's very first Cabinet in January 1933?
A: Only three (including Hitler).
Q4: 15 Second Challenge: Try to speak for 15 seconds non-stop about why Franz von Papen believed he could control Hitler in 1933.
A: Papen believed he could 'tame' Hitler by offering him the Chancellorship but surrounding him with conservative ministers, famously claiming 'we will push him into a corner until he squeaks'.

Lesson 10: lesson_3_2 - Key Topic 3.2: The Police State and Religion, 1933–1939
========================================

--- DO NOW (Checks Previous Knowledge) ---
Q1: What was the name of the law passed in March 1933 that effectively allowed Hitler to rule Germany as a dictator for four years?
A: The Enabling Act.
Q2: What is meant by the historical term Gleichschaltung?
A: The process of Nazification by which Hitler brought all aspects of German society and government under Nazi control.
Q3: On what exact date did Hitler launch the Night of the Long Knives?
A: 30 June 1934.
Q4: 15 Second Challenge: Try to speak for 15 seconds non-stop about why Hitler felt he had to murder Ernst Röhm.
A: Röhm led the SA which had 3 million members; he wanted a 'second revolution' to redistribute wealth; he wanted the SA to replace the regular army, which alienated the powerful army generals Hitler needed.

Lesson 11: lesson_3_3 - Key Topic 3.3: Controlling and Influencing Attitudes, 1933–1939
========================================

--- DO NOW (Checks Previous Knowledge) ---
Q1: What was the name of the government department set up in 1935 to weaken the power of Christianity?
A: The Ministry of Church Affairs.
Q2: What is meant by the German term Schutzhaft (Protective Custody)?
A: A Nazi legal loophole allowing the Gestapo to imprison anyone indefinitely without trial.
Q3: Who was the brutal judge placed in charge of the People's Court?

Q4: 15 Second Challenge: Try to speak for 15 seconds non-stop about the difference between the SS, the SD, and the Gestapo.
A: The SS were the main paramilitary bodyguards who ran the police and camps; the SD were the intelligence wing who kept index cards; the Gestapo were the non-uniformed secret police who arrested people.

LESSON 5 5 KT5.5: Surgical Breakthroughs: The Thomas Splint, Wound Debridement & Mobile X-Rays (1914–1918)
Layer 2: Plate 1 (Act 2) Narrative Visual
Caption: The Thomas Splint in Practice: Designed by Welsh orthopaedic surgeon Hugh Owen Thomas and introduced to the Western Front in 1915 by his nephew Robert Jones. Continuous tension on the leg kept broken femur bone ends aligned, preventing severed femoral arteries and reducing fracture mortality from 80% to 20%.
File: /images/thomas_splint_authentic.jpg
Layer 2: Plate 2 (Act 4) Narrative Visual
Caption: Mobile Radiography Unit (1917): Converted motor vans equipped with petrol-driven electric generators, darkroom equipment, and radiographic tubes allowed RAMC radiologists to image wounded soldiers within miles of the front, pinpointing shrapnel fragments prior to surgery.
File: /images/mobile_xray_field_hospital_1917.jpg

Badge: TRAUMA SURGERY: CASUALTY CLEARING STATION
Operating theatre suite inside No. 23 Casualty Clearing Station on the Western Front. Multiple operating tables with surgical instruments and overhead illumination enabled specialist surgical teams to perform urgent abdominal laparotomies and debridement simultaneously.
Citation: Imperial War Museum, London (IWM Q 33441 / Public Domain).

Layer 3: Source C Primary Visual
Source C (Photograph): Marie Curie at the Wheel of a Mobile Field Radiography Car ('Petite Curie') on the Western Front (c.1915)
Badge: MOBILE RADIOLOGY: THE 'PETITES CURIES'
Marie Curie at the wheel of a mobile radiographic car ('petite Curie') on the Western Front (c.1915). Equipped with a petrol dynamo, portable X-ray apparatus, and photographic darkroom supplies, these mobile radiological vans brought diagnostic imaging directly to frontline dressing stations and casualty clearing units, enabling surgeons to locate bullets and shrapnel before operating.

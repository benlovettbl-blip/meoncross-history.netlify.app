Key Stage 3 Curriculum Intent & Pedagogical Architecture

1. Curriculum Rationale & Local Maritime Heritage

Our Key Stage 3 history curriculum is intentionally constructed to link national and global historical narratives with the distinctive maritime geography and lived environment of The History Portal pupils. From the industrial revolution of the Fareham ironmaster (Henry Cort at Funtley) to the strategic supremacy of Portsmouth Royal Dockyard and the profound sacrifices recorded on the Stubbington village war memorials, history is made tangible, relevant, and deeply rooted in our coastal community.

2. Disciplinary Progression & Historical Enquiry

Across Years 7, 8, and 9, disciplinary thinking progresses sequentially to secure the foundational skills required for GCSE excellence:

  • Chronological Rigour & Global Encounters: De-centering traditional anglocentric narratives by examining early global trade, Indigenous perspectives in Australia, and African resistance to the Transatlantic Slave Trade alongside British social transformations.
  • Causation & Consequence: Moving from simple direct triggers in Year 7 to evaluating multi-tiered, long-term geopolitical catalysts (e.g. European alliance systems, naval arms races).
  • Source Utility & Forensic Evidence: Progressing from basic comprehension to rigorous interrogation of provenance, evaluating authorial motive, audience, and historical reliability.
  • Historiographical Debate: Engaging pupils with active academic debates (e.g., assessing the Magna Carta, or challenging the 'Lions led by Donkeys' narrative of the Great War) grounded in historical evidence.

3. Adaptive Teaching, Dual Coding & Inclusion

Pupils work through bespoke printed A4 workbooks closely paired with our digital learning portal. Standardized PEE/PEEL Structure Strips, Causal Connective Banks, consistent dual coding, and digital accessibility features offload working memory, ensuring all SEND and lower-attaining pupils achieve ambitious historical progress with zero cognitive overload.

Lesson Number & Title Learning Objectives & Enquiry Curriculum Rigour: Evidence, Debate & Assessment
Lesson 1
How much progress did the Romans make in public health?
🎯 Change & Continuity
  • Describe the simple, practical sanitation methods used by Iron Age Britons.
  • Explain how Roman engineers introduced revolutionary sanitation technology.
  • Evaluate the significance of Roman bathhouses and latrines for public health.
Hinge Q: How did the Romans manage to keep the water flowing constantly into their forts?
📜 Primary Evidence & Source Analysis:
Housesteads Fort Roman communal latrines (Hadrian's Wall); Seneca's moral treatise on Roman baths; Fishbourne Roman Palace mosaics
⚖️ Disciplinary Debate / Historian Focus:
Roman engineering triumphalism vs lived public health reality: lead piping & parasite transmission
✍️ Core Written Assessment:
Write a PEEL paragraph explaining how the Romans tried to keep their towns clean.
Lesson 2
Why did public health decline during the Middle Ages?
🎯 Historical Evidence & Monastic Life
  • Describe the simple cesspits used by peasants in rural Medieval villages.
  • Explain why Medieval monasteries built complex water systems.
  • Evaluate the role of gongfermers and night-work in medieval towns.
Hinge Q: Why were medieval drinking wells so dangerous?
📜 Primary Evidence & Source Analysis:
Canterbury Cathedral Waterworks Plan (c. 1165); City of London Sanitary Ordinance of 1357; Monastic reredorter latrine architecture
⚖️ Disciplinary Debate / Historian Focus:
Medieval hygiene myths: Religious purity and monastic engineering vs urban municipal paralysis
✍️ Core Written Assessment:
Write a PEEL paragraph explaining why public health in towns got worse during the Middle Ages.
Lesson 3
To what extent did towns become filthier during the Early Modern period?
🎯 Causation & Etiological Beliefs
  • Describe the sanitation problems in growing Tudor and Stuart towns.
  • Explain why early flushing toilets like Sir John Harington's failed to catch on.
  • Evaluate how Samuel Pepys's diary reveals the reality of Early Modern sanitation.
Hinge Q: Why did the rivers in early modern towns become open sewers?
📜 Primary Evidence & Source Analysis:
Sir John Harington's 1596 'Ajax' Toilet Diagram; Samuel Pepys' 1660 Diary on cellar privies; The Cowdray Engraving of Tudor Portsmouth (1545)
⚖️ Disciplinary Debate / Historian Focus:
Laissez-faire policy debate & Roy Porter on early modern infrastructure limits; technological innovation vs municipal failure
✍️ Core Written Assessment:
[Evaluative Enquiry] How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into how London authorities and medical practitioners attempted to control the 1665 Great Plague?
Lesson 4
How did the Industrial Revolution lead to a public health crisis?
🎯 Historical Significance & State Intervention
  • Describe the overcrowded and unhygienic conditions of industrial back-to-back housing.
  • Explain how Edwin Chadwick argued for public health reform in his 1842 report.
  • Evaluate Dr. John Snow's discovery that cholera was waterborne.
Hinge Q: Why did the rapid building of factories lead to a housing crisis?
📜 Primary Evidence & Source Analysis:
Chadwick's 1842 Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population; Dr John Snow's Broad Street Cholera Spot Map (1854); Portsmouth cholera burial registers
⚖️ Disciplinary Debate / Historian Focus:
Industrial urbanisation: Miasma theory vs early contagionist epidemiology; laissez-faire vs state health intervention
✍️ Core Written Assessment:
[Evaluative Enquiry] How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into why Victorian medical authorities resisted John Snow’s discovery that cholera was water-borne?
Lesson 5
Why did it take the 'Great Stink' to finally clean up Britain's streets?
🎯 Source Utility & Epidemiological Mapping
  • Describe the events of the Great Stink in 1858.
  • Explain how Joseph Bazalgette's sewer system transformed London.
  • Evaluate how Louis Pasteur's Germ Theory revolutionized our understanding of disease.
Hinge Q: Why did Parliament suddenly care about public health in 1858?
📜 Primary Evidence & Source Analysis:
Punch Cartoon 'The Silent Highwayman' (1858 Great Stink); Bazalgette's Victorian intercepting sewer blueprints (1859–1865); Eastney Beam Engine Pumping Station, Portsmouth: Civic Sanitation Masterpiece (1887)
⚖️ Disciplinary Debate / Historian Focus:
The Great Stink of 1858: Parliamentary self-interest and physical stench vs municipal laissez-faire ideology
✍️ Core Written Assessment:
Write a PEEL paragraph explaining how Joseph Bazalgette's sewers improved public health in London.
Lesson 6
End of Unit Assessment: Cholera Investigation
🎯 Turning Point Analysis & Civil Engineering
  • Evaluate how Dr John Snow used map data and scientific deduction to prove cholera was waterborne in 1854.
  • Explain the shift from laissez-faire government attitudes to compulsory state intervention in British public health.
  • Produce a structured 8-mark explanation assessing the significance of Victorian sanitation reforms.
📜 Primary Evidence & Source Analysis:
Dr John Snow's 1854 Broad Street Cholera Spot Map; Soho mortality records & Eley Brothers percussion cap factory logs
⚖️ Disciplinary Debate / Historian Focus:
Snow's empirical epidemiological mapping vs the entrenched medical establishment and Board of Health
✍️ Core Written Assessment:
Explain the significance of John Snow's discovery during the 1854 Broad Street Cholera outbreak. (8 marks)

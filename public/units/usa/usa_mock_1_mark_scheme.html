Paper 3: Conflict at Home and Abroad: the USA, 1954–75 (1HI0/33)
Question 1: Inference from Source A (4 Marks)
Target: AO3 (4 marks). Award 1 mark for each valid inference, up to 2. Award 1 mark for each corresponding quote/detail, up to 2.
Sample Acceptable Inferences: • Inference 1: Southern officials deliberately created impossible barriers to block Black citizens from exercising their constitutional voting rights. - Detail from source: "Local registrars administer arbitrary 'literacy exams' that require Black applicants to interpret intricate constitutional clauses..." • Inference 2: Black citizens faced economic reprisal and terrorism for attempting to register. - Detail from source: "any Black citizen attempting to register risks immediate dismissal by their employer. Intimidation is omnipresent."
Question 2: Causal Explanation (12 Marks)
Target: AO1/AO2 (12 marks). Level 4 (10–12 marks): An analytical explanation showing a line of reasoning with clear links to the outcome, supported by accurate, detailed knowledge.
“One reason why Black Americans faced segregation and discrimination in the Southern states in the early 1950s was that federal judicial rulings provided constitutional backing for segregation.” In 1896, the landmark Supreme Court decision in Plessy v. Ferguson established the 'separate but equal' doctrine. This legal precedent allowed Southern states to pass sweeping Jim Crow laws enforcing segregation in schools, public transport, and restaurants. Therefore, federal judicial approval was a primary reason why Black Americans faced segregation and discrimination in the Southern states in the early 1950s because it legally shielded discriminatory state laws from constitutional challenges. “A second reason why Black Americans faced segregation and discrimination in the Southern states in the early 1950s was that Southern state authorities systematically disenfranchised Black voters.” States introduced impossible literacy tests, requiring Black applicants to explain complex constitutional clauses, alongside poll taxes and grandfather clauses. This meant that by 1956, only 20% of eligible Southern Black citizens were registered to vote. Therefore, political disenfranchisement was a crucial cause of why Black Americans faced segregation and discrimination in the Southern states in the early 1950s because it prevented Black citizens from obtaining the democratic representation needed to challenge racist local policies. “A third reason why Black Americans faced segregation and discrimination in the Southern states in the early 1950s was that segregation was ruthlessly enforced through organized white violence and social terror.” The Ku Klux Klan and White Citizens' Councils carried out lynchings, bombings, and economic intimidation, such as firing Black workers who tried to register. This campaign was reinforced by local police complicity and all-white juries who refused to convict white attackers. Therefore, grassroots violence and state-level complicity were decisive reasons why Black Americans faced segregation and discrimination in the Southern states in the early 1950s because they made active resistance too dangerous, ensuring daily compliance.

The interpretations differ because the historians have given weight to different sources. Interpretation 1 is supported by Source B, which states that they have "successfully outlawed the exclusionary white primary system in Texas", leading to the view that civil rights organisations achieved "critical legal milestones" in the courts. Conversely, Interpretation 2 is supported by Source C, which states that "federal policy adjustments do not automatically dissolve local hostility", leading to the view that "day-to-day conditions for Southern Black Americans remained virtually unaltered" on the ground.
Question 3(d): Evaluation of Interpretation (16 Marks + 4 SPaG)
Target: AO4 (16 marks) + SPaG (4 marks). Level 4 (13–16 marks): Balanced discussion analyzing both interpretations with sustained contextual knowledge, leading to a justified judgment.
I agree with Interpretation 2 that practical progress was extremely limited. My own knowledge confirms that despite NAACP legal efforts, only roughly 20% of Southern Black Americans could register to vote due to the KKK's terror tactics and the complicity of local law enforcement. Furthermore, Source C reveals activists endured insults, physical assault while local law enforcement officers watched with amusement, showing the extreme resistance. This confirms Interpretation 2's claim that day-to-day conditions "remained virtually unaltered" and that "power on the ground was maintained by hostile white groups". However, Interpretation 1 is also valid in stating that the legal foundations were successfully challenged. The NAACP's strategy of litigation culminated in the monumental 1954 Brown v. Topeka ruling, which officially overturned the 1896 Plessy doctrine of separate but equal. Furthermore, Source B highlights that litigation was successfully targeting segregation in higher education and the white primary system in Texas. This matches Interpretation 1's view that they achieved "critical legal milestones" that provided "a crucial legal precedent" that followed. Overall, while Interpretation 1 correctly identifies that the NAACP laid the crucial legal groundwork, Interpretation 2 is ultimately stronger for this early period. Until the mass grassroots movements of the 1960s forced the federal government to physically enforce these new laws, the daily reality of Black Americans "remained virtually unaltered".

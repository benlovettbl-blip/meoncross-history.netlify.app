Lesson 1: The East India Company & Mughal Bengal (Q1–Q8)
1. ▸
1600 — On 31 December 1600, Elizabeth I chartered 218 London merchants with an exclusive 15-year trade monopoly over the Indian Ocean.
2. ▸
Factories — Factories (in Surat, Madras, and Calcutta) were fortified commercial compounds where mercantile agents ("factors") stockpiled calicoes and spices.
3. ▸
Emperor Jahangir — Jahangir granted commercial privileges by imperial firman, treating the English as peaceful sea merchants paying valuable customs dues.
4. ▸▸
The Company built unauthorised fortifications and evaded provincial trade customs using illegal passes — Siraj ud-Daulah asserted sovereign Bengali authority against illicit British military expansion and commercial tax fraud.
5. ▸▸
By bribing the Nawab’s general, Mir Jafar, to betray his ruler, and keeping Company gunpowder dry during a monsoon downpour — Clive conspired with Indian bankers and Mir Jafar; when rain ruined Bengali powder, British tarpaulins kept artillery dry for a devastating barrage.
6. ▸▸
The Treaty of Allahabad (The Diwani) — The Diwani granted the Company civil tax rights over 20 million Bengalis, yielding £2 million annually while leaving figurehead Nawabs without revenue.
7. ▸▸▸
It turned a private joint-stock commercial firm into a sovereign military ruler collecting taxes from millions of Indian subjects — A private London corporate boardroom held sovereign executive power over an empire larger than Britain, answering to shareholders rather than Parliament.
8. ▸▸▸
The Company maintained full tax collection and hoarded grain for profit during a severe drought, leading to up to 10 million deaths — Prioritising corporate revenue over famine relief exposed the fatal ruthlessness of company rule, provoking parliamentary scrutiny in the 1773 Regulating Act.
Lesson 2: The Transatlantic Slave Trade & Middle Passage (Q9–Q16)
9. ▸
1619 — A privateer ship landed approximately twenty enslaved Africans at Point Comfort, Virginia, initiating hereditary bondage in British North America.
10. ▸
Chattel slavery — Under chattel slavery, enslaved people were legally defined as property, purchasable, taxable, and inherited across generations by maternal descent.
11. ▸
The Middle Passage — The Middle Passage formed the central leg of the Triangular Trade, forcing millions into horrific confinement aboard specialised slave ships.
12. ▸▸
The Royal African Company — Chartered by Charles II, the Royal African Company branded captive Africans "RAC" on their chests and shipped more people into slavery than any single entity.
13. ▸▸
They calculated that packing maximum captives yielded higher net auction profits even after 10–20% mortality from dysentery and smallpox — Captains treated human beings as depreciating maritime freight, calculating that surviving numbers would offset deaths caused by stifling, unventilated holds.
14. ▸▸
Captain Collingwood threw 133 living captives overboard to claim maritime insurance payouts for lost cargo rather than diseased casualties — British courts originally treated the deliberate drowning of 133 human beings as an ordinary cargo loss, shocking the public and galvanising Granville Sharp.
15. ▸▸▸
The Brookes slave ship plan ("Description of a Slave Ship") — The cross-section showing 454 men, women, and children packed tightly on wooden shelves provided undeniable visual proof of systemic cruelty.
16. ▸▸▸
They dismantled pro-slavery racist theories by providing articulate, firsthand moral witness to the horrors of kidnapping, trafficking, and bondage — Equiano’s 1789 narrative became a national bestseller, turning abstract parliamentary debate into an undeniable human and moral crisis.
Lesson 3: Plantation Reality & Maroon Resistance (Q17–Q20)
17. ▸
1655 — Cromwell’s "Western Design" seized Jamaica in 1655, laying the foundation for Britain’s immensely lucrative Caribbean sugar empire.
18. ▸
Sugar cane — Sugar transformed British diets and generated astronomical mercantile profits that financed Georgian architecture, merchant banks, and industry.
19. ▸
Maroons — From the Spanish "cimarrón" (wild/untamed), Maroons established fortified, independent societies in Jamaica’s rugged Cockpit Country and Blue Mountains.
20. ▸▸
It divided enslaved workers by physical strength into three gangs driven by armed overseers to labour up to 18 hours daily — The Great Gang handled brutal planting and cane-cutting, the Second Gang did weeding, and young children in the Small Gang performed light chores under the whip.

Lesson 3: Plantation Reality & Maroon Resistance (Q21–Q24)
21. ▸▸
The Abeng — The abeng produced sharp, resonant sonic tones that echoed across deep ravines, alerting Maroon fighters to approaching British redcoats miles away.
22. ▸▸
Guerrilla warfare using mountain camouflage, surprise ambushes on steep ridge trails, and booby-trapped defiles — Queen Nanny’s warriors disguised themselves in leafy branches and used mountain terrain to outmanoeuvre and ambush British forces laden with heavy baggage.
23. ▸▸▸
It forced the British imperial Crown to formally recognise Maroon sovereignty, granting 1,500 acres of independent land and self-government — After spending £250,000 failing to conquer the Maroons, Britain signed a formal treaty conceding military defeat to free African leaders.
24. ▸▸▸
Maroons were legally bound to capture and return new runaway slaves to British authorities in exchange for their own recognized freedom — British colonial authorities used divide-and-rule diplomacy, turning sovereign Maroons into an external frontier police against future island-wide rebellions.
Lesson 4: The Thirteen Colonies & Boston Tea Party (Q25–Q32)
25. ▸
The Royal Proclamation of 1763 — Issued after Pontiac’s War to prevent costly frontier clashes with Native American confederacies, it infuriated land-hungry colonial speculators.
26. ▸
The Stamp Act — The Stamp Act was Parliament’s first direct domestic levy on the colonies, provoking fierce constitutional protests against unrepresented taxation.
27. ▸
"No Taxation Without Representation" — Colonists argued that under the 1689 Bill of Rights, English subjects could only be taxed through assemblies where they had direct elected representation.
28. ▸▸
The Sons of Liberty — The Sons of Liberty organised street protests, intimidated royal tax collectors, hanged officials in effigy, and coordinated colonial non-importation pacts.
29. ▸▸
It depicted British soldiers firing an orderly volley into a peaceful, helpless crowd of citizens on the orders of Captain Preston — Revere’s sensationalised print transformed a chaotic street brawl involving snowball-throwing youths into an image of cold-blooded military tyranny.
30. ▸▸
Because purchasing the taxed tea would accept Parliament’s legal right to tax the colonies and accepted an East India Company trade monopoly — Patriots recognized a constitutional trap: buying cheaper corporate tea meant surrendering the fundamental principle of self-taxation.
31. ▸▸▸
Disguised as Mohawk warriors, they boarded three merchant ships and dumped 342 chests of East India Company tea into Boston Harbour — Over three hours, patriots smashed open wooden crates containing £1.5 million worth of tea, destroying 46 tons without harming any other cargo.
32. ▸▸▸
Parliament passed the punitive Coercive Acts (Intolerable Acts), closing Boston Harbour and suspending Massachusetts self-government, uniting all thirteen colonies — Instead of isolating rebellious Massachusetts, the authoritarian military crackdown prompted the colonies to convene the First Continental Congress and form armed militias.
Lesson 5: The American Revolution & Black Loyalists (Q33–Q40)
33. ▸
1775 (Lexington and Concord) — On 19 April 1775, colonial minutemen clashed with British regulars attempting to seize militia ammunition stores at Concord, starting the war.
34. ▸
Thomas Jefferson — Jefferson drafted the historic parchment in Philadelphia, drawing upon Enlightenment philosophy to proclaim universal unalienable rights.
35. ▸
Lord Dunmore’s Proclamation — Dunmore’s proclamation panicked Virginia slaveholders, prompting thousands of enslaved men to join the British Ethiopian Regiment wearing "Liberty to Slaves" sashes.
36. ▸▸
American slaveholders loudly demanding liberty from King George III while holding hundreds of thousands of African people in chattel bondage — Johnson famously quipped: "How is it that we hear the loudest yelps for liberty among the drivers of negroes?", exposing the contradiction of slaveholder freedom.
37. ▸▸
It convinced King Louis XVI of France to enter the conflict as an open military and naval ally against Great Britain — French battleships, troops, and loans turned a colonial counter-insurgency into a multi-theatre global war that overstretched the Royal Navy across the Atlantic and Indian Oceans.
38. ▸▸
The French fleet blockaded Chesapeake Bay, cutting off British naval supply and trapping General Cornwallis’s army on the peninsula — Cornwallis was trapped between Washington’s siege artillery on land and French warships at sea, forcing the surrender of 8,000 British soldiers.
39. ▸▸▸
The Book of Negroes — British General Guy Carleton refused Washington’s demands to surrender formerly enslaved people, compiling the Book of Negroes to evacuate free Black Loyalists to Nova Scotia and London.
40. ▸▸▸
The Treaty of Paris (1783) — Britain formally recognized the United States from the Atlantic to the Mississippi, ending the First Empire and shifting British colonial ambition toward India and Australasia.

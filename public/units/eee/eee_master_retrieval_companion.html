Enquiries 1–4: Key Topic 1 Queen, Government & Religion • Qs 1–32 Official 2-Mark Standard: 1m Fact + 1m Explanation
Q1 £300,000 (inherited from Mary I).
The Crown owed £300,000 against ordinary annual revenues of barely £286,000, creating immediate financial vulnerability.
Q2 14 per cent.
Over £100,000 was owed to Antwerp financiers, leaving England vulnerable to economic blackmail from the Habsburg Netherlands.
Q3 19 members.
Led by Principal Secretary William Cecil, this compact executive body managed national defense, finance, and foreign diplomacy daily.
Q4 Royal patronage.
By balancing rival court factions and distributing lucrative privileges, Elizabeth maintained sovereign control without a standing army.
Q5 The Pope had refused to annul Henry’s first marriage to Catherine of Aragon.
Catholics viewed Catherine as Henry’s only lawful wife, branding Elizabeth an illegitimate child with zero divine right to rule.
Q6 The Second Succession Act.
Passed after Anne Boleyn was executed for treason, the act created enduring statutory doubt despite Henry’s 1544 will.
Q7 Justices of the Peace (JPs).
Around 20–40 JPs per county administered poor relief, fixed wage rates, and maintained public order without a salaried police force.
Q8 Sole authority to approve extraordinary taxation (subsidies).
Because ordinary feudal revenues were insufficient in wartime, Elizabeth was forced to summon Parliament whenever she needed tax revenue.
Q9 Catholic North and West vs Protestant South and East.
Landed northern aristocrats remained Catholic, whereas London, Kent, and East Anglia embraced Protestant reformation ideas.
Q10 Nearly 300 Protestants.
These Marian burnings created intense anti-Catholic trauma and generated deep public hatred of Spanish and papal influence.
Q11 Supreme Governor of the Church of England.
This compromise pacified Catholics who believed Christ or the Pope was Head, and Puritans who believed no woman could be head of church.
Q12 The Oath of Supremacy.
Refusing the oath meant immediate loss of office, property confiscation, and charges of treason upon repeated refusal.
Q13 One shilling (12 pence) per missed service.
While affordable for wealthy gentry, 12d represented a full week’s wages for ordinary agricultural labourers.
Q14 The 1559 Book of Common Prayer.
It combined Edward VI’s 1549 and 1552 prayer books, using deliberate ambiguity over the Eucharist to allow diverse interpretations.
Q15 The Ornaments Rubric.
Elizabeth insisted that churches retain traditional candles and clerical robes, provoking furious objections from returning Puritans.
Q16 57 Royal Injunctions.
Drafted by William Cecil, they detailed everything from preaching quarterly sermons to vetting prospective clergy wives.
Q17 Marian Exiles (or Puritans).
They had witnessed Calvinist reform abroad and demanded the complete eradication of all remaining Catholic ceremonies in England.
Q18 Presbyterianism.
Presbyterians challenged the monarch’s power to appoint bishops, which Elizabeth viewed as a direct threat to royal authority.
Q19 A silver crucifix (cross).
Puritan bishops threatened resignation over what they saw as Catholic idol worship; Elizabeth backed down and removed the cross.
Q20 The surplice.
Puritans argued that plain black scholar gowns should be worn, claiming ornate vestments had no authority in the Bible.
Q21 The Book of Advertisements.
Parker mandated that all clergy wear the surplice during communion and outdoor apparel, triggering the Vestments Controversy.
Q22 37 ministers.
Despite their dismissal, the ministers formed underground Puritan printing presses and separatist congregations in London.
Q23 Church Papists.
Most English Catholics conformed outwardly during the 1560s because early recusancy enforcement was deliberately lenient.
Q24 The Duke of Alba.
Alba established the brutal Council of Troubles ("Council of Blood"), positioning a massive Spanish army directly across the Channel.
Q25 Margaret Tudor (Henry VIII’s elder sister).
As Henry VIII’s great-niece, Mary was viewed by strict Catholics as the legitimate, Catholic, God-ordained heir to Elizabeth.
Q26 Henry Stuart, Lord Darnley.
Darnley was also a Tudor descendant; their marriage caused intense panic in Whitehall over a unified Catholic succession.
Q27 Kirk o' Field.
Darnley was found strangled in an adjoining garden, triggering a massive political scandal that destroyed Mary’s authority in Scotland.
Q28 James Hepburn, 4th Earl of Bothwell.
Marrying Bothwell under Protestant rites alienated Scottish nobles and Catholic Europe, triggering an immediate armed uprising.
Q29 Carberry Hill.
Mary’s army melted away without fighting; Bothwell fled into Scandinavian exile while Mary was taken captive to Edinburgh.
Q30 Lochleven Castle.
Under threat of death, Mary signed deeds of abdication on 24 July 1567 in favour of her one-year-old infant son, James VI.
Q31 James VI (with the Earl of Moray as regent).
Protestant regent Moray established firm control, ensuring Scotland remained allied to Elizabethan England.
Q32 Workington in Cumberland.
Following her defeat at the Battle of Langside, Mary fled across the Solway Firth seeking Elizabeth’s military protection.

Enquiries 5–8: Key Topic 2 Plots, Foreign Relations & The Armada • Qs 33–64 Official 2-Mark Standard: 1m Fact + 1m Explanation
Q33 The Earl of Northumberland and the Earl of Westmorland.
They resented their loss of court power to Protestant newcomers like Cecil and sought to restore traditional Catholic worship.
Q34 Durham Cathedral.
Celebrating Mass at Durham symbolized the restoration of Roman Catholicism across the north of England.
Q35 Approximately 450 rebels.
Elizabeth ordered public hangings in every rebellious northern village to terrify the population and permanently crush feudal rebellion.
Q36 Regnans in Excelsis.
The bull declared Elizabeth a heretic and released English Catholics from their allegiance, transforming religious belief into treason.
Q37 Roberto Ridolfi.
Ridolfi travelled between Rome, Madrid, and Brussels planning a Spanish invasion under Alba to marry Mary to the Duke of Norfolk.
Q38 Thomas Howard, 4th Duke of Norfolk.
Norfolk was the premier peer of England; his execution showed that royal justice applied to the highest nobility in the realm.
Q39 The Duke of Guise (funded by Philip II).
Guise planned to land in Sussex, liberate Mary, and murder Elizabeth; Walsingham intercepted Throckmorton’s coded documents.
Q40 The Bond of Association.
Drafted by Cecil and Walsingham, signers swore to execute anyone attempting or benefiting from the assassination of the Queen.
Q41 King Philip II.
Ruling Spain, the Spanish Netherlands, and the American empire, Philip saw himself as the supreme defender of the Catholic faith.
Q42 William of Orange (William the Silent).
Assassinated in July 1584, William’s death left Dutch rebels leaderless and forced Elizabeth to consider direct intervention.
Q43 The Sea Beggars.
They disrupted Spanish supply fleets in the Channel until Elizabeth expelled them in 1572, prompting them to seize the Dutch port of Brill.
Q44 Sir John Hawkins.
The Spanish ambush destroyed several English ships, convincing Hawkins and Drake that Spanish treaties could never be trusted.
Q45 1577 (returning in September 1580).
Backed secretly by Elizabeth and Privy Councillors, Drake’s expedition raided undefended Spanish treasure ports across the Pacific.
Q46 Nuestra Señora de la Concepción (the 'Cacafuego').
Drake seized eighty pounds of gold, thirteen chests of coins, and 26 tons of silver, making the voyage the richest plunder in history.
Q47 Aboard the Golden Hind at Deptford in April 1581.
Elizabeth’s public knighthood demonstrated open royal defiance against Philip’s demands for Drake to be hanged as a pirate.
Q48 Portugal.
Annexing Portugal gave Spain control of Lisbon harbour, the Portuguese oceanic battle fleet, and extensive Atlantic trade routes.
Q49 Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester.
Leicester led 7,000 soldiers to support the Dutch, but suffered from inadequate funding and severe tactical disagreements.
Q50 Governor-General of the Low Countries.
Elizabeth was outraged because accepting sovereign executive title implied England sought to depose Philip and annex the Netherlands.
Q51 Sir Philip Sidney.
Sidney’s death made him a Protestant martyr, but the battle failed to capture the Spanish-held town of Zutphen.
Q52 Deventer (alongside the Zutphen sconces).
Stanley and Rowland York defected to Spain, destroying Dutch confidence in English military leadership and alliances.
Q53 19 April 1587.
Drake sailed twenty-four warships directly into Cadiz harbour in a bold pre-emptive strike against assembling Armada transports.
Q54 30 vessels.
Drake burned and captured warships and supply transports over 36 hours, exposing the vulnerability of Spanish home naval ports.
Q55 That he had "singed the King of Spain's beard".
Drake’s phrase mocked Philip II and demonstrated English naval mastery over Spanish defensive coastal positions.
Q56 Seasoned oak barrel staves.
Destroying seasoned wood forced Spain to use green timber barrels, causing food to rot and fresh drinking water to leak during 1588.
Q57 130 ships.
The fleet carried 8,000 sailors, 19,000 soldiers, and 2,400 cannons under the grand Enterprise of England.
Q58 The Duke of Parma and his 27,000 veteran soldiers.
Parma’s Army of Flanders was to be ferried across the English Channel in flat-bottomed barges under Armada naval escort.
Q59 The crescent formation.
Heavily armed galleons guarded the curved wings, preventing English captains from isolating and boarding individual transports.
Q60 Culverins.
Culverins fired 17-pound balls at long range; English captains stayed upwind to avoid close-quarters Spanish infantry boarding.
Q61 Calais Roads.
Medina Sidonia anchored in open water, but Parma was blockaded by Dutch flyboats in Dunkirk and unable to embark.
Q62 Sent eight pitch-soaked fireships into the packed anchorage.
Fearing explosive "hellburners", Spanish captains cut their anchor cables in panic and scattered into the dark North Sea.
Q63 The Battle of Gravelines.
English ships closed to point-blank range, firing rapid broadsides that sank three galleons and killed over 1,000 Spanish soldiers.
Q64 The "Protestant Wind".
Southwesterly gales prevented Medina Sidonia from rejoining Parma, forcing the battered fleet around Scotland and Ireland.

Enquiries 9–12: Key Topic 3 Society, Exploration & Colonisation • Qs 65–96 Official 2-Mark Standard: 1m Fact + 1m Explanation
Q65 Humanism.
Humanists argued that education should develop rational, moral citizens capable of serving in royal government and local courts.
Q66 Approximately 30% of men compared to under 10% of women.
Literacy rose among merchants, yeomen, and gentry, while poor labourers and working women remained largely unschooled.
Q67 Petty Schools (or Dame Schools for girls).
Pupils learned basic English reading using hornbooks before boys of the middling sort progressed to grammar school.
Q68 Over 70 grammar schools.
Independent of the Catholic Church, grammar schools allowed sons of yeomen and merchants to achieve modest social mobility.
Q69 Latin (grammar, rhetoric, and classical literature).
Pupils memorized Latin texts by Cicero, Virgil, and Seneca, speaking exclusively in Latin to build fluency for university.
Q70 Oxford and Cambridge.
Both universities founded new colleges and reformed curricula to produce educated Anglican clergy and royal statesmen.
Q71 The Inns of Court.
Studying common law at the Inns provided essential legal knowledge for managing country estates and serving as Justices of the Peace.
Q72 Hunting deer, hawking, real tennis, and fencing.
These expensive pastimes reinforced aristocratic chivalry, requiring vast private parks, specialized gear, and leisure time.
Q73 From 3 million to over 4.2 million.
A 40% population surge caused consumer demand for bread grain to drastically outstrip agricultural production.
Q74 Real wages collapsed by roughly 50%.
Food prices rose while wages remained fixed, leaving ordinary rural labourers unable to afford basic bread subsistence.
Q75 Enclosure.
Landlords converted strip-farming crop fields into private sheep runs, needing only a few shepherds and evicting tenant families.
Q76 80 per cent of all exports.
Wool profitability drove landowners to maximize grazing acreage at the direct expense of labour-intensive arable farming.
Q77 The Impotent Poor vs the Idle/Sturdy Poor.
The Impotent Poor were genuinely unable to work (elderly, sick, orphans); the Sturdy Poor were able-bodied but accused of laziness.
Q78 Thomas Harman.
Harman sensationalized vagrant categories like "counterfeit cranks" and "priggers", stoking upper-class terror of rebellion.
Q79 Severe whipping and boring a hole through the right ear with a hot iron.
Repeat offenders faced hanging as felons, reflecting Tudor determination to terrorize vagrants into staying in their home parishes.
Q80 The Poor Rate.
Every parish was legally mandated to assess local property owners and appoint Overseers of the Poor to distribute relief.
Q81 Astrolabes and quadrants.
Measuring the angular height of the Pole Star or midday sun allowed captains to determine latitude on uncharted oceanic voyages.
Q82 The Mercator Projection (Gerardus Mercator).
Mercator’s conformal cylindrical chart represented lines of constant compass bearing as straight lines, revolutionizing navigation.
Q83 The race-built galleon.
Longer, lower, and narrower than bulky Spanish ships, race-built galleons sailed faster and tacked closer into prevailing winds.
Q84 Culverins.
Compact four-wheeled carriages allowed English gun crews to rapidly haul back, reload, and run out guns through low gunports.
Q85 Joint-stock companies.
Investors bought shares and split profits or losses, enabling expensive, hazardous expeditions without risking individual ruin.
Q86 The Muscovy Company.
Founded after Richard Chancellor reached Archangel, it bypassed Baltic Hanseatic merchants to trade directly with Tsar Ivan IV.
Q87 Martin Frobisher (or John Davis).
Though he failed to find the passage and brought back worthless iron pyrite (fool’s gold), he mapped Arctic waters around Baffin Island.
Q88 The Strait of Magellan.
Surviving violent gales that destroyed four accompanying ships, Drake entered the Pacific alone in his renamed Golden Hind.
Q89 Sir Walter Raleigh.
Raleigh funded expeditions to claim North American territory, though Elizabeth forbade him from leaving court to lead them in person.
Q90 Virginia.
The name honored Elizabeth’s virgin status and established England’s first formal territorial claim on the North American continent.
Q91 Richard Hakluyt.
Hakluyt argued a colony would provide naval bases to raid Spanish treasure fleets, supply exotic timber, and reduce English poverty.
Q92 Sir Richard Grenville (naval fleet) and Ralph Lane (colony governor).
Grenville departed with the ships after landing, leaving Lane to govern 107 settlers with strict, rigid army discipline.
Q93 Lacked farmers and was dominated by gentlemen and soldiers who refused manual work.
Gentlemen expected servants to feed them, while soldiers were prone to violence rather than tilling soil or building shelters.
Q94 Ran aground on a coastal sandbar, flooding the hold with seawater.
Seawater ruined planting seeds and grain stores, forcing immediate, humiliating dependency upon the local Indigenous population.
Q95 Chief Wingina.
Wingina initially provided fish and corn from weirs, but English demands and European disease epidemics caused relations to collapse.
Q96 Sir Francis Drake.
Drake arrived after raiding Spanish Florida; facing starvation and Indigenous attacks after Lane killed Wingina, the settlers fled.

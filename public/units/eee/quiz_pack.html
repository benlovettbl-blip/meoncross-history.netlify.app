KEY TOPIC 1.1 The Situation on Elizabeth’s Accession, 1558
Q1. £300,000 (inherited from Mary I). The Crown owed £300,000 against ordinary annual revenues of barely £286,000, creating immediate financial vulnerability.
Q2. 14 per cent. Over £100,000 was owed to Antwerp financiers, leaving England vulnerable to economic blackmail from the Habsburg Netherlands.
Q3. 19 members. Led by Principal Secretary William Cecil, this compact executive body managed national defense, finance, and foreign diplomacy daily.
Q4. Royal patronage. By balancing rival court factions and distributing lucrative privileges, Elizabeth maintained sovereign control without a standing army.
Q5. The Pope had refused to annul Henry’s first marriage to Catherine of Aragon. Catholics viewed Catherine as Henry’s only lawful wife, branding Elizabeth an illegitimate child with zero divine right to rule.
Q6. The Second Succession Act. Passed after Anne Boleyn was executed for treason, the act created enduring statutory doubt despite Henry’s 1544 will.
Q7. Justices of the Peace (JPs). Around 20–40 JPs per county administered poor relief, fixed wage rates, and maintained public order without a salaried police force.
Q8. Sole authority to approve extraordinary taxation (subsidies). Because ordinary feudal revenues were insufficient in wartime, Elizabeth was forced to summon Parliament whenever she needed tax revenue.
Q9. Calais. Its capture by the Duke of Guise shattered English national prestige and stripped the realm of its last military foothold on mainland Europe.
Q10. The Auld Alliance. French garrisons stationed in Scotland under regent Mary of Guise left England vulnerable to a coordinated two-front invasion.
KEY TOPIC 1.2 The 'Settlement' of Religion, 1559
Q1. Catholic North and West vs Protestant South and East. Landed northern aristocrats remained Catholic, whereas London, Kent, and East Anglia embraced Protestant reformation ideas.
Q2. Nearly 300 Protestants. These Marian burnings created intense anti-Catholic trauma and generated deep public hatred of Spanish and papal influence.
Q3. Supreme Governor of the Church of England. This compromise pacified Catholics who believed Christ or the Pope was Head, and Puritans who believed no woman could be head of church.
Q4. The Oath of Supremacy. Refusing the oath meant immediate loss of office, property confiscation, and charges of treason upon repeated refusal.
Q5. One shilling (12 pence) per missed service. While affordable for wealthy gentry, 12d represented a full week’s wages for ordinary agricultural labourers.
Q6. The 1559 Book of Common Prayer. It combined Edward VI’s 1549 and 1552 prayer books, using deliberate ambiguity over the Eucharist to allow diverse interpretations.
Q7. The Ornaments Rubric. Elizabeth insisted that churches retain traditional candles and clerical robes, provoking furious objections from returning Puritans.
Q8. 57 Royal Injunctions. Drafted by William Cecil, they detailed everything from preaching quarterly sermons to vetting prospective clergy wives.
Q9. An English Bible. This reinforced the central Protestant doctrine that scripture must be accessible to all believers in their native tongue.
Q10. All Marian bishops except one refused the Oath and were dismissed. Only the Bishop of Llandaff conformed; Elizabeth replaced all dismissed Catholic bishops with loyal Protestant reformers.
KEY TOPIC 1.3 Challenge to the Religious Settlement
Q1. Marian Exiles (or Puritans). They had witnessed Calvinist reform abroad and demanded the complete eradication of all remaining Catholic ceremonies in England.
Q2. Presbyterianism. Presbyterians challenged the monarch’s power to appoint bishops, which Elizabeth viewed as a direct threat to royal authority.
Q3. A silver crucifix (cross). Puritan bishops threatened resignation over what they saw as Catholic idol worship; Elizabeth backed down and removed the cross.
Q4. The surplice. Puritans argued that plain black scholar gowns should be worn, claiming ornate vestments had no authority in the Bible.
Q5. The Book of Advertisements. Parker mandated that all clergy wear the surplice during communion and outdoor apparel, triggering the Vestments Controversy.
Q6. 37 ministers. Despite their dismissal, the ministers formed underground Puritan printing presses and separatist congregations in London.
Q7. Church Papists. Most English Catholics conformed outwardly during the 1560s because early recusancy enforcement was deliberately lenient.
Q8. The Duke of Alba. Alba established the brutal Council of Troubles ("Council of Blood"), positioning a massive Spanish army directly across the Channel.
Q9. 400,000 florins (the Genoese Loan). Elizabeth seized money intended to pay Alba’s troops, claiming the silver still legally belonged to Genoese bankers.
Q10. Imposed a total trade embargo on English cloth in Antwerp. The embargo disrupted English wool exports, pushing London merchants into economic recession and escalating Anglo-Spanish hostility.
KEY TOPIC 1.4 The Problem of Mary, Queen of Scots, 1568–69
Q1. Margaret Tudor (Henry VIII’s elder sister). As Henry VIII’s great-niece, Mary was viewed by strict Catholics as the legitimate, Catholic, God-ordained heir to Elizabeth.
Q2. Henry Stuart, Lord Darnley. Darnley was also a Tudor descendant; their marriage caused intense panic in Whitehall over a unified Catholic succession.
Q3. Kirk o' Field. Darnley was found strangled in an adjoining garden, triggering a massive political scandal that destroyed Mary’s authority in Scotland.
Q4. James Hepburn, 4th Earl of Bothwell. Marrying Bothwell under Protestant rites alienated Scottish nobles and Catholic Europe, triggering an immediate armed uprising.
Q5. Carberry Hill. Mary’s army melted away without fighting; Bothwell fled into Scandinavian exile while Mary was taken captive to Edinburgh.
Q6. Lochleven Castle. Under threat of death, Mary signed deeds of abdication on 24 July 1567 in favour of her one-year-old infant son, James VI.
Q7. James VI (with the Earl of Moray as regent). Protestant regent Moray established firm control, ensuring Scotland remained allied to Elizabethan England.
Q8. Workington in Cumberland. Following her defeat at the Battle of Langside, Mary fled across the Solway Firth seeking Elizabeth’s military protection.
Q9. The Casket Letters. Eight French love letters allegedly written by Mary to Bothwell were produced by Moray to prove her complicity in Darnley’s murder.
Q10. George Talbot, 6th Earl of Shrewsbury. Mary remained under Shrewsbury’s surveillance for fifteen years, acting as a permanent magnetic pole for Catholic conspiracies.

KEY TOPIC 2.1 Plots and Revolts at Home, 1569–1587
Q1. The Earl of Northumberland and the Earl of Westmorland. They resented their loss of court power to Protestant newcomers like Cecil and sought to restore traditional Catholic worship.
Q2. Durham Cathedral. Celebrating Mass at Durham symbolized the restoration of Roman Catholicism across the north of England.
Q3. Approximately 450 rebels. Elizabeth ordered public hangings in every rebellious northern village to terrify the population and permanently crush feudal rebellion.
Q4. Regnans in Excelsis. The bull declared Elizabeth a heretic and released English Catholics from their allegiance, transforming religious belief into treason.
Q5. Roberto Ridolfi. Ridolfi travelled between Rome, Madrid, and Brussels planning a Spanish invasion under Alba to marry Mary to the Duke of Norfolk.
Q6. Thomas Howard, 4th Duke of Norfolk. Norfolk was the premier peer of England; his execution showed that royal justice applied to the highest nobility in the realm.
Q7. The Duke of Guise (funded by Philip II). Guise planned to land in Sussex, liberate Mary, and murder Elizabeth; Walsingham intercepted Throckmorton’s coded documents.
Q8. The Bond of Association. Drafted by Cecil and Walsingham, signers swore to execute anyone attempting or benefiting from the assassination of the Queen.
Q9. Waterproof cartridges inside beer barrels. Double agent Gilbert Gifford intercepted every letter, allowing Walsingham’s cryptanalyst Thomas Phelippes to decode them.
Q10. 8 February 1587. Mary’s execution removed the domestic figurehead for Catholic plots, but provoked Philip II into launching the Spanish Armada.
KEY TOPIC 2.2 Relations with Spain, 1569–1585
Q1. King Philip II. Ruling Spain, the Spanish Netherlands, and the American empire, Philip saw himself as the supreme defender of the Catholic faith.
Q2. William of Orange (William the Silent). Assassinated in July 1584, William’s death left Dutch rebels leaderless and forced Elizabeth to consider direct intervention.
Q3. The Sea Beggars. They disrupted Spanish supply fleets in the Channel until Elizabeth expelled them in 1572, prompting them to seize the Dutch port of Brill.
Q4. Sir John Hawkins. The Spanish ambush destroyed several English ships, convincing Hawkins and Drake that Spanish treaties could never be trusted.
Q5. 1577 (returning in September 1580). Backed secretly by Elizabeth and Privy Councillors, Drake’s expedition raided undefended Spanish treasure ports across the Pacific.
Q6. Nuestra Señora de la Concepción (the 'Cacafuego'). Drake seized eighty pounds of gold, thirteen chests of coins, and 26 tons of silver, making the voyage the richest plunder in history.
Q7. Aboard the Golden Hind at Deptford in April 1581. Elizabeth’s public knighthood demonstrated open royal defiance against Philip’s demands for Drake to be hanged as a pirate.
Q8. Portugal. Annexing Portugal gave Spain control of Lisbon harbour, the Portuguese oceanic battle fleet, and extensive Atlantic trade routes.
Q9. The Treaty of Joinville. The treaty united France and Spain against European Protestantism, eliminating the threat of France attacking Spain during an invasion.
Q10. The Treaty of Nonsuch. Elizabeth placed the Netherlands under English military protection, crossing the Rubicon into direct armed conflict with Spain.
KEY TOPIC 2.3 The Outbreak of War with Spain, 1585–1588
Q1. Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester. Leicester led 7,000 soldiers to support the Dutch, but suffered from inadequate funding and severe tactical disagreements.
Q2. Governor-General of the Low Countries. Elizabeth was outraged because accepting sovereign executive title implied England sought to depose Philip and annex the Netherlands.
Q3. Sir Philip Sidney. Sidney’s death made him a Protestant martyr, but the battle failed to capture the Spanish-held town of Zutphen.
Q4. Deventer (alongside the Zutphen sconces). Stanley and Rowland York defected to Spain, destroying Dutch confidence in English military leadership and alliances.
Q5. 19 April 1587. Drake sailed twenty-four warships directly into Cadiz harbour in a bold pre-emptive strike against assembling Armada transports.
Q6. 30 vessels. Drake burned and captured warships and supply transports over 36 hours, exposing the vulnerability of Spanish home naval ports.
Q7. That he had "singed the King of Spain's beard". Drake’s phrase mocked Philip II and demonstrated English naval mastery over Spanish defensive coastal positions.
Q8. Seasoned oak barrel staves. Destroying seasoned wood forced Spain to use green timber barrels, causing food to rot and fresh drinking water to leak during 1588.
Q9. A full twelve months (one year). The delay gave England critical time to mobilize county militias, fortify southern coastal beacons, and complete race-built galleons.
Q10. The Duke of Medina Sidonia. Medina Sidonia was an experienced land administrator but possessed no naval combat experience and suffered from chronic seasickness.
KEY TOPIC 2.4 The Spanish Armada: Strategy, Conflict & Defeat, 1588
Q1. 130 ships. The fleet carried 8,000 sailors, 19,000 soldiers, and 2,400 cannons under the grand Enterprise of England.
Q2. The Duke of Parma and his 27,000 veteran soldiers. Parma’s Army of Flanders was to be ferried across the English Channel in flat-bottomed barges under Armada naval escort.
Q3. The crescent formation. Heavily armed galleons guarded the curved wings, preventing English captains from isolating and boarding individual transports.
Q4. Culverins. Culverins fired 17-pound balls at long range; English captains stayed upwind to avoid close-quarters Spanish infantry boarding.
Q5. Calais Roads. Medina Sidonia anchored in open water, but Parma was blockaded by Dutch flyboats in Dunkirk and unable to embark.
Q6. Sent eight pitch-soaked fireships into the packed anchorage. Fearing explosive "hellburners", Spanish captains cut their anchor cables in panic and scattered into the dark North Sea.
Q7. The Battle of Gravelines. English ships closed to point-blank range, firing rapid broadsides that sank three galleons and killed over 1,000 Spanish soldiers.
Q8. The "Protestant Wind". Southwesterly gales prevented Medina Sidonia from rejoining Parma, forcing the battered fleet around Scotland and Ireland.
Q9. At the military troop camp at Tilbury. Elizabeth rallied county militia troops preparing for invasion, projecting heroic defiance and sovereign leadership.
Q10. Barely 67 ships. Over forty vessels were wrecked on the rocky coasts of Ireland and Scotland, shattering the myth of Spanish naval invincibility.

KEY TOPIC 3.1 Education and Leisure in Elizabethan England, 1558–1588
Q1. Humanism. Humanists argued that education should develop rational, moral citizens capable of serving in royal government and local courts.
Q2. Approximately 30% of men compared to under 10% of women. Literacy rose among merchants, yeomen, and gentry, while poor labourers and working women remained largely unschooled.
Q3. Petty Schools (or Dame Schools for girls). Pupils learned basic English reading using hornbooks before boys of the middling sort progressed to grammar school.
Q4. Over 70 grammar schools. Independent of the Catholic Church, grammar schools allowed sons of yeomen and merchants to achieve modest social mobility.
Q5. Latin (grammar, rhetoric, and classical literature). Pupils memorized Latin texts by Cicero, Virgil, and Seneca, speaking exclusively in Latin to build fluency for university.
Q6. Oxford and Cambridge. Both universities founded new colleges and reformed curricula to produce educated Anglican clergy and royal statesmen.
Q7. The Inns of Court. Studying common law at the Inns provided essential legal knowledge for managing country estates and serving as Justices of the Peace.
Q8. Hunting deer, hawking, real tennis, and fencing. These expensive pastimes reinforced aristocratic chivalry, requiring vast private parks, specialized gear, and leisure time.
Q9. Bear-baiting (alongside bull-baiting and cockfighting). Huge crowds from vagrants to Queen Elizabeth enjoyed watching mastiffs attack chained bears, wagering heavily on the fights.
Q10. The Theatre (built by James Burbage). Built outside the City of London’s jurisdiction, it welcomed up to 3,000 groundlings paying 1d and wealthy patrons in galleries.
KEY TOPIC 3.2 The Problem of the Poor and Vagrancy, 1558–1588
Q1. From 3 million to over 4.2 million. A 40% population surge caused consumer demand for bread grain to drastically outstrip agricultural production.
Q2. Real wages collapsed by roughly 50%. Food prices rose while wages remained fixed, leaving ordinary rural labourers unable to afford basic bread subsistence.
Q3. Enclosure. Landlords converted strip-farming crop fields into private sheep runs, needing only a few shepherds and evicting tenant families.
Q4. 80 per cent of all exports. Wool profitability drove landowners to maximize grazing acreage at the direct expense of labour-intensive arable farming.
Q5. The Impotent Poor vs the Idle/Sturdy Poor. The Impotent Poor were genuinely unable to work (elderly, sick, orphans); the Sturdy Poor were able-bodied but accused of laziness.
Q6. Thomas Harman. Harman sensationalized vagrant categories like "counterfeit cranks" and "priggers", stoking upper-class terror of rebellion.
Q7. Severe whipping and boring a hole through the right ear with a hot iron. Repeat offenders faced hanging as felons, reflecting Tudor determination to terrorize vagrants into staying in their home parishes.
Q8. The Poor Rate. Every parish was legally mandated to assess local property owners and appoint Overseers of the Poor to distribute relief.
Q9. Wool, hemp, flax, and iron. For the first time, statute law accepted state responsibility to provide work for the able-bodied rather than merely punishing them.
Q10. Houses of Correction. Vagrants who refused available work were locked in Bridewells and subjected to hard manual labour under corporal discipline.
KEY TOPIC 3.3 Exploration and Voyages of Discovery, 1558–1588
Q1. Astrolabes and quadrants. Measuring the angular height of the Pole Star or midday sun allowed captains to determine latitude on uncharted oceanic voyages.
Q2. The Mercator Projection (Gerardus Mercator). Mercator’s conformal cylindrical chart represented lines of constant compass bearing as straight lines, revolutionizing navigation.
Q3. The race-built galleon. Longer, lower, and narrower than bulky Spanish ships, race-built galleons sailed faster and tacked closer into prevailing winds.
Q4. Culverins. Compact four-wheeled carriages allowed English gun crews to rapidly haul back, reload, and run out guns through low gunports.
Q5. Joint-stock companies. Investors bought shares and split profits or losses, enabling expensive, hazardous expeditions without risking individual ruin.
Q6. The Muscovy Company. Founded after Richard Chancellor reached Archangel, it bypassed Baltic Hanseatic merchants to trade directly with Tsar Ivan IV.
Q7. Martin Frobisher (or John Davis). Though he failed to find the passage and brought back worthless iron pyrite (fool’s gold), he mapped Arctic waters around Baffin Island.
Q8. The Strait of Magellan. Surviving violent gales that destroyed four accompanying ships, Drake entered the Pacific alone in his renamed Golden Hind.
Q9. Nova Albion (New Britain). Drake careened his ship, made peaceful contact with local Coast Miwok people, and erected a brass plate claiming sovereign English title.
Q10. A staggering 4,700% return. Bringing home £140,000 in official treasure, the loot wiped out Elizabeth’s foreign debt and laid the seeds of global commercial empire.
KEY TOPIC 3.4 Raleigh and the Colonisation of Virginia, 1584–1590
Q1. Sir Walter Raleigh. Raleigh funded expeditions to claim North American territory, though Elizabeth forbade him from leaving court to lead them in person.
Q2. Virginia. The name honored Elizabeth’s virgin status and established England’s first formal territorial claim on the North American continent.
Q3. Richard Hakluyt. Hakluyt argued a colony would provide naval bases to raid Spanish treasure fleets, supply exotic timber, and reduce English poverty.
Q4. Sir Richard Grenville (naval fleet) and Ralph Lane (colony governor). Grenville departed with the ships after landing, leaving Lane to govern 107 settlers with strict, rigid army discipline.
Q5. Lacked farmers and was dominated by gentlemen and soldiers who refused manual work. Gentlemen expected servants to feed them, while soldiers were prone to violence rather than tilling soil or building shelters.
Q6. Ran aground on a coastal sandbar, flooding the hold with seawater. Seawater ruined planting seeds and grain stores, forcing immediate, humiliating dependency upon the local Indigenous population.
Q7. Chief Wingina. Wingina initially provided fish and corn from weirs, but English demands and European disease epidemics caused relations to collapse.
Q8. Sir Francis Drake. Drake arrived after raiding Spanish Florida; facing starvation and Indigenous attacks after Lane killed Wingina, the settlers fled.
Q9. Virginia Dare. Daughter of Ananias Dare and Eleanor White, her birth symbolized the attempt to establish a permanent civilian family colony.
Q10. CROATOAN. Delayed for three years by the Spanish Armada, White found the colony abandoned, with the carve indicating they moved to nearby Croatoan Island.

KT 1.1: The Origins of the Republic, 1918–1919

Knowledge Masterclass
Origins & Constitution
Strategic Context & Geopolitical Overview

In November 1918, facing imminent military collapse on the Western Front, economic exhaustion from the Allied naval blockade, and widespread mutinies across major naval ports, Imperial Germany collapsed. Following the abdication of Kaiser Wilhelm II, moderate socialist Friedrich Ebert established a parliamentary republic. However, the nascent democracy was instantly compromised by the military’s "stab in the back" myth and structural constitutional vulnerabilities.

1. The Legacy of WWI & Military Collapse A Broken Empire
  • Catastrophic Human & Financial Cost: 2 million soldiers dead, 4.2 million wounded, and national debt tripling to 150 billion marks created severe social trauma.
  • Home Front Starvation: The Allied naval blockade caused over 750,000 civilian deaths from malnutrition, culminating in the "Turnip Winter" of 1916–17.
  • Military Deception: Generals Hindenburg and Ludendorff insisted on an armistice but shifted blame to civilian politicians to preserve army honour.
2. Revolution from Below & Abdication Kiel Mutiny to Republic
  • Kiel Mutiny (Oct 1918): Sailors ordered on a suicidal charge mutinied, sparking revolutionary Workers’ and Soldiers’ Councils across Germany.
  • General Strike in Berlin (9 Nov): With the capital paralysed, Chancellor Max von Baden announced Kaiser Wilhelm II’s abdication without his consent.
  • Proclamation of Democracy: SPD deputy Philipp Scheidemann declared the Republic to forestall Karl Liebknecht’s rival communist revolution.
3. The Weimar Constitution of 1919 Progressive yet Fragile
  • Democratic Breakthrough: Universal suffrage at age 20, a comprehensive bill of fundamental rights, and an elected President and Reichstag.
  • Proportional Representation (PR): Resulted in coalition instability (20 cabinets in 14 years), giving radical anti-democratic splinter groups a platform.
  • Article 48 Emergency Powers: Granted the President dictatorial decree powers without parliamentary consent, creating a fatal authoritarian loophole.

KT 1.1: The Origins of the Republic, 1918–1919: Forensic Analysis & Word Bank

Deep Knowledge
Forensic Case Studies
1. The Legacy of the First World War: Socio-Economic Collapse
  • By autumn 1918, Imperial Germany was militarily broken and economically exhausted; over 2 million soldiers were dead, 4.2 million wounded, and the British naval blockade had caused over 750,000 civilian deaths from starvation and hypothermia.
  • War expenditures had tripled the national debt from 50 billion to 150 billion marks, driving catastrophic inflation and widespread social destitution while the "Turnip Winter" of 1916–17 shattered working-class faith in the imperial state.
  • General Erich Ludendorff and Paul von Hindenburg informed Kaiser Wilhelm II that the Western Front had collapsed, cynically advising a transition to civilian government to shift blame for the military catastrophe onto democratic politicians.
2. The German Revolution: Kiel Mutiny to Imperial Abdication
  • On 28 October 1918, naval commanders ordered the High Seas Fleet at Kiel to launch a suicidal sortie against the Royal Navy; sailors refused to sail, mutinied, and took control of the naval base alongside local shipyard workers.
  • Within days, revolutionary Workers’ and Soldiers’ Councils (Räte) seized control of major cities including Hamburg, Hanover, Munich, and Frankfurt, paralyzing imperial administrative authority across Germany.
  • On 9 November 1918, mass general strikes crippled Berlin; Chancellor Max von Baden announced the abdication of Kaiser Wilhelm II without his consent, handing executive authority to moderate SPD leader Friedrich Ebert.
3. The Birth of the Republic & The Ebert-Groener Pact
  • SPD politician Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed the German Republic from a Reichstag balcony on 9 November to pre-empt communist Karl Liebknecht, who declared a "Free Socialist Republic" from the Royal Palace two hours later.
  • On 10 November, Ebert secured a secret telephone pact with General Wilhelm Groener: the Supreme Army Command promised military loyalty to the new republic in exchange for Ebert guaranteeing the army’s autonomy and crushing left-wing revolution.
  • Ebert’s representative Matthias Erzberger signed the Armistice at Compiègne on 11 November 1918, ending WWI hostilities but instantly saddling the nascent republic with the toxic "November Criminals" label.
4. The Weimar Constitution: Democratic Breakthrough & Structural Flaws
  • Drafted in Weimar by Hugo Preuss, the August 1919 Constitution was among Europe’s most progressive: granting universal suffrage at age 20, proportional representation, a bill of rights, and an elected Reichstag and President.
  • Proportional Representation (PR) required 60,000 votes per seat, resulting in fragmented parliaments with up to 28 parties; no single party ever won a majority, forcing 20 unstable coalition governments between 1919 and 1933.
  • Article 48 granted the President sweeping emergency powers to pass decrees without Reichstag approval; designed as a protective safeguard, it became a lethal authoritarian back door exploited by Hindenburg to destroy democracy.

KT 1.2: Early Challenges to the Republic, 1919–1923

Knowledge Masterclass
Crises & Extremism
Strategic Context & Geopolitical Overview

Between 1919 and 1923, the Weimar Republic faced near-continuous existential crises. The punitive terms of the Treaty of Versailles alienated millions of patriotic Germans, while armed rebellions erupted from both the radical left (Spartacists) and the militant right (Kapp Putsch). The crisis culminated in 1923 when the French invaded the Ruhr to extract overdue reparations, triggering passive resistance and hyperinflation that wiped out middle-class savings.

1. The Treaty of Versailles (June 1919) The Punitive Diktat
  • Territorial & Colonial Amputations: Lost 13% of European land (Alsace-Lorraine, Polish Corridor) and all overseas colonies, losing 6 million citizens.
  • Military Disarmament: Army capped at 100,000 volunteers, conscription banned, Rhineland demilitarised, and navy limited to 6 small battleships.
  • Article 231 & Reparations: Forced to accept sole war guilt, saddling the state with an astronomical £6.6 billion (132 billion gold marks) debt.
2. Armed Revolts from Left and Right Street Warfare & Paramilitaries
  • Spartacist Uprising (Jan 1919): Luxemburg and Liebknecht’s communist coup in Berlin brutally crushed by Noske’s Freikorps death squads.
  • Kapp Putsch (March 1920): 12,000 Freikorps seized Berlin; collapsed only after a massive four-day workers’ general strike halted utilities.
  • Political Murders: Right-wing squads assassinated 354 republicans (e.g. Erzberger, Rathenau); biased conservative judges convicted only one killer.
3. The 1923 Ruhr Occupation & Hyperinflation Economic Catastrophe
  • French Occupation (Jan 1923): 60,000 French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr industrial heartland after Germany defaulted on timber payments.
  • Passive Resistance: Weimar ordered workers to strike while printing trillions of paper marks to pay wages without tax or gold backing.
  • Hyperinflation Ruin: By November 1923, $1 USD equalled 4.2 trillion marks; middle-class savings and pensions were permanently vaporised.

1. The Treaty of Versailles & The Dolchstosslegende
  • Signed on 28 June 1919 under Allied threat of military invasion, the Treaty of Versailles was denounced across Germany as a punitive "Diktat" (dictated peace) that stripped Germany of 13% of its European territory and 100% of its overseas colonies.
  • Article 231 (the "War Guilt Clause") forced Germany to accept sole moral responsibility for the war, justifying astronomical reparations fixed in 1921 at £6.6 billion (132 billion gold marks), which crippled state finances.
  • Right-wing nationalists and military elites propagated the "Stab in the Back" myth (Dolchstosslegende), falsely claiming the heroic German Army had remained undefeated in the field and was betrayed from behind by socialist and Jewish politicians.
2. Left-Wing Revolutionary Threats: The Spartacist Uprising (1919)
  • In January 1919, the Communist Spartacus League led by Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht launched an armed insurrection in Berlin, seizing newspaper offices and rail stations to establish a Soviet-style dictatorship.
  • Lacking regular troops, Defense Minister Gustav Noske mobilised the Freikorps—demobilised, fiercely nationalist ex-soldiers equipped with machine guns, flamethrowers, and trench artillery.
  • The Freikorps brutally crushed the uprising within a week, executing over 100 workers; Luxemburg was beaten to death and her corpse dumped in the Landwehr Canal, leaving a permanent, irreconcilable rift between the SPD and KPD.
3. Right-Wing Putschism & Political Murders (1920–22)
  • In March 1920, when Ebert attempted to disband the Erhardt Marine Brigade to comply with Versailles troop limits, 12,000 Freikorps troops led by Wolfgang Kapp marched on Berlin in the Kapp Putsch, forcing the government to flee to Stuttgart.
  • General von Seeckt refused Ebert’s order to fire, declaring "Reichswehr does not fire on Reichswehr"; the putsch collapsed only after Berlin trade unions launched a massive four-day general strike that cut gas, water, and transport.
  • Right-wing death squads (Organisation Consul) assassinated 354 republicans between 1919 and 1922, including Armistice signatory Matthias Erzberger (1921) and Jewish Foreign Minister Walther Rathenau (1922), with judiciary showing blatant right-wing bias.
4. The 1923 Crisis: Occupation of the Ruhr & Hyperinflation
  • In January 1923, after Germany defaulted on timber and telegraph pole reparations, 60,000 French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr industrial basin, seizing factories, coal mines, and railways to extract payments by force.
  • Chancellor Wilhelm Cuno ordered "passive resistance", urging workers to strike while the government paid their wages by recklessly printing paper bank notes without gold backing.
  • Hyperinflation spiraled uncontrollably: by November 1923, a loaf of bread cost 201 billion marks; life savings, pensions, and insurance bonds of the middle class were wiped out overnight, permanently destroying their loyalty to Weimar.

  • Between 1933 and 1939, over 200,000 Germans passed through camps including Sachsenhausen, Buchenwald, and Ravensbrück; the camp population expanded to include Jehovah’s Witnesses, homosexuals, Roma, and persistent criminals.

Historiography • Page 36
1. The Fall of Weimar: Determinism vs. Contingency
Deterministic / "Doomed from the Start"
• Core Thesis: Weimar was structurally fatally flawed from 1919: born of defeat, stabbed in the back, burdened by Versailles, and crippled by PR and Article 48.
• Turning Point: 1919 Versailles signing and 1923 hyperinflation permanently alienating the middle class and army.
• Key Historians: A.J.P. Taylor, William Shirer.
Contingency / "The Great Depression"
• Core Thesis: Weimar had stabilized brilliantly under Stresemann (1924–29); it was destroyed only by the external, unpredicted shock of the 1929 Wall Street Crash.
• Turning Point: October 1929 crash recalling American loans, triggering 6.1 million unemployed and Brüning’s austerity.
• Key Historians: Detlev Peukert, Richard Evans.
Conservative Intrigue / "Backroom Deal"
• Core Thesis: Democracy was deliberately dismantled not by voters, but by an aristocratic military camarilla (Papen, Schleicher, Hindenburg) seeking authoritarian restoration.
• Turning Point: Papen’s secret pact at the Cologne villa in Jan 1933 handing power to Hitler.
• Key Historians: Ian Kershaw, Hans Mommsen.
2. The Nature of Nazi Rule: Intentionalism vs. Structuralism
Intentionalist / "The Masterplan"
• Core Thesis: Hitler was an absolute, all-powerful dictator with an unswerving master plan outlined in *Mein Kampf* (anti-Semitism, Lebensraum, one-party state).
• Mechanism: Every policy (1933 Boycott, Nuremberg Laws, Kristallnacht) was a planned, deliberate step toward total destruction.
• Key Historians: Klaus Hildebrand, Andreas Hillgruber.
Structuralist / "Cumulative Radicalisation"
• Core Thesis: The Nazi state was a chaotic "polycracy" of competing party agencies and SS rivals; Hitler was a "weak dictator" who avoided administrative detail.
• Mechanism: Policies radicalised as rival agencies competed by "working towards the Führer" to anticipate his radical racial desires.
• Key Historians: Martin Broszat, Hans Mommsen.
Synthesis / "The Hitler Myth"
• Core Thesis: Synthesises both: Hitler set broad ideological parameters, while competing bureaucratic structures drove implementation through popular consent and terror.
• Mechanism: The "Hitler Myth" sustained mass loyalty, while Himmler’s SS state executed racial policy without legal limits.
• Key Historians: Sir Ian Kershaw, Richard J. Evans.

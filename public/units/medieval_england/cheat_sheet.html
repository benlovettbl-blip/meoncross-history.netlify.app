1. Master Disciplinary Glossary (39 High-Yield Concepts)

Historical Term Rigorous Academic Definition
Power The ability to control people, events, or resources — a central theme of medieval history.
Claim A demand for the right to rule, based on blood ties, promises, or military strength.
Conquest The act of taking control of a country or territory by military force.
Anglo-Saxon The Germanic peoples who settled in England from the 5th century and ruled until the Norman Conquest of 1066.
Significance The importance of a historical event — why it mattered at the time and why it still matters today.
Feudal system The medieval social hierarchy where the king granted land to lords in exchange for military service, who in turn controlled peasants.
Resistance Actions taken to oppose or fight against those in power.
Propaganda Information (often biased or misleading) used to promote a political cause or point of view.
Domesday Book A comprehensive survey of England ordered by William I in 1086 to record who owned what land and its value.
Motte-and-bailey An early type of Norman castle with a raised mound (motte) topped by a wooden tower and an enclosed courtyard (bailey).
Church The Catholic Church — the most powerful religious institution in medieval Europe, led by the Pope in Rome.
Monarchy Rule by a king or queen. In medieval England, the monarch claimed authority from God (Divine Right).
Martyr A person who is killed because of their religious beliefs, often becoming a powerful symbol.
Excommunication Being expelled from the Catholic Church — the most severe spiritual punishment in the medieval world.
Canon law The system of laws and regulations made by the Church, which often clashed with the king's secular laws.
Magna Carta The 'Great Charter' of 1215, forced upon King John by his barons, limiting royal power and establishing legal rights.
Democracy A system of government where power is held by the people, usually through elected representatives.
Parliament An assembly of representatives that gradually gained the power to make laws and hold the monarch to account.
Interpretation A particular way of explaining or understanding a historical event — historians often disagree in their interpretations.
Baron A powerful nobleman who held land from the king under the feudal system and owed military service in return.
Peasantry The class of poor agricultural labourers who worked the land under the feudal system.
Tithe A tax of one-tenth of a person's produce or income, paid to the Church.
Feudalism The dominant social system in medieval Europe, based on land ownership, loyalty, and obligation.
Serf A peasant who was legally tied to the land they worked and could not leave without their lord's permission.
Strip farming The medieval agricultural system where peasants farmed narrow strips of land scattered across open fields.
Black Death The devastating plague pandemic of 1348-1350 that killed approximately one-third of England's population.
Change A fundamental shift in how things work — the Black Death caused dramatic change to medieval society.
Labour shortage A situation where there are not enough workers available, which gives remaining workers bargaining power.
Statute of Labourers A law passed in 1351 attempting to fix wages at pre-plague levels and prevent peasants from demanding higher pay.
Social order The hierarchical structure of society — in medieval England, this was the feudal pyramid of King, Lords, and Peasants.
Revolt An organised uprising against those in power, driven by grievance and a desire for change.
Poll tax A flat-rate tax charged on every adult, regardless of wealth — widely seen as deeply unfair to the peasantry.
Wat Tyler The leader of the Peasants' Revolt of 1381, killed during negotiations with King Richard II.
Villeinage The condition of being a villein (serf) — tied to the land with obligations to a feudal lord.
Dynasty A series of rulers from the same family, such as the Plantagenets, Yorkists, and Tudors.
Usurper A person who seizes power illegally or by force, without a legitimate claim.
Synthesis Bringing together knowledge from across a whole unit to form an overall judgement.
Evidence Historical facts, sources, or examples used to support an argument or judgement.
Judgement A considered conclusion reached after weighing up different pieces of evidence.

The 4 Golden Rules of Extended Historical Writing (PEEL Standard)
  1. [P] Direct Thematic Point: Open every paragraph with a direct, analytical answer to the enquiry question.
  2. [E] Specific Historical Evidence: Deploy precise names, dates, legislation, casualties, or contemporary quotes.
  3. [E] Causal Explanation: Explain the exact mechanism: why and how one factor caused or accelerated the outcome.
  4. [L] Evaluative Judgement: Weigh factors against explicit criteria to form a sustained, independent historical verdict.

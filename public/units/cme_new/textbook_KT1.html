The Middle East is the ultimate geopolitical crossroads of the earth—the physical land bridge connecting Europe, Asia, and Africa, the guardian of vital maritime chokepoints, and the birthplace of three world religions. To understand the bloody wars between Arabs and Israelis from 1945 to 1995, one must first understand its geography: how European diplomats drew straight ruler lines across deserts, why fresh water from the River Jordan is a matter of national survival, and why controlling the high ground of the Golan Heights or the narrow waters of the Straits of Tiran dictated the difference between victory and annihilation.

Teacher Model / Pupil Reasoning:
For over four centuries leading up to the First World War, the Middle East was ruled as part of the Turkish Ottoman Empire. When the Ottomans allied with Germany in 1914, Britain and France planned to dismember the empire and expand their imperial influence. In May 1916, British diplomat Sir Mark Sykes and French diplomat François Georges-Picot negotiated the secret Sykes-Picot Agreement, carving the region into spheres of influence: France claimed Syria and Lebanon, while Britain took Mesopotamia (Iraq) and Transjordan to secure Persian Gulf oil routes. Palestine was designated an international zone due to the religious sensitivities of Jerusalem. Following Allied victory in 1918, the League of Nations formalised this partition through the Mandates System. These arbitrary straight-line borders ignored historic ethnic, tribal, and religious communities, establishing chronic regional instability.

During the First World War, the British government made contradictory promises to Arab and Jewish leaders to secure wartime support, creating a legacy of mutual betrayal.

1. The McMahon-Hussein Correspondence (1915–16): British High Commissioner Sir Henry McMahon promised Sharif Hussein of Mecca that Britain would recognise an independent Arab state in exchange for an armed Arab revolt against Ottoman forces. Arab leaders understood this pledge to include Palestine.

2. The Balfour Declaration (2 November 1917): Foreign Secretary Arthur Balfour officially wrote to Lord Walter Rothschild pledging British support for the establishment of a "national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine. Crucially, the letter stated that nothing should prejudice the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities, conspicuously ignoring political or national rights for the 90% Arab majority.

The Interwar Mandate & The 1939 White Paper: Rising Jewish immigration during the 1930s—fuelled by Nazi persecution in Europe—sparked intense Palestinian Arab alarm, culminating in the 1936–39 Arab Revolt. Faced with imminent war against Germany and desperate to secure Middle Eastern oil and Arab loyalty, Britain issued the 1939 MacDonald White Paper. This strictly capped Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years and required Arab consent thereafter. The White Paper outraged Zionists as a betrayal during the Holocaust, while leaving Arabs unsatisfied, locking both sides in bitter conflict by 1945.
The physical geography of the Middle East created four critical flashpoints that dictated military operations and causes of war between 1948 and 1973:
  1. The Maritime Chokepoints (Suez Canal & Straits of Tiran): The Suez Canal linked Europe to Asian oil, while the narrow Straits of Tiran at the mouth of the Gulf of Aqaba controlled Israel’s southern sea route from Eilat. Whenever Egypt blockaded the Straits (in 1956 and 1967), it severed Israel's oil supplies, acting as an immediate trigger for war.
  2. The Elevated High Ground (The Golan Heights): A volcanic plateau in south-western Syria rising 3,000 feet above Galilee. Prior to 1967, Syrian artillery used this vantage point to shell Israeli border settlements below.
  3. Freshwater Basins & Territorial Depth (West Bank & River Jordan): The River Jordan and the Sea of Galilee supply vital fresh water. Before 1967, Israel’s narrow central waist was only 9 miles wide, making the West Bank hill ridge a critical natural frontier.
  4. The Sacred Core (Jerusalem & The Old City): One square kilometre of ancient holy sites claimed by Jews, Muslims, and Christians, divided between Israel and Jordan along the 1949 Green Line.

In 1945, the world was reeling from the horrors of the Holocaust, creating immense international pressure for a Jewish homeland. Teacher Model / Pupil Sentence:
Following World War Two in 1945, Great Britain found itself trapped in an untenable position in its Palestine Mandate. Over 250,000 Jewish Holocaust survivors languished in European Displaced Persons (DP) camps, prompting Zionist demands for 100,000 immediate immigration certificates. However, Foreign Secretary Ernest Bevin strictly enforced the 1939 White Paper quota of 1,500 monthly permits to protect British oil interests with Arab states, provoking fierce condemnation from US President Harry S. Truman.
Palestinian Arabs—who formed a two-thirds majority of Palestine—firmly rejected partition and mass Jewish immigration. Weakened by the suppression of the 1936–39 Revolt, Palestinian leaders argued European antisemitism should not be resolved at their expense. The newly formed Arab League warned that imposing a Jewish state on Arab land would trigger armed conflict across the entire Middle East.
Outraged by Bevin’s immigration restrictions, Jewish militias ended their wartime cooperation with Britain and launched the armed Jewish Insurgency. The moderate Haganah allied with militant paramilitary splinter groups, the Irgun (led by Menachem Begin) and Lehi. Together, they executed coordinated sabotage operations, blowing up bridges and severing railway networks to paralyse British military communications.
Between 1945 and 1947, Zionist paramilitary groups escalated attacks against British military infrastructure, raiding airfields, sabotaging oil pipelines, and ambushing colonial patrols. In response to British curfews and martial law, the Irgun waged aggressive psychological warfare, demonstrating that 100,000 British soldiers could not maintain security or governance in Palestine.

On 22 July 1946, the Irgun bombed the King David Hotel in Jerusalem, the central headquarters of British civil administration and military command. Smuggling explosives into the basement in milk cans, the blast collapsed the entire south-western wing, killing 91 British, Arab, and Jewish staff. The attack shocked British domestic opinion and proved the Mandate was becoming financially and militarily unsustainable.

In July 1947, the insurgency reached a climax in the Sergeants Affair: the Irgun abducted and hanged two 20-year-old British Army intelligence sergeants (Clifford Martin and Mervyn Paice) in retaliation for British executions of Irgun militants, booby-trapping their bodies. The incident ignited anti-Jewish riots across British cities and triggered furious public demands to "bring our boys home", destroying any remaining British political resolve.

In July 1947, the Royal Navy intercepted the SS Exodus carrying 4,500 Holocaust survivors and forcibly deported them back to detention camps in Germany. Photographs of British troops turning away destitute refugees provoked global moral outrage and alienated US financial support. Facing economic exhaustion and military quagmire, Britain announced in September 1947 that it would terminate the Mandate and withdraw all forces by 15 May 1948, referring Palestine to the United Nations.
On 29 November 1947, the UN General Assembly approved Resolution 181 to partition Palestine: 55% was allocated to a Jewish state, 45% to an Arab state, with Jerusalem designated an international zone (corpus separatum) under UN control. Zionist leaders accepted partition to gain sovereign legitimacy, but Palestinian Arabs and the Arab League rejected it as an unjust imperial partition of their homeland, triggering immediate civil war.

From December 1947, violent civil conflict erupted as British forces disengaged. In March 1948, the Haganah enacted Plan Dalet (Plan D) to secure strategic roads and Jewish settlements between Tel Aviv and Jerusalem. Intense fighting and the Irgun-Lehi massacre of over 100 villagers at Deir Yassin (9 April 1948) ignited widespread panic, prompting over 250,000 Palestinian Arabs to abandon their homes and flee.

[[SRC_MARKER:L1_Source_15]]

On 14 May 1948, as the British Mandate officially expired and the last British troops departed, David Ben-Gurion declared the birth of the sovereign State of Israel beneath a portrait of Theodor Herzl in Tel Aviv. The United States and Soviet Union immediately extended diplomatic recognition, while neighboring Arab states mobilised for an all-out military assault.
On 15 May 1948, five Arab League armies (Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, Iraq, and Lebanon) invaded Israel. Although initially pushed to the brink, Israel used the four-week First UN Truce (June 1948) with decisive effect: secretly importing modern weapons and fighter aircraft from Czechoslovakia, and integrating forces into the unified Israeli Defence Forces (IDF). Resuming combat in July 1948 in the "Ten Days Offensive", the IDF reopened the besieged corridor to Jerusalem and drove Egyptian forces from the Negev. Subsequent autumn campaigns secured the entire Galilee, compelling Arab commanders to negotiate separate armistices.
Between February and July 1949, UN armistice agreements established the Green Line. Israel expanded its territory to 79% of mandatory Palestine—far exceeding the UN Partition allocation. Transjordan occupied and annexed the West Bank and East Jerusalem, while Egypt controlled the Gaza Strip. Over 700,000 Palestinian Arabs were permanently displaced from their homes, an enduring catastrophe Palestinians mourn as the Nakba.

The 1948–49 war resulted in what Palestinians mourn as the Nakba ("Catastrophe"), displacing approximately 700,000 Palestinian Arabs from their ancestral homes and villages. Flight was driven by wartime terror from massacres like Deir Yassin, the collapse of local administration, and military expulsions under Plan Dalet. Source C: The 1949 Armistice Green Line Archival Evidence • Expand ▼
[[SRC_MARKER:L2_Source_3]]
Map showing the 1949 armistice lines: Israel gained 78% of the territory, while Jordan annexed the West Bank and Egypt administered Gaza.
The 1949 Armistice Agreements fundamentally redrew the political map along the Green Line. Israel expanded its territorial control to 79% of mandatory Palestine—over 20% more than allocated by the 1947 UN Partition Plan. The proposed Palestinian Arab state was extinguished: the Kingdom of Jordan occupied and annexed the West Bank and East Jerusalem, while Egypt placed the Gaza Strip under military administration, leaving Jerusalem bitterly divided by concrete walls and barbed wire.
In December 1948, UN General Assembly Resolution 194 stated that Palestinian refugees wishing to return and live at peace should be permitted to do so, or receive financial compensation. Israel strictly rejected this "Right of Return", arguing it would destroy the Jewish demographic majority and endanger national security. Conversely, Arab host states (except Jordan) refused to grant refugees citizenship, leaving generations stranded in permanent, impoverished UNRWA camps.
In July 1950, the Israeli Knesset passed the landmark Law of Return, granting every Jewish person worldwide the automatic right to settle in Israel and receive immediate citizenship. This triggered mass immigration of over 700,000 newcomers within four years, absorbing European Holocaust survivors and Sephardic and Mizrahi Jews fleeing persecution across Arab nations. This influx doubled Israel’s population, providing vital workforce and military manpower despite severe economic rationing.
To guarantee survival along vulnerable borders, Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion unified all independent underground militias under the official Israeli Defence Forces (IDF). Enacted under the Defence Service Law of 1949, Israel instituted universal compulsory military conscription for all 18-year-old citizens (men and women), backed by regular reserve service until age 45. This forged Israel into a "nation in arms" capable of mobilising a combat-ready citizen army within 48 hours.
In its early years, Israel faced near-total economic insolvency from mass immigration and military expenditure. Survival was secured through two crucial financial lifelines. First, US President Harry S. Truman provided over $135 million in emergency loans and grants, cementing a vital diplomatic and financial partnership. Second, in September 1952, Ben-Gurion signed the West German Reparations Agreement, securing $822 million to finance modern industrial infrastructure, agricultural irrigation, and merchant shipping.

[[SRC_MARKER:L2_Source_8]]

Hostile relations persisted as Arab neighbours enforced a strict diplomatic and trade boycott against Israel. Egypt banned Israeli shipping from the Suez Canal and established a military blockade at the Straits of Tiran (Sharm el-Sheikh), choking off Israeli trade from Eilat. Concurrently, displaced Palestinians launched cross-border guerrilla raids known as Fedayeen ("self-sacrificers") from Gaza and the West Bank to lay mines, ambush roads, and sabotage infrastructure.
To establish psychological deterrence along its borders, Israel adopted an aggressive reprisal policy: every cross-border raid was met with disproportionate military retaliation. In August 1953, the IDF formed Unit 101, a commando unit led by Ariel Sharon. Following a fatal civilian attack in Israel, Unit 101 raided the West Bank village of Qibya, blowing up 45 houses and killing 69 civilians. While drawing intense global condemnation, the raid solidified Israel’s uncompromising deterrence doctrine.

HISTORICAL OVERVIEW • 1945–1995 TERRITORY, FAITH & DIPLOMACY

Imperial promises carved across desert sand, post-Holocaust desperation, and sacred claims to the same ancient earth ignited fifty years of unrelenting conflict. From the fiery collapse of the British Mandate and the 1948–49 War of Independence to Gamal Abdel Nasser’s defiance during the 1956 Suez Crisis, the lightning preemptive strikes of the 1967 Six-Day War, and the existential shock of the 1973 Yom Kippur assault, the Middle East became the world’s most volatile geopolitical crucible.

As displaced Palestinian refugees mobilized through the PLO and asymmetric resistance, the contest evolved from interstate warfare into insurgent confrontation and urban intifada. Yet amidst recurrent tragedy, courageous diplomatic gambits emerged—from Anwar Sadat’s historic journey to Jerusalem and the Camp David Accords to the secret Norwegian backchannels of the Oslo Peace Process. This master classroom textbook provides an authoritative, unbroken 28-page historical narrative charting five decades of war, diplomacy, and the elusive search for peace.

The Post-War Crisis & The Collapse of the British Mandate, 1945–1947

KEY ENQUIRY QUESTION Why did British imperial control in Mandatory Palestine collapse between 1945 and 1947?
[1.1] 1945

The Post-War Crisis & Conflicting Demands

Following World War Two in 1945, Great Britain found itself trapped in an untenable position in its Palestine Mandate. Over 250,000 Jewish Holocaust survivors languished in European Displaced Persons (DP) camps, prompting Zionist demands for 100,000 immediate immigration certificates. However, Foreign Secretary Ernest Bevin strictly enforced the 1939 White Paper quota of 1,500 monthly permits to protect British oil interests with Arab states, provoking fierce condemnation from US President Harry S. Truman.

[1.2]

Arab Opposition & The Arab League

Palestinian Arabs—who formed a two-thirds majority of Palestine—firmly rejected partition and mass Jewish immigration. Weakened by the suppression of the 1936–39 Revolt, Palestinian leaders argued European antisemitism should not be resolved at their expense. The newly formed Arab League warned that imposing a Jewish state on Arab land would trigger armed conflict across the entire Middle East.

[1.3] Autumn 1945

The Jewish Insurgency Begins

Outraged by Bevin’s immigration restrictions, Jewish militias ended their wartime cooperation with Britain and launched the armed Jewish Insurgency. The moderate Haganah allied with militant paramilitary splinter groups, the Irgun (led by Menachem Begin) and Lehi. Together, they executed coordinated sabotage operations, blowing up bridges and severing railway networks to paralyse British military communications.

[1.4] 1946

Urban Guerrilla Warfare & Sabotage

Between 1945 and 1947, Zionist paramilitary groups escalated attacks against British military infrastructure, raiding airfields, sabotaging oil pipelines, and ambushing colonial patrols. In response to British curfews and martial law, the Irgun waged aggressive psychological warfare, demonstrating that 100,000 British soldiers could not maintain security or governance in Palestine.

DECISIVE HISTORICAL MECHANISM Paramilitary insurgency, domestic economic exhaustion, and international outrage over Holocaust refugees rendered the British Mandate ungovernable, forcing Britain to surrender Palestine to the United Nations.
[1.5] 22 July 1946

The Bombing of the King David Hotel

On 22 July 1946, the Irgun bombed the King David Hotel in Jerusalem, the central headquarters of British civil administration and military command. Smuggling explosives into the basement in milk cans, the blast collapsed the entire south-western wing, killing 91 British, Arab, and Jewish staff. The attack shocked British domestic opinion and proved the Mandate was becoming financially and militarily unsustainable.

[1.6] July 1947

The Sergeants Affair & British Public Backlash

In July 1947, the insurgency reached a climax in the Sergeants Affair: the Irgun abducted and hanged two 20-year-old British Army intelligence sergeants (Clifford Martin and Mervyn Paice) in retaliation for British executions of Irgun militants, booby-trapping their bodies. The incident ignited anti-Jewish riots across British cities and triggered furious public demands to "bring our boys home", destroying any remaining British political resolve.

[1.7] Summer 1947

The SS Exodus & Handover to the UN

In July 1947, the Royal Navy intercepted the SS Exodus carrying 4,500 Holocaust survivors and forcibly deported them back to detention camps in Germany. Photographs of British troops turning away destitute refugees provoked global moral outrage and alienated US financial support. Facing economic exhaustion and military quagmire, Britain announced in September 1947 that it would terminate the Mandate and withdraw all forces by 15 May 1948, referring Palestine to the United Nations.

UN Partition, Civil War & The Creation of Israel, 1947–1949

KEY ENQUIRY QUESTION How was the State of Israel established and defended amidst communal civil war and international invasion?
[2.1] November 1947

UN Resolution 181: The Partition Plan

On 29 November 1947, the UN General Assembly approved Resolution 181 to partition Palestine: 55% was allocated to a Jewish state, 45% to an Arab state, with Jerusalem designated an international zone (corpus separatum) under UN control. Zionist leaders accepted partition to gain sovereign legitimacy, but Palestinian Arabs and the Arab League rejected it as an unjust imperial partition of their homeland, triggering immediate civil war.

[2.2] Spring 1948

Communal Civil War & Plan Dalet

From December 1947, violent civil conflict erupted as British forces progressively disengaged. In March 1948, the Haganah launched Plan Dalet (Plan D)—an operational military plan designed to secure Jewish settlements, open vital supply corridors (especially the besieged road between Tel Aviv and Jerusalem), and clear hostile villages in anticipation of invasion by regular Arab armies. The civil war descended into a vicious spiral of communal violence and retaliatory massacres. On 9 April 1948, radical underground fighters from the Irgun and Lehi attacked the Arab village of Deir Yassin, killing over 100 villagers including women and children (an atrocity publicly condemned by the Haganah leadership and Jewish Agency). Four days later, on 13 April, Arab militants ambushed the Hadassah medical convoy on Mount Scopus, killing 78 Jewish doctors, nurses, and university students in retaliation. News and exaggerated rumours of these atrocities spread terror across the region, triggering the flight and expulsion of over 250,000 Palestinian Arabs before the formal expiration of the British Mandate.

[2.3] 14 May 1948

The Declaration of the State of Israel

On 14 May 1948, as the British Mandate officially expired and the last British troops departed, David Ben-Gurion declared the birth of the sovereign State of Israel beneath a portrait of Theodor Herzl in Tel Aviv. The United States and Soviet Union immediately extended diplomatic recognition, while neighboring Arab states mobilised for an all-out military assault.

[2.4]

The 1948–49 War, First UN Truce & Czech Arms

On 15 May 1948, five Arab League armies (Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, Iraq, and Lebanon) invaded Israel. Although initially pushed to the brink, Israel used the four-week First UN Truce (June 1948) with decisive effect: secretly importing modern weapons and fighter aircraft from Czechoslovakia, and integrating forces into the unified Israeli Defence Forces (IDF). Resuming combat in July 1948 in the "Ten Days Offensive", the IDF reopened the besieged corridor to Jerusalem and drove Egyptian forces from the Negev. Subsequent autumn campaigns secured the entire Galilee, compelling Arab commanders to negotiate separate armistices.

[2.5]

The 1949 Armistice Agreements & The Green Line

Between February and July 1949, UN armistice agreements established the Green Line. Israel expanded its territory to 79% of mandatory Palestine—far exceeding the UN Partition allocation. Transjordan occupied and annexed the West Bank and East Jerusalem, while Egypt controlled the Gaza Strip. Over 700,000 Palestinian Arabs were permanently displaced from their homes, an enduring catastrophe Palestinians mourn as the Nakba.

The Aftermath of the 1948–49 War & The Palestinian Refugee Crisis

KEY ENQUIRY QUESTION What were the territorial, human, and geopolitical consequences of the 1948–49 War?
[3.1] 1948–1949

The Palestinian Nakba & Creation of UNRWA

The 1948–49 war resulted in what Palestinians mourn as the Nakba ("Catastrophe"), displacing approximately 700,000 Palestinian Arabs from their ancestral homes and villages. Flight was driven by wartime terror from massacres like Deir Yassin, the collapse of local administration, and military expulsions under Plan Dalet. Refugees fled into squalid tent camps in the West Bank, Gaza, Jordan, Syria, and Lebanon. In December 1949, the UN established UNRWA to provide emergency food, medical clinics, and schooling across 59 refugee camps.

[3.2]

Territorial Changes & The 1949 Green Line

The 1949 Armistice Agreements fundamentally redrew the political map along the Green Line. Israel expanded its territorial control to 79% of mandatory Palestine—over 20% more than allocated by the 1947 UN Partition Plan. The proposed Palestinian Arab state was extinguished: the Kingdom of Jordan occupied and annexed the West Bank and East Jerusalem, while Egypt placed the Gaza Strip under military administration, leaving Jerusalem bitterly divided by concrete walls and barbed wire.

[3.3]

UN Resolution 194 & The Refugee Deadlock

In December 1948, UN General Assembly Resolution 194 stated that Palestinian refugees wishing to return and live at peace should be permitted to do so, or receive financial compensation. Israel strictly rejected this "Right of Return", arguing it would destroy the Jewish demographic majority and endanger national security. Conversely, Arab host states (except Jordan) refused to grant refugees citizenship, leaving generations stranded in permanent, impoverished UNRWA camps.

[3.4]

The Law of Return (1950) & Demographic Boom

In July 1950, the Israeli Knesset passed the landmark Law of Return, granting every Jewish person worldwide the automatic right to settle in Israel and receive immediate citizenship. This triggered mass immigration of over 700,000 newcomers within four years, rapidly doubling Israel’s population. Crucially, alongside European Holocaust survivors from Displaced Persons camps, Israel absorbed hundreds of thousands of Sephardic and Mizrahi Jews fleeing anti-Jewish riots, legal disenfranchisement, and property confiscation across the Arab world. Between 1948 and the 1970s, approximately 850,000 Jews left or were expelled from historic communities in Iraq, Yemen (Operation Magic Carpet), Egypt, Syria, and North Africa. Most arrived destitute and were housed in primitive canvas transit camps (ma'abarot) amid strict food rationing, creating a second major refugee population in the Middle East and fundamentally transforming Israeli society.

[3.5]

Creation of the Israeli Defence Forces (IDF) & Universal Conscription

To guarantee survival along vulnerable borders, Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion unified all independent underground militias under the official Israeli Defence Forces (IDF). Enacted under the Defence Service Law of 1949, Israel instituted universal compulsory military conscription for all 18-year-old citizens (men and women), backed by regular reserve service until age 45. This forged Israel into a "nation in arms" capable of mobilising a combat-ready citizen army within 48 hours.

[3.6]

Economic Survival: US Financial Aid & Reparations

In its early years, Israel faced near-total economic insolvency from mass immigration and military expenditure. Survival was secured through two crucial financial lifelines. First, US President Harry S. Truman provided over $135 million in emergency loans and grants, cementing a vital diplomatic and financial partnership. Second, in September 1952, Ben-Gurion signed the West German Reparations Agreement, securing $822 million to finance modern industrial infrastructure, agricultural irrigation, and merchant shipping.

[3.7]

Arab Blockades & Cross-Border Fedayeen Raids

Hostile relations persisted as Arab neighbours enforced a strict diplomatic and trade boycott against Israel. Egypt banned Israeli shipping from the Suez Canal and established a military blockade at the Straits of Tiran (Sharm el-Sheikh), choking off Israeli trade from Eilat. Concurrently, displaced Palestinians launched cross-border guerrilla raids known as Fedayeen ("self-sacrificers") from Gaza and the West Bank to lay mines, ambush roads, and sabotage infrastructure.

[3.8] 1953

Israeli Deterrence Doctrine & Retaliatory Raids

To establish psychological deterrence along its borders, Israel adopted an aggressive reprisal policy: every cross-border raid was met with disproportionate military retaliation. In August 1953, the IDF formed Unit 101, a commando unit led by Ariel Sharon. Following a fatal civilian attack in Israel, Unit 101 raided the West Bank village of Qibya, blowing up 45 houses and killing 69 civilians. While drawing intense global condemnation, the raid solidified Israel’s uncompromising deterrence doctrine.

Increased Tension, Nasser, and the Suez Crisis, 1955–1963

KEY ENQUIRY QUESTION Why did the nationalisation of the Suez Canal trigger a global crisis in 1956?
[4.1] 1954

Gamal Abdel Nasser & Egyptian Arab Leadership

In 1954, Colonel Gamal Abdel Nasser became President of Egypt, emerging as the undisputed champion of Pan-Arabism—uniting Arab nations to end Western colonial influence and confront Israel. Nasser negotiated the withdrawal of 80,000 British troops from the Suez Canal Zone and planned the colossal Aswan High Dam on the River Nile to generate electricity, control flooding, and irrigate desert agriculture, requiring major foreign financing.

[4.2]

The Gaza Raid (Feb 1955) & The Czech Arms Deal

Tensions escalated on 28 February 1955 when Israeli paratroopers launched a devastating raid into the Gaza Strip, killing 37 Egyptian soldiers and exposing Egyptian military vulnerability. Humiliated, Nasser sought modern weapons. When Western powers refused sales without political conditions, Nasser signed the Czech Arms Deal in September 1955, importing 200 Soviet MiG-15 jets and 300 tanks via Czechoslovakia. This broke the Western arms monopoly and drew the Cold War into the Middle East.

[4.3] July 1956

Cancellation of Aswan Dam Loans & Suez Nationalisation

Alarmed by Nasser’s Soviet arms deal and recognition of Communist China, the United States and Britain abruptly cancelled funding for the Aswan High Dam in July 1956. Nasser reacted defiantly. On 26 July 1956 in Alexandria, Nasser announced the nationalisation of the Suez Canal Company, declaring that Egypt would use its £35 million annual tolls to finance the dam. British Prime Minister Anthony Eden viewed Nasser’s control of imperial oil routes as an intolerable threat to Britain’s global standing.

[4.4] October 1956

The Secret Tripartite Agreement

Canal nationalisation outraged Britain and France. France sought to punish Nasser for aiding Algerian rebels, while Israel wanted to crush fedayeen bases and reopen the Straits of Tiran. In October 1956, the three nations secretly signed the tripartite agreement: Israel would launch a surprise invasion of Sinai, allowing Britain and France to issue an ultimatum demanding both sides withdraw from the canal. When Egypt refused, Anglo-French forces would invade Port Said under the guise of "peacekeepers" to seize the canal and depose Nasser.

[4.5] Oct–Nov 1956

The Sinai Campaign & Tripartite Invasion

On 29 October 1956, Israel launched the Sinai campaign, advancing across the desert to Sharm el-Sheikh and lifting the blockade of the Straits of Tiran. Britain and France issued their pre-planned ultimatum. When Egypt refused to withdraw from its sovereign territory, Anglo-French forces bombed airfields and landed troops at Port Said on 5 November. Nasser responded by scuttling over 40 ships, completely blocking the Suez Canal to international shipping.

[4.6]

Superpower Intervention: Eisenhower’s Financial Ultimatum

The invasion collapsed under decisive superpower pressure. Furious at being deceived, US President Dwight D. Eisenhower refused emergency IMF loans and threatened to collapse the British pound sterling by dumping US holdings of British currency, while the Soviet Union threatened rocket attacks on London and Paris. Facing imminent financial bankruptcy, British Prime Minister Anthony Eden was forced to order a ceasefire on 6 November 1956, compelling an ignominious military retreat.

[4.7]

Geopolitical Consequences of the Suez Crisis

The Suez Crisis permanently transformed Middle Eastern and global geopolitics:

• End of European Imperial Power: Britain and France were exposed as second-tier powers unable to act without US consent; Prime Minister Anthony Eden resigned in disgrace.

• Nasser’s Political Triumph: Despite battlefield defeat, Nasser emerged as an undisputed Pan-Arab folk hero for defying European empires and retaining control of the canal.

• Israeli Strategic Gains: The IDF demonstrated decisive military superiority, opening the Gulf of Aqaba to Israeli commerce and establishing effective border deterrence.

• Cold War Realignment: The Middle East became a direct Cold War arena, with expanding US support for Israel and Soviet patronage for Egypt and Syria.

[4.8] 1958

UNEF Peacekeepers & The United Arab Republic

In 1957, the United Nations deployed the United Nations Emergency Force (UNEF) along the Gaza-Sinai frontier and Sharm el-Sheikh to deter fedayeen attacks and guarantee passage through the Straits of Tiran. Capitalising on his anti-imperialist prestige, Nasser formed the United Arab Republic (UAR) in February 1958, a political union between Egypt and Syria that cemented Egypt’s leadership of the Arab world.

The Outbreak of the Six-Day War (1964–1967)

KEY ENQUIRY QUESTION How did water disputes, border raids, and escalating rhetoric make a second Arab-Israeli war inevitable?
[5.1]

The 1964 Cairo Conference & The Water Wars

In January 1964, Arab League leaders gathered at the Cairo Summit, convened by Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser. The catalyst was Israel's National Water Carrier, a pipeline diverting water from the Sea of Galilee to the Negev desert . Arab leaders viewed this as economic expansionism. In response, the summit voted to establish the Palestine Liberation Organisation (PLO) and approved an Arab plan to divert the River Jordan headwaters in Syria and Lebanon, threatening roughly one-third of Israel’s freshwater supply .

This dispute quickly ignited military clashes. Between 1964 and 1966, Israeli tanks and aircraft bombarded Syrian engineering sites, halting the diversion canals. Simultaneously, Yasser Arafat's Fatah began cross-border guerrilla raids targeting Israeli water infrastructure and railways. Following a radical military coup in February 1966, Syria’s new government openly sponsored Fatah’s "popular liberation war" against Israel, transforming regional water disputes into active frontier warfare.

[5.2]

Syrian Border Clashes & Israeli Reprisals

Between 1965 and 1967, the Golan Heights became the Middle East’s most volatile frontier. Syrian artillery batteries atop the 1,000-foot volcanic plateau held tactical dominance over low-lying Israeli kibbutzim in the Hula Valley . Syrian gunners regularly shelled farmers cultivating disputed demilitarised zones, forcing Israeli tractors to be custom-armored and school children to sleep in reinforced underground bunkers.

In response, Israeli Chief of Staff Yitzhak Rabin and Prime Minister Levi Eshkol enforced aggressive military retaliation, declaring that host Arab states were directly responsible for guerrilla raids. This hardline doctrine created an escalatory cycle: Fatah landmines triggered Israeli air strikes and artillery barrages, which in turn invited Syrian counter-bombardments and fueled Arab demands for war.

The Samu Raid & The 7 April 1967 Dogfight

In late 1966, escalating friction triggered major military confrontations. On 11 November 1966, a Fatah landmine killed three Israeli soldiers near the border. Despite King Hussein of Jordan attempting to curb guerrillas, Israel launched a massive daytime reprisal against the West Bank town of Samu on 13 November 1966, destroying dozens of houses and killing 15 Jordanian soldiers. The Samu raid triggered violent riots across the West Bank against King Hussein.

The decisive flashpoint occurred on 7 April 1967. Following Syrian shelling of an Israeli tractor, an aerial battle erupted. Israeli Mirage jets shot down six Syrian MiG-21s, chasing them to Damascus . This humiliating defeat prompted Syrian broadcasts to accuse Nasser of cowardice for hiding behind UNEF peacekeepers in the Sinai, placing intense political pressure on Cairo to mobilize for war.

[5.4]

Why Was War Inevitable by Spring 1967?

By May 1967, war had become inevitable due to three converging catalysts: competition over the River Jordan water supply, state-sponsored Fatah guerrilla sabotage, and Israel's doctrine of disproportionate military retaliation. Each local clash escalated regional hostility beyond diplomatic control.

Crucially, when false Soviet intelligence claimed in mid-May that Israel was massing twelve brigades to invade Syria, Nasser felt compelled to mobilize Egyptian troops into the Sinai. Neither side initially sought a total war, but rhetorical brinkmanship and interlocking mutual defense commitments made peaceful de-escalation impossible.

The Course of the Six-Day War (May–June 1967)

KEY ENQUIRY QUESTION Why was the Six-Day War decided in three hours, and did Nasser intend to fight?
[6.1] May 1967

The Spiral into Crisis

On 13 May 1967, the Soviet Union gave Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser false intelligence claiming Israel was massing twelve brigades to invade Syria. Though UN observers found no buildup, Nasser acted swiftly to reclaim Arab leadership: he deployed 100,000 troops and 1,000 tanks into Sinai, expelled the UN Emergency Force (UNEF) on 16 May, and closed the Straits of Tiran on 22 May.

Closing the Straits of Tiran severed Israel's sole maritime trade corridor to Asia and blocked 90% of its oil imports through Eilat—an explicit act of war (casus belli). When King Hussein of Jordan flew to Cairo on 30 May to sign a mutual defense pact, Israel faced military encirclement. Fearing national destruction, Prime Minister Levi Eshkol formed a national unity government and appointed war hero Moshe Dayan as Minister of Defence to launch a pre-emptive strike.

[6.2] 5 June 1967

The Pre-Emptive Dawn Airstrike

At 7:45 am on 5 June 1967, Israel launched a pre-emptive dawn airstrike. Leaving only twelve jets to defend home skies, nearly 200 Israeli aircraft flew low over the Mediterranean—just 30 feet above the waves—to evade Egyptian radar. They struck seventeen Egyptian airbases from the west at breakfast time, catching Egyptian pilots completely unprepared.

Using rocket-boosted cratering bombs, Israeli pilots cratered runways before strafing parked aircraft. In under three hours, over 300 Egyptian combat planes were destroyed on the ground . When Syria and Jordan launched retaliatory air strikes, Israeli jets destroyed their air forces as well, winning total air supremacy and leaving Arab ground forces completely exposed.

Three Fronts in Six Days

How the Map Changed in Six Days

In just six days, Israel quadrupled its territory, capturing the Sinai Peninsula, Gaza Strip, West Bank, East Jerusalem, and the Golan Heights. Israeli losses were under 1,000 soldiers, while Arab armies suffered over 15,000 Egyptian, 6,000 Jordanian, and 1,000 Syrian casualties.

While these conquests provided strategic buffer zones, they brought over one million Palestinian Arabs under Israeli military occupation, sparking a fresh refugee exodus. The catastrophic humiliation suffered by Arab states ensured that rather than securing permanent peace, the 1967 war laid the groundwork for future armed conflict.

Conquered Territories & "Land for Peace" (1967)

KEY ENQUIRY QUESTION "Land for Peace" or permanent stalemate: how did the 1967 conquered territories transform the conflict?
[7.1] Summer 1967

The Reality of the Occupied Territories

The June 1967 ceasefire left Israel in control of vast lands : the Sinai Peninsula and Gaza Strip from Egypt, the West Bank and East Jerusalem from Jordan, and the Golan Heights from Syria. These created natural buffer zones—the Suez Canal, Jordan River, and Golan cliffs—shielding Israel’s heartland from surprise ground assault.

However, occupation created immense humanitarian dilemmas. Israel brought over one million Palestinian Arabs under military administration, while 300,000 Palestinians fled across the Jordan River into refugee camps. Israel immediately annexed East Jerusalem as its unified capital and soon authorized the first civilian Jewish settlements in the Jordan Valley and Gush Etzion, entrenching future territorial disputes.

[7.2]

The Khartoum Summit & The"Three Noes"

To coordinate an Arab response to defeat, eight Arab heads of state convened in Khartoum, Sudan, in August 1967. While King Hussein urged quiet diplomacy to reclaim lost lands, hardliners led by Nasser insisted that negotiating from weakness would legitimize Israeli conquest and humiliate the Arab world.

The conference concluded with the famous "Three Noes" of Khartoum: "No peace with Israel, no recognition of Israel, no negotiations with it" . To Israelis, this rigid stance proved Arab leaders remained dedicated to Israel's destruction, leading Israel's cabinet to abandon early plans for territorial compromise and harden resolve to retain the occupied territories.

UN Resolution 242 &"Land for Peace"

On 22 November 1967, the UN Security Council unanimously passed Resolution 242, establishing the "Land for Peace" principle. Drafted by Britain’s Lord Caradon, it asserted the "inadmissibility of the acquisition of territory by war," calling for Israeli armed forces to withdraw from occupied areas in return for Arab states recognizing Israel's right to live in peace within secure borders.

However, deliberate linguistic ambiguity crippled implementation. The English text called for withdrawal from "territories occupied" (some territories), whereas the French text specified "des territoires occupés" (all territories) . Furthermore, Resolution 242 addressed Palestinians only as a generic "refugee problem," entirely omitting their national right to self-determination.

[7.4]

The Roots of Entrenched Conflict

In the long term, the 1967 conquests replaced the quest for peace with a diplomatic stalemate. Israel fortified its borders—building the Bar-Lev Line along the Suez Canal—and subsidized Jewish settlements, while Arab leaders maintained the Khartoum "Three Noes."

Consequently, Palestinians concluded that conventional Arab armies would never liberate their homeland, accelerating the growth of independent guerrilla groups like the PLO. Without diplomatic progress, Resolution 242 could not avert the impending War of Attrition and the 1973 Yom Kippur War.

The Rise of Palestinian Resistance: The PLO, Black September & Munich (1968–1972)

KEY ENQUIRY QUESTION How did Palestinian nationalism transform from reliance on Arab states into independent armed resistance?
[8.1] 1968–1969

From Dependence to Armed Resistance

The 1967 defeat shattered Palestinian faith in Arab leaders like Nasser. Concluding they had to liberate their homeland themselves, young Palestinians joined armed guerrilla groups (fedayeen). On 21 March 1968, at the Battle of Karameh in Jordan, Yasser Arafat's Fatah fighters and Jordanian artillery fiercely resisted an Israeli armored reprisal raid.

Although Israel destroyed the guerrilla camp, Fatah inflicted 28 Israeli fatalities. Karameh was celebrated across the Arab world as a heroic moral victory. Thousands of recruits joined Fatah, and in February 1969, Yasser Arafat was elected Chairman of the Palestine Liberation Organisation (PLO), converting it into an independent armed national movement.

[8.2] 1970

Dawson's Field & The Crisis of Black September

By 1970, Palestinian guerrillas operated as a "state within a state" in Jordan, defying King Hussein’s laws. To capture international attention, George Habash’s Marxist PFLP pioneered airline hijackings. In September 1970, militants hijacked four Western jetliners, forcing three to land at Dawson's Field in the Jordanian desert.

After evacuating over 300 passengers, the hijackers blew up the empty airliners on camera . Viewing this as a direct challenge to his monarchy, King Hussein declared martial law on 17 September 1970. In the brutal civil conflict known as Black September, the Jordanian army crushed Palestinian militias, expelling the PLO to southern Lebanon by 1971.

The 1972 Munich Olympics Massacre

Exiled in Lebanon, a radical covert faction named Black September sought revenge. On 5 September 1972, during the Munich Olympic Games, eight Black September gunmen infiltrated the Olympic Village, killing two Israeli athletes and taking nine others hostage.

Militants demanded the release of 234 Palestinian prisoners. Israeli Prime Minister Golda Meir refused to negotiate with terrorists. A botched West German rescue attempt at Fürstenfeldbruck airbase ended in disaster: all nine Israeli hostages, five terrorists, and one German policeman were killed . In response, Meir launched Operation Wrath of God, authorizing Mossad to track down and assassinate the organizers.

[8.4]

Did Terrorism Help or Harm the Cause?

International terrorism had contradictory outcomes. On one hand, high-profile attacks like Munich thrust the Palestinian national struggle onto the world stage, forcing the UN in 1974 to recognize the PLO as the legitimate representative of the Palestinian people.

On the other hand, terrorism caused severe strategic harm. It provoked the bloodshed of Black September, alienated Western public opinion, and invited devastating Israeli reprisals. Recognising that violence alone could not defeat Israel, Arafat began steering the PLO toward international diplomacy by the mid-1970s.

The War of Attrition & The Yom Kippur War (1969–1973)

KEY ENQUIRY QUESTION How did the surprise assault on Yom Kippur shatter the myth of Israeli invincibility and transform Middle Eastern diplomacy?
[9.1] 1969–1973

The Impasse of Attrition & Anwar Sadat's Plan

Between 1969 and 1970, Nasser waged the War of Attrition across the Suez Canal, attempting to exhaust Israeli forces through relentless artillery bombardments. In response, Israel built the Bar-Lev Line, a fortified chain of sand ramparts over 60 feet high. A US-brokered ceasefire ended fighting in August 1970 after thousands of deaths, leaving frontlines unchanged.

Following Nasser's death in September 1970, new Egyptian President Anwar Sadat developed a pragmatic military strategy. Realising Egypt’s economy was crippled and Israel would never surrender Sinai through diplomacy while holding military superiority, Sadat planned a limited surprise offensive to shatter the myth of Israeli invincibility and force American diplomatic intervention.

[9.2] 6 October 1973

The Surprise Assault on the Holy Day

On 6 October 1973, at 2:00 pm, Egypt and Syria launched a joint surprise attack on Yom Kippur (the holiest day in Judaism) during the Muslim holy month of Ramadan. With Israeli soldiers on leave, Syrian tanks assaulted the Golan Heights while 80,000 Egyptian infantry crossed the Suez Canal under a 2,000-gun artillery barrage.

Egyptian engineers used high-pressure water cannons to blast breaches through the Bar-Lev sand ramparts within hours . Israeli tank and air counter-attacks were shattered by Soviet-supplied SAM-6 anti-aircraft missiles and wire-guided Sagger anti-tank missiles, inflicting heavy early losses on Israeli forces.

The 1973 war was a psychological earthquake. For Israel, losing over 2,600 soldiers shattered military complacency, prompting the Agranat Commission inquiry and the resignations of military leaders and Prime Minister Golda Meir in 1974.

For Egypt, crossing the canal restored national pride, giving Sadat the political authority to negotiate with Israel from strength. The crisis convinced Israel that occupation did not ensure security, while the oil embargo forced US commitment to regional peacemaking—paving the way for Kissinger's shuttle diplomacy and the 1978 Camp David Accords.

Diplomatic Negotiations: From Shuttle Diplomacy to Camp David, 1974–1979

KEY ENQUIRY QUESTION How did bilateral diplomacy between Egypt and Israel achieve peace, while leaving the Palestinian issue unresolved?
[10.1] 1973–1975

The 1973 Oil Shock & Kissinger's Shuttle Diplomacy

The 1973 Yom Kippur War caused an unprecedented global economic crisis. Angered by emergency US military airlifts to Israel, Arab OPEC members enacted the "oil weapon," cutting oil production and imposing a total embargo on the United States and the Netherlands. Consequently, crude oil prices quadrupled from $3 to $12 a barrel, triggering fuel shortages, soaring inflation, and severe "stagflation" across Western economies. For Washington, resolving the Arab-Israeli conflict shifted from a distant regional issue into an urgent strategic and economic imperative.

To defuse frontline flashpoints and prevent a superpower confrontation, US Secretary of State Henry Kissinger launched "shuttle diplomacy". Flying tirelessly between Cairo, Damascus, and Jerusalem to relay messages between hostile leaders who refused direct talks, Kissinger pioneered "step-by-step diplomacy." Rather than seeking an impossible comprehensive peace, he brokered phased military disengagements: Sinai I (January 1974), the Golan Disengagement (May 1974), and Sinai II (September 1975). These pacts returned strategic passes and oil fields to Egypt, allowing the Suez Canal to officially reopen on 5 June 1975 after an eight-year closure and providing a vital economic lifeline.

[10.2] 1977

The Likud Election & Sadat's Historic Knesset Speech

In May 1977, Israeli politics underwent a seismic shock when the right-wing Likud coalition, led by former Irgun commander Menachem Begin, won the national election, ending 29 unbroken years of Labour Party dominance. Begin held fierce ideological convictions regarding the biblical "Greater Land of Israel" (Eretz Yisrael), viewing the West Bank (Judea and Samaria) as non-negotiable Jewish land. International observers feared that Begin’s hawkish ideology had permanently halted any hope of regional peacemaking.

However, Egyptian President Anwar Sadat faced desperate internal pressures: a bankrupt national economy, crippling foreign debt, and violent food riots in Cairo. Concluding that Egypt could never develop economically while locked in perpetual mobilization, Sadat made an astonishing gamble. On 19 November 1977, Sadat flew directly to Tel Aviv—becoming the first Arab head of state to visit Israel. Addressing the Israeli parliament in his historic Knesset speech , Sadat announced: "We accept to live with you in permanent peace based on justice... No more war, no more bloodshed!" This bold act shattered thirty years of psychological taboo in a single afternoon.

The Camp David Summit & The Two Frameworks

Following Sadat’s visit, direct bilateral negotiations quickly deadlocked over Israeli settlements in Sinai and Palestinian self-rule. To rescue the peace initiative, US President Jimmy Carter invited Begin and Sadat to the secluded presidential retreat at Camp David, Maryland, in September 1978. For thirteen grueling days, Carter mediated between the two leaders, who grew so personally antagonistic that they refused to meet face-to-face for days. Carter conducted relentless single-text diplomacy, personally drafting and revising over twenty proposals between their private cabins .

On 17 September 1978, the summit concluded with the historic Camp David Accords, structured into Two Distinct Frameworks. The first, a vague "Framework for Peace in the Middle East," proposed a five-year transitional self-governing authority for Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza, but lacked precise timetables and was largely disregarded by Begin. The second, a concrete "Framework for an Egyptian-Israeli Peace Treaty," established a historic trade-off of "land for peace": Israel agreed to completely evacuate the Sinai Peninsula, dismantle all 18 Jewish settlements, and return oil fields to Egypt (as mapped in the Sinai Peninsula territory agreement), in exchange for full diplomatic recognition, demilitarised buffer zones, and guaranteed Israeli passage through the Suez Canal and Straits of Tiran.

[10.4]

The 1979 Peace Treaty & The Deadly Backlash

On 26 March 1979, Begin, Sadat, and Carter signed the formal Egypt-Israel Peace Treaty (Treaty of Washington) on the White House lawn. Backed by billions of dollars in annual US economic and military subsidies to both nations, the treaty saw Israel complete its phased withdrawal from Sinai by April 1982. Crucially, the pact achieved a massive strategic transformation: it detached the Arab world’s largest and most formidable military power from the conflict, rendering another unified Arab multi-front war against Israel virtually impossible.

However, this separate peace provoked furious outrage across the Arab world. Arab governments and the PLO denounced Sadat as a traitor who had abandoned the Palestinian struggle in exchange for Egyptian desert. The Arab League expelled Egypt, moved its headquarters from Cairo to Tunis, and severed diplomatic ties. Domestically, radical Islamist groups inside Egypt condemned Sadat’s secular compromise. On 6 October 1981, during a military parade in Cairo, radical soldiers assassinated Sadat in a hail of gunfire. Although the new Egyptian government honoured the peace treaty, Sadat’s assassination proved the extreme domestic and regional perils facing Arab leaders who compromised with Israel.

The Palestinian Issue: Lebanon, Sabra & Shatila, and the First Intifada, 1974–1993

KEY ENQUIRY QUESTION How did the trauma of the 1982 Lebanon War and the eruption of the First Intifada redefine the Palestinian struggle?
[11.1] 1974–1978

Arafat at the UN & The Lebanese Frontier

Following the 1973 Yom Kippur War, the Arab League formally recognized the PLO as the "sole legitimate representative of the Palestinian people." In November 1974, PLO Chairman Yasser Arafat made history as the first representative of a non-state movement invited to address the UN General Assembly in New York . Wearing a holster, Arafat famously concluded: "I have come bearing an olive branch and a freedom-fighter's gun. Do not let the olive branch fall from my hand." This landmark address marked the PLO’s emergence onto the global diplomatic stage, securing permanent UN observer status while maintaining armed struggle.

However, following their violent expulsion from Jordan during Black September (1970–71), armed Palestinian factions relocated to southern Lebanon. Exploiting Lebanon’s weak central government and sectarian fractures, the PLO constructed an autonomous, heavily fortified border enclave known as "Fatahland". From these southern bases, guerrillas launched rocket barrages and cross-border raids into northern Israel. On 11 March 1978, Fatah commandos landed by boat and hijacked a civilian bus on Israel's Coastal Road, killing 38 Israeli civilians. In direct retaliation, Prime Minister Menachem Begin ordered Operation Litani, deploying 25,000 Israeli troops into southern Lebanon to push the PLO north of the Litani River. Although UN Security Council Resolution 425 established the UNIFIL peacekeeping buffer, cross-border skirmishes continued.

[11.2] 1982

Operation Peace for Galilee & The Siege of Beirut

On 3 June 1982, the rogue Abu Nidal faction critically wounded Israeli Ambassador Shlomo Argov in London. Although British intelligence confirmed that Arafat's PLO was not involved, hawkish Israeli Defence Minister Ariel Sharon seized upon the assassination attempt as the pretext to launch a long-planned, total military offensive: Operation Peace for Galilee. On 6 June 1982, Israel launched an invasion with 76,000 troops. While Prime Minister Begin publicly announced a limited 40-kilometre security buffer to protect northern Galilee from artillery fire, Sharon systematically drove his armored divisions over 80 kilometres north, advancing all the way to encircle the Lebanese capital, Beirut .

For ten brutal weeks during the summer of 1982, Israeli forces placed West Beirut under siege, trapping 14,000 PLO fighters and hundreds of thousands of Lebanese and Palestinian civilians. Sharon cut off municipal water, electricity, and food supplies while unleashing ferocious air and artillery bombardments. Sharon’s secret strategic objective was total: to eradicate the PLO’s political and military infrastructure, expel Syrian forces from Lebanon, and install a pro-Israeli Christian Maronite government under Bachir Gemayel. Facing mounting civilian casualties and global moral outcry, an American-brokered multinational peacekeeping force supervised the evacuation of Yasser Arafat and over 14,000 fighters by sea in August 1982, scattering the PLO leadership into distant exile in Tunis, Tunisia, 1,500 miles away from Israel's borders.

[11.3] 1982–1987

Sabra & Shatila and the Outbreak of the First Intifada

On 14 September 1982, Lebanon’s newly elected president, Bachir Gemayel, was assassinated in a massive bomb blast. Seeking revenge, Lebanese Christian Phalangist militias demanded access to the Sabra and Shatila refugee camps in West Beirut. Israeli forces surrounded the camps, sealed all exits, and permitted roughly 150 Phalangist gunmen to enter. Over the next 36 hours, Phalangists slaughtered between 800 and 2,000 unarmed Palestinian civilians while Israeli troops fired illumination flares into the night sky. The atrocity ignited intense international condemnation and a massive protest of 400,000 Israelis in Tel Aviv. In 1983, the independent Israeli Kahan Commission ruled that while Phalangists committed the murders, Defence Minister Ariel Sharon bore "personal and indirect responsibility" for failing to anticipate the bloodshed, forcing his resignation.

With the PLO leadership stranded in distant Tunis, the primary front of Palestinian resistance shifted directly to the 1.5 million Palestinians living under military occupation in the West Bank and Gaza Strip. After twenty years of occupation, land confiscations, and identity checks, local resentment reached boiling point. On 8 December 1987, an Israeli military transport vehicle collided with civilian cars near the Jabalia refugee camp in Gaza, killing four Palestinian labourers. Convinced the crash was intentional revenge for a recent stabbing, thousands of Palestinians erupted in spontaneous protests at their funerals. When Israeli soldiers shot and killed a teenage demonstrator, the First Palestinian Intifada ("the shaking off") erupted across Gaza and spread rapidly through the West Bank .

[11.4]

The Dynamics of the Intifada & The 1988 Geneva Turn

The First Intifada was a popular grassroots civil rebellion led by local underground committees (the Unified National Leadership of the Uprising, UNLU). It mobilized entire communities through general strikes, commercial closures, tax boycotts, barricades, and teenagers hurling stones at Israeli patrols. In December 1987, the rival militant Islamist faction Hamas was founded, rejecting diplomacy and demanding the complete liberation of historic Palestine through armed struggle. In response, Defence Minister Yitzhak Rabin enforced an uncompromising "Iron Fist" policy, instructing troops to use force and "break their bones." Global television broadcasts of heavily armed soldiers beating stone-throwing youths shattered Israel’s traditional image as an underdog, transforming it in world opinion into an oppressive occupying power.

Crucially, the Intifada convinced Israeli military leaders, including Rabin, that permanent military occupation was unsustainable and that there was no purely military solution to the Palestinian question. Simultaneously, fearing that grassroots leaders inside the West Bank and Gaza were eclipsing his authority, Yasser Arafat seized the diplomatic initiative. Addressing the UN General Assembly in Geneva in December 1988, Arafat took the historic step of formally renouncing all forms of terrorism, recognizing the State of Israel’s right to exist in peace, and accepting UN Resolutions 242 and 338. This momentous concession satisfied longstanding American conditions, prompting the United States to open official diplomatic dialogue with the PLO for the first time.

Attempts at a Solution: From the Oslo Accords to Oslo II, 1988–1995

KEY ENQUIRY QUESTION Why did the Oslo Accords bring historic hope for peace, yet fail to resolve the conflict?
[12.1] 1989–1992

Soviet Collapse, Gulf War Disaster & The Road to Madrid

The collapse of the Soviet Union (1989–1991) fundamentally transformed the geopolitical landscape of the Middle East. The PLO and hardline Arab states lost their primary diplomatic patron, arms supplier, and superpower shield. Simultaneously, over 400,000 Soviet Jews immigrated to Israel between 1989 and 1992, placing massive financial strain on the Israeli state. When right-wing Prime Minister Yitzhak Shamir directed these immigrants into West Bank settlements, US President George H.W. Bush dramatically intervened by withholding $10 billion in US loan guarantees until settlement construction ceased. This unprecedented American financial pressure exposed Israel’s economic reliance on Washington and undermined Shamir’s Likud government.

Concurrently, the PLO suffered a catastrophic self-inflicted blow during the 1990–1991 Gulf War. When Iraqi dictator Saddam Hussein invaded Kuwait, PLO Chairman Yasser Arafat made the disastrous decision to publicly endorse Saddam, hoping Iraq would link its withdrawal to Palestinian statehood. Following the swift US-led coalition victory, infuriated Gulf monarchies—especially Saudi Arabia and Kuwait—severed all financial subsidies to the PLO and expelled over 300,000 Palestinian workers. Bankrupt, diplomatically isolated, and outflanked by the grassroots momentum of the First Intifada inside the occupied territories, Arafat was in a desperate position. Leveraging this historic alignment of superpower dominance, the US and USSR co-sponsored the Madrid Peace Conference in October 1991 , seating Israeli and Arab delegates together for the first time. Although formal public negotiations deadlocked, the June 1992 election of Yitzhak Rabin's Labour government on a peace platform created an opening for genuine diplomacy.

[12.2]

The Secret Oslo Channel & 1993 Handshake

Frustrated by public posturing and procedural deadlock at official talks in Washington, newly elected Israeli Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin and Foreign Minister Shimon Peres authorized a deniable, secret diplomatic backchannel. Beginning in January 1993, Norwegian social scientist Terje Rød-Larsen and Foreign Minister Johan Jørgen Holst hosted 14 clandestine rounds of direct negotiations between Israeli academics and senior PLO representatives in isolated country houses near Oslo, Norway. Free from media scrutiny and political posturing, the negotiators forged personal bonds and crafted an unprecedented bilateral compromise: mutual diplomatic recognition.

In early September 1993, Rabin and Arafat signed the historic Letters of Mutual Recognition: the PLO formally recognized Israel’s right to exist in peace and security, accepted UN Resolutions 242 and 338, and renounced all forms of terrorism; in return, Israel officially recognized the PLO as the legitimate representative of the Palestinian people. This paved the way for the signing of the Declaration of Principles (Oslo I) on 13 September 1993 on the South Lawn of the White House . Before 3,000 dignitaries and a global television audience, US President Bill Clinton gently nudged Rabin and Arafat into a historic handshake. Oslo I established a 5-year interim timetable for Palestinian self-government, beginning with Israeli withdrawal from the Gaza Strip and the West Bank town of Jericho, while deferring explosive "permanent status" issues—Jerusalem, refugees, borders, and Jewish settlements—to future talks.

Regional Peace & The Oslo II Partition

The breakthrough of Oslo I unleashed immediate regional diplomatic momentum. In May 1994, Rabin and Arafat signed the Cairo Agreement (Gaza-Jericho Agreement), initiating the withdrawal of Israeli forces and enabling Yasser Arafat to make a triumphant return from exile on 1 July 1994 to head the newly established Palestinian National Authority (PNA). Recognizing that the PLO had made peace with Israel, King Hussein of Jordan felt politically liberated to formalize relations. On 26 October 1994, Jordan and Israel signed a comprehensive Peace Treaty in the Arava Valley, settling border disputes, guaranteeing Jordanian water quotas from the Sea of Galilee, and recognizing Jordan’s historic custody over Islamic holy sites in Jerusalem. In December 1994, Rabin, Peres, and Arafat were jointly awarded the Nobel Peace Prize.

However, translating interim principles into broader territorial self-rule across the West Bank proved intensely difficult. On 28 September 1995, Rabin and Arafat signed the Interim Agreement on the West Bank and Gaza Strip (Oslo II / Taba Agreement). Oslo II partitioned the West Bank into three distinct administrative categories : Area A (18% of land, 55% of Palestinians) encompassing major urban cities under full PNA civil and internal security control; Area B (22% of land, 41% of Palestinians) covering 450 rural towns and villages under PNA civil administration but joint Israeli military security; and Area C (60% of land, 4% of Palestinians) covering all open land, strategic aquifers, Jordan Valley borders, and all 130 Jewish settlements, remaining under exclusive Israeli civil and military control. This created an archipelago of non-contiguous Palestinian enclaves surrounded by Israeli military checkpoints.

Extremist Backlash, Rabin’s Assassination & Deadlock

Territorial compromise provoked violent resistance from ideological extremists on both fringes who rejected any mutual coexistence. On 25 February 1994, radical American-Israeli settler Baruch Goldstein opened fire with an automatic rifle inside Hebron’s Ibrahimi Mosque, murdering 29 Palestinian worshippers during Ramadan. In declared retaliation, the militant Islamist group Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad launched a devastating campaign of suicide bus bombings inside Tel Aviv, Jerusalem, and Afula, killing scores of Israeli civilians and severely eroding the Israeli public’s faith in the peace process. Simultaneously, right-wing Israeli nationalist and religious factions mounted venomous campaigns against Prime Minister Rabin, depicting him at street rallies in Nazi SS uniforms and branding him a traitor for ceding biblical land ("Judea and Samaria").

On the evening of 4 November 1995, following a massive peace rally of over 100,000 citizens in Tel Aviv’s Kings of Israel Square under the banner "Yes to Peace, No to Violence" , Yitzhak Rabin was assassinated. As the Prime Minister walked toward his car having just sung "A Song for Peace," Yigal Amir, a 25-year-old right-wing Jewish law student and religious extremist, shot Rabin at point-blank range in the back. Amir declared that Jewish law sanctioned the murder of any leader who surrendered ancestral land to non-Jews. Rabin’s assassination dealt a devastating, fatal psychological blow to the peace process. Deprived of Rabin’s unmatched military credibility and stature, the peace movement foundered; in May 1996, following renewed Hamas bus bombings, Israeli voters narrowly elected right-wing Likud leader Benjamin Netanyahu, who opposed the Oslo Accords. By deferring permanent status issues and failing to curb extremist violence on either side, the Oslo framework collapsed into mutual recrimination and eventual deadlock.

[1.1] In January 1964, Arab League leaders gathered at the Cairo Summit, convened by Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser. The catalyst was Israel's National Water Carrier, a pipeline diverting water from the Sea of Galilee to the Negev desert (see National Water Carrier Map). Simultaneously, Yasser Arafat's Fatah began cross-border guerrilla raids targeting Israeli water infrastructure and railways. Following a radical military coup in February 1966, Syria’s new government openly sponsored Fatah’s "popular liberation war" against Israel, transforming regional water disputes into active frontier warfare.

[3.1] On 22 November 1967, the UN Security Council unanimously passed Resolution 242, establishing the "Land for Peace" principle. Drafted by Britain’s Lord Caradon, it asserted the "inadmissibility of the acquisition of territory by war," calling for Israeli armed forces to withdraw from occupied areas in return for Arab states recognizing Israel's right to live in peace within secure borders. [3.2] However, deliberate linguistic ambiguity crippled implementation. The English text called for withdrawal from "territories occupied" (some territories), whereas the French text specified "des territoires occupés" (all territories) (Source C). Furthermore, Resolution 242 addressed Palestinians only as a generic "refugee problem," entirely omitting their national right to self-determination.
[4.1] In the long term, the 1967 conquests replaced the quest for peace with a diplomatic stalemate. Israel fortified its borders—building the Bar-Lev Line along the Suez Canal—and subsidized Jewish settlements, while Arab leaders maintained the Khartoum "Three Noes." [4.2] Consequently, Palestinians concluded that conventional Arab armies would never liberate their homeland, accelerating the growth of independent guerrilla groups like the PLO. Without diplomatic progress, Resolution 242 could not avert the impending War of Attrition and the 1973 Yom Kippur War.

THE STRUGGLE FOR SOVEREIGNTY • 1517 TO 1689

Who Held True Power: Pope, Crown, or Parliament?

HISTORICAL NARRATIVE SPHERE
Over 172 years, the answer to "Who rules Britain?" changed violently three times. Trace the shift across four eras:
Phase 1: Medieval Catholic Unity (Before 1534) SOVEREIGN: THE POPE

The King of England is an obedient Catholic prince. The Pope in Rome holds supreme authority over religious law, taxes (Peter’s Pence), and marriages. Disobeying the Church means damnation or death as a heretic.

Phase 2: Tudor Royal Supremacy (1534–1603) SOVEREIGN: THE MONARCH

Henry VIII fires the Pope and crowns himself Supreme Head of the Church. The monarch controls both Crown and Altar. Elizabeth I preserves this through the 1559 "Middle Way", defeating Catholic plots and the 1588 Spanish Armada.

Phase 3: Civil War, Regicide & Republic (1625–1660) SOVEREIGN: THE ARMY & CROMWELL

Charles I claims Divine Right; Parliament fights back. In 1649, Parliament beheads the King and creates a Republic. Oliver Cromwell rules as Lord Protector, but strict Puritan laws make military dictatorship deeply unpopular.

Phase 4: Parliamentary Constitutional Monarchy (1689 Onward) SOVEREIGN: PARLIAMENT UNDER LAW

The 1688 Glorious Revolution replaces James II with William and Mary. In 1689, the Bill of Rights forces the Crown to obey Parliament: the King reigns, but Parliament rules. Modern constitutional Britain is born.

Enquiry Focus Question: "Between 1517 and 1689, which was more dangerous to a King of England: defying the Pope, or defying Parliament?" Discuss with your partner.

ENQUIRY LESSON 1 • CORE KNOWLEDGE & NARRATIVE SPINE

Why did a German monk and an English King tear the Catholic Church apart?

LESSON 1
CHRONOLOGY
1517 Luther’s Hammer
1527 Royal Desperation
1534 Firing the Pope
1536 Monastery Gold
Act I: The Spark at Wittenberg (1517) ACT 1
[1.1] In October 1517, a furious German monk named Martin Luther stormed up to the castle church in Wittenberg and nailed 95 fiery arguments to the oak door. Luther was disgusted by corrupt Catholic friars selling "indulgences"—printed certificates promising wealthy sinners less time suffering in Purgatory for cash. Thanks to the newly invented printing press, Luther’s ideas swept Europe like wildfire. He declared that ordinary people did not need priests or Latin rituals to reach God: faith and the Bible in their own language were enough. A great religious rebellion had begun: the Protestant Reformation.
✎ Note Prompt: Why did Martin Luther’s 95 Theses threaten the power and wealth of the Catholic Church?
Act II: The King’s "Great Matter" (1527) ACT 2
[1.2] Across the English Channel, King Henry VIII initially despised Luther, writing a book defending the Pope that earned him the title Defender of the Faith. But by 1527, Henry faced a dynastic nightmare. His Spanish wife, Catherine of Aragon, was past forty and had produced only one living child: a girl, Princess Mary. Convinced England would collapse into bloody civil war without a male heir, Henry fell desperately in love with the clever, ambitious Anne Boleyn. He begged Pope Clement VII to annul his marriage, claiming it was cursed by God.
✎ Note Prompt: Why was Henry VIII so desperate to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon?
Act III: Firing the Pope: The Act of Supremacy (1534) ACT 3
[1.3] The Pope was trapped. Rome was occupied by Catherine’s powerful nephew, the Holy Roman Emperor, so the Pope stalled for seven agonising years. Henry’s patience snapped. Guided by his ruthless advisor Thomas Cromwell, Henry decided that if the Pope would not give him a divorce, he would simply fire the Pope! In 1534, Henry forced Parliament to pass the historic Act of Supremacy. It declared King Henry VIII the sole "Supreme Head" of the Church of England. Refusing to swear an oath accepting Henry’s royal supremacy was high treason; his former friend Sir Thomas More was promptly beheaded.
✎ Note Prompt: How did the 1534 Act of Supremacy fundamentally change who controlled England?
Act IV: Stripping the Monasteries (1536–1540) ACT 4
[1.4] Royal power was sweet, but monastery gold was sweeter. Between 1536 and 1540, Henry and Cromwell sent commissioners to inspect over 800 Catholic monasteries and abbeys across England. Accusing the monks of laziness and corruption, Henry closed them down, melted lead from their roofs, smashed precious shrines, and seized their vast estates. Henry sold the land to loyal nobles and spent the fortune on warships and French wars. England was no longer Catholic: religion now belonged completely to the Crown.
✎ Note Prompt: What did Henry VIII gain by closing down over 800 monasteries across England?
★ Did You Know? (Human Quirks of History): Young Henry VIII was an athletic, vain prince who wrote love letters and bad poetry to Anne Boleyn. Terrified of bad smells and the sweating sickness, he carried a hollow pomander ball filled with perfumed spices everywhere, and wore custom-padded shoes wider than they were long to look colossal!

ENQUIRY LESSON 2 • CORE KNOWLEDGE & NARRATIVE SPINE

Could a young queen heal a divided kingdom without losing her throne?

LESSON 2
CHRONOLOGY
1547 Smashed Statues (Edward)
1553 Smithfield Fires (Mary)
1559 The "Middle Way"
1570 Papal Death Mark
Act I: The Tudor Rollercoaster (1547–1558) ACT 1
[2.1] When Henry VIII died in 1547, England plunged into religious whiplash. First, his sickly nine-year-old son, Edward VI, instituted radical Protestantism: whitewashing colourful church walls, smashing stained glass, and forcing everyone to use an English prayer book. But Edward died at fifteen, and his Catholic half-sister Mary I seized the throne. Furious at her father’s rebellion, "Bloody Mary" dragged England back to the Pope, burning nearly 300 Protestant "heretics" alive at the stake at Smithfield. When Mary died in 1558, England was traumatised, paranoid, and bitterly divided.
✎ Note Prompt: Why was the period between 1547 and 1558 described as a "Tudor Rollercoaster"?
Act II: A Young Queen Inherits a Crisis (1558) ACT 2
[2.2] Into this hornets’ nest stepped twenty-five-year-old Elizabeth Tudor. Her situation seemed impossible. In sixteenth-century Europe, people believed women were too weak and emotional to rule alone. England was deeply in debt, at war with France, and Catholic kings in Spain and France viewed Elizabeth as an illegitimate bastard who had no right to wear the crown. English MPs badgered her constantly to marry a strong man to rule for her, but Elizabeth shrewdly refused to give away her power.
✎ Note Prompt: What three major problems threatened Elizabeth I when she became queen in 1558?
Act III: The "Middle Way" Religious Settlement (1559) ACT 3
[2.3] Elizabeth knew that if she chose extreme Protestantism, English Catholics would rebel; if she chose Catholicism, Protestants would riot. Her genius answer was the 1559 Religious Settlement—often called the "Middle Way". The Church of England was officially Protestant: services and Bibles were in English, and Elizabeth was Supreme Governor. But she allowed crucifixes, stained glass, and fancy priestly robes to remain. Elizabeth famously declared that she had "no desire to make windows into men’s souls": as long as people attended church quietly on Sunday, she did not care what they privately believed.
✎ Note Prompt: How was Elizabeth’s 1559 "Middle Way" designed to keep peace between Protestants and Catholics?
Act IV: Assassination Plots & The Papal Death Mark (1570–1587) ACT 4
[2.4] Compromise did not satisfy religious extremists. Strict Protestants (Puritans) demanded all "popish" decorations be destroyed. Worse, in 1570, Pope Pius V issued a papal bull excommunicating Elizabeth, declaring her a heretic and telling English Catholics it was their holy duty to depose her! Catholic plotters launched conspiracy after conspiracy (Ridolfi, Throckmorton, Babington) to assassinate Elizabeth and place her Catholic cousin, Mary Queen of Scots, on the throne. Elizabeth’s spy master Sir Francis Walsingham cracked their secret coded letters. In February 1587, Elizabeth signed Mary’s death warrant; Mary was beheaded at Fotheringhay Castle.
✎ Note Prompt: Why did the Pope’s 1570 excommunication turn Mary Queen of Scots into a deadly threat to Elizabeth?
★ Did You Know? (Human Quirks of History): Queen Elizabeth I loved candied violets and sugar comfits so passionately that her teeth turned completely black from rot. Foreign ambassadors noted that they could barely understand her speech, yet wealthy Tudor courtiers blackened their own teeth with charcoal just to copy the Queen’s aristocratic look!

ENQUIRY LESSON 3 • CORE KNOWLEDGE & NARRATIVE SPINE

Why did Philip II launch the Spanish Armada, and why did it fail?

LESSON 3
CHRONOLOGY
1585 Sea Dog Piracy
1587 Cadiz Raid
1588 Midnight Fireships
1588 The Wrecking Gale
Act I: The Cold War Turns Hot (1585–1587) ACT 1
[3.1] By the 1580s, Catholic Spain was the undisputed superpower of the world, flooded with silver plundered from South America. But King Philip II had three bitter grudges against England: Elizabeth had rejected his marriage proposal, she was secretly financing Dutch Protestant rebels fighting against Spanish rule, and her pirate "sea dogs" (like Sir Francis Drake) kept robbing Spanish treasure galleons. When Elizabeth beheaded Catholic Mary Queen of Scots in 1587, Philip decided God had commanded him to conquer England and overthrow the heretic Queen.
✎ Note Prompt: What three major reasons provoked King Philip II to launch an invasion of England?
Act II: The "Invincible" Crescent (July 1588) ACT 2
[3.2] In May 1588, Philip launched his giant fleet: the Spanish Armada. It consisted of 130 massive, towering warships carrying 30,000 soldiers and sailors, accompanied by 180 priests to convert England back to Rome. Philip’s plan was to sail up the English Channel in a tight, impenetrable crescent formation, rendezvous in the Netherlands with the Duke of Parma’s crack army of 30,000 veteran infantry, and ferry them across the Thames Estuary to seize London. For nine days, English ships followed the Armada up the Channel, but the Spanish crescent could not be broken.
✎ Note Prompt: What was Philip’s battle plan to conquer London using Parma’s army?
Act III: Midnight Hell-Burners at Calais (7 August 1588) ACT 3
[3.3] On 7 August, the Spanish anchored off Calais, waiting for Parma’s army. But Parma was trapped by Dutch gunboats and was not ready. At midnight, English commanders Lord Howard and Sir Francis Drake enacted a terrifying plan. They took eight old merchant ships, smeared their wooden hulls with pitch, tar, and gunpowder, loaded their cannons to fire automatically when the heat reached them, set them ablaze, and sent them drifting with the tide straight into the packed Spanish fleet! Panic erupted. Believing these were "hell-burners" designed to blow them to pieces, Spanish captains severed their anchor ropes and scattered blindly into the stormy darkness.
✎ Note Prompt: Why did the midnight fireships at Calais cause total chaos among the Spanish fleet?
Act IV: The Battle of Gravelines & The Wrecking Gale (8 August 1588) ACT 4
[3.4] At dawn off Gravelines, the English struck. With their crescent broken and anchors lost, the Spanish were easy prey. Smaller, faster English galleons equipped with long-range culverin cannons darted circles around the clumsy Spanish giants, pounding their oak hulls from safe distances. Battered and running low on ammunition, the surviving Armada could not sail back down the Channel against the wind. They were forced to flee north around the rocky coasts of Scotland and Ireland. Savage Atlantic storms ("The Protestant Wind") smashed dozens of galleons onto the rocks. Less than half the Armada limped back to Spain. Philip wept: "I sent my ships to fight the English, not the elements."
✎ Note Prompt: How did superior English naval technology and bad weather combine to destroy the Armada?
★ Did You Know? (Human Quirks of History): King Philip II of Spain was such an obsessive micromanager that he spent 14 hours a day in a tiny stone cell in his palace, personally writing letters about how much salt pork and vinegar the sailors should eat! Meanwhile in England, legend says Sir Francis Drake was playing a game of bowls when the Armada was spotted, calmly announcing: "There is plenty of time to win this game and thrash the Spaniards too!"

ENQUIRY LESSON 4 • CORE KNOWLEDGE & NARRATIVE SPINE

From Divine Right to cutting off the King’s head: Why did Britain execute Charles I?

LESSON 4
CHRONOLOGY
1629 11 Years’ Tyranny
1642 King Invades Parliament
1649 Beheading a King
1689 The Bill of Rights
Milestone 1: The Tyrant King? (1629–1640) ACT 1
[4.1] When the Stuarts took the throne, conflict exploded. King Charles I firmly believed in the Divine Right of Kings: the idea that he was chosen by God Almighty and that questioning his decisions was a sin. When MPs in Parliament refused to grant him taxes, Charles locked the doors of Westminster and ruled alone for 11 years (the "Personal Rule"). To raise cash, he revived ancient medieval fines, forcing inland towns to pay Ship Money (traditionally only paid by coastal ports in wartime). When Puritans protested, Charles had their ears sliced off in public pillories. Resentment boiled.
✎ Note Prompt: Why did Charles I believe he could rule without Parliament for 11 years?
Milestone 2: The Nation at War (1642–1648) ACT 2
[4.2] In January 1642, Charles committed the ultimate outrage: he marched 400 armed guards straight onto the floor of the House of Commons to arrest five rebel MPs for treason. The Speaker refused to help him, and the MPs escaped down the River Thames. War was unavoidable. The nation tore itself apart: aristocratic Royalists with long hair and swords (Cavaliers) supported the King, while Puritan merchants and townsfolk with short hair (Roundheads) fought for Parliament. Parliament won because a Puritan MP named Oliver Cromwell created the New Model Army: a disciplined, professional fighting force promoted on merit, not noble birth.
✎ Note Prompt: Why did the creation of the New Model Army allow Parliament to defeat the King?
Milestone 3: The Unthinkable Crime: Regicide (January 1649) ACT 3
[4.3] Even after Charles was captured, he secretly plotted with Scottish armies to restart the war. Cromwell and radical army officers decided there would never be peace while Charles lived. In January 1649, an event occurred that shocked the crowned heads of Europe: Parliament put King Charles I on public trial for treason against his own people! Charles refused to plead, sneering that no earthly court had the legal right to judge an anointed King. On 30 January 1649, Charles was walked out of a window of his own Whitehall Banqueting House and beheaded on a black scaffold before a gasping crowd. Britain was now a Kingless Republic.
✎ Note Prompt: Why was putting King Charles I on trial for treason considered an unthinkable act in 1649?
Milestone 4: The Crown Tamed: The 1689 Bill of Rights (1660–1689) ACT 4
[4.4] For eleven years, Britain was ruled by Cromwell and the Puritans, who banned Christmas, horse-racing, makeup, and theatres. Exhausted by military dictatorship, Britain welcomed back Charles’s son (Charles II) in the 1660 Restoration. But when his brother, James II, tried to restore absolute Catholic rule in 1688, Parliament acted decisively. In the Glorious Revolution, MPs invited the Dutch Protestant Prince William of Orange and his English wife Mary to take the throne. In 1689, William and Mary were forced to sign the Bill of Rights. Never again could an English monarch suspend laws, dismiss judges, levy taxes, or maintain a standing army without Parliament’s consent. Parliament had won permanent power.
✎ Note Prompt: How did the 1689 Bill of Rights permanently end the Divine Right of Kings in Britain?
★ Did You Know? (Human Quirks of History): Oliver Cromwell was so strictly Puritan that his soldiers patrolled London streets on 25 December sniffing chimney smoke—if they smelled roast turkey or mince pies, they kicked the door down and confiscated Christmas dinner! Furthermore, Charles I wore two thick shirts to his execution on a freezing January morning so that he wouldn’t shiver, saying: "I fear not death! If I tremble with the cold, my enemies will say I tremble with fear."

ENQUIRY LESSON 5 • CORE KNOWLEDGE & NARRATIVE SPINE

“The World Turned Upside Down”? How far did life and power in Britain really change between 1517 and 1689?

LESSON 5
CHRONOLOGY
1517 Papal Rome & Kings
1649 Regicide & Diggers
1689 Bill of Rights
Continuity The 90% at the Bottom
Act I: Power & Government: Taming the Crown ACT 1
[5.1] In 1517, Henry VIII ruled as an all-powerful medieval prince. He claimed to be God’s deputy on Earth, locked opponents in the Tower, and beheaded ministers without challenge. By 1689, royal absolutism was dead. The 1689 Bill of Rights established that Parliament, not the monarch, held supreme authority over taxes, courts, and armies. A King could no longer dismiss Parliament on a whim. Power at the top had fundamentally shifted from one man’s royal will to an elected assembly of lawmakers.
✎ Note Prompt: In what ways did the 1689 Bill of Rights represent a revolutionary shift in political power?
Act II: Faith & Religion: From Rome to Toleration ACT 2
[5.2] In 1517, every single English person was Catholic, answering to the Pope in Rome; questioning Church dogma meant burning as a heretic. By 1689, Britain was officially a Protestant kingdom anchored by the national Church of England. Furthermore, following the religious violence of the Civil War, the 1689 Toleration Act allowed Protestant dissenters (Baptists, Quakers, Presbyterians) to worship in their own chapels without arrest. While Catholics and Jews still faced discrimination, the medieval Catholic monopoly was shattered forever.
✎ Note Prompt: How had religious freedom and tolerance expanded in Britain between 1517 and 1689?
Act III: Ideas & Free Speech: The Pamphlet Revolution ACT 3
[5.3] Under the Tudors, speaking against the monarch resulted in sliced ears, hanging, or public branding. But during the 1640s Civil War, strict royal censorship collapsed. An explosion of radical ideas flooded London’s streets: over 22,000 pamphlets and newspapers were printed! Radical groups emerged: the Levellers demanded the right to vote for all adult men, while the Diggers argued all private property was theft. London coffee houses became noisy public centres where ordinary merchants argued politics over hot bowls of bitter Turkish brew.
✎ Note Prompt: How did the collapse of royal censorship change the way ordinary people debated ideas?
Act IV: Everyday Life: The Great Continuity ACT 4
[5.4] Yet, did "the world turn upside down" for the vast majority? For 90% of the population—poor agricultural labourers, weavers, domestic servants, and women—life remained virtually unchanged. Parliament did not represent ordinary people: only wealthy landowners owning significant property could vote or sit as MPs. Women possessed zero legal rights, could not vote or own land independently, and lived under the complete authority of husbands or fathers. Disease, child mortality, and bone-crushing agricultural toil remained the harsh daily reality from 1517 to 1689.

Extent of Change: EXPLOSION OF IDEAS

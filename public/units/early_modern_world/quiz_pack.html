Chronology Domino Flowchart

Three Centuries of Global Transformation, Revolution & Empire (1450 – 1750)

Timeline Chain
Causal Chain: Trace how religious conflict, constitutional revolution, and commercial capitalism transformed Britain from an isolated island into a global power.
1453
Fall of Constantinople: Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II captures the Byzantine capital, severing overland Silk Road trade.
1492
Voyage to the Americas: Columbus lands in the Caribbean, initiating transatlantic contact and the Columbian Exchange.
1517
The Protestant Reformation: Martin Luther posts his 95 Theses; Western European Christendom fractures into Catholic and Protestant states.
1588
The Spanish Armada: Philip II’s Catholic invasion fleet is defeated by fireships at Gravelines and wrecked by Atlantic storms.
1600
East India Company Chartered: Elizabeth I grants a royal charter to London merchants, pioneering joint-stock corporate trade.
1605
The Gunpowder Plot: Robert Catesby and Guy Fawkes fail to blow up Parliament; severe anti-Catholic penal laws enacted.
1642
English Civil War Outbreak: Clash between Charles I’s Divine Right and Parliamentary sovereignty triggers nationwide war.
1649
Execution of Charles I: Monarchy abolished; Britain declared a Republic (Commonwealth) under Oliver Cromwell.
1660
The Stuart Restoration: Charles II recalled from exile, restoring the monarchy, House of Lords, and Church of England.
1660
Royal Society Founded: King Charles II grants a royal charter to the Royal Society, institutionalizing empirical scientific inquiry.
1672
Royal African Company Chartered: Monarchy charters monopoly over the transatlantic slave trade, trafficking thousands to sugar colonies.
1687
Newton’s Principia Mathematica: Isaac Newton formulates universal laws of motion and gravity, crystallizing the Scientific Revolution.
1688
The Glorious Revolution: Catholic James II ousted; William and Mary sign the 1689 Bill of Rights, establishing constitutional monarchy.
1694
Bank of England Founded: National debt and paper money created, financing modern British fiscal-military power.
1707
Act of Union: England and Scotland unite into the Kingdom of Great Britain, opening global imperial markets.

Lesson 1: Global Power in 1450
1.
The Ottoman Empire under Sultan Mehmed II — Using massive bronze siege cannons, the Ottomans breached the Byzantine walls, ending over 1,000 years of the Eastern Roman Empire.
[✓][✗]
2.
The Ming Dynasty — China dominated global porcelain, silk, and agricultural wealth, having constructed the Forbidden City and vast fleets under Admiral Zheng He.
[✓][✗]
3.
The Songhai Empire (and Mali) — Timbuktu was a world-famous intellectual and trading capital where salt from northern deserts was traded for gold from southern forests.
[✓][✗]
4.
The Silk Road — The Ottoman conquest placed overland Asian trade under heavy taxes and Muslim control, forcing European monarchs to seek maritime alternatives.
[✓][✗]
5.
Europeans desperately sought an ocean sea route to India and the Spice Islands — Portuguese and Spanish navigators sailed south around Africa and west across the Atlantic to secure direct sea access to valuable spices.
[✓][✗]
6.
The Aztec Empire — With advanced chinampa agriculture, causeways, and stone temples, the Aztecs dominated central Mexico until the Spanish conquest of 1521.
[✓][✗]
7.
No, Europe was fractured into competing feudal kingdoms and principalities — Constant warfare between rival monarchs in England, France, and the Holy Roman Empire drove intense military innovation and competition.
[✓][✗]
8.
The magnetic mariner’s compass — Originally invented in China, the magnetic compass allowed European caravels to navigate deep open oceans away from coastal landmarks.
[✓][✗]
Lesson 2: Reformation, Rivalry & Armada
9.
Martin Luther — Luther attacked Catholic corruption and the sale of papal indulgences, sparking religious division that fractured Christian Europe.
[✓][✗]
10.
King Henry VIII — Henry passed the Act of Supremacy declaring himself Supreme Head of the Church to divorce Catherine of Aragon and seize monastery wealth.
[✓][✗]
11.
The Spanish Armada — Comprising 130 warships and 30,000 soldiers, the Armada aimed to transport Spanish invasion veterans from the Netherlands to London.
[✓][✗]
12.
Silver and gold bullion from mines like Potosí — Enslaved indigenous and African workers mined millions of silver pesos, funding Spanish imperial armies and mercenary warfare across Europe.
[✓][✗]
13.
Eight flaming fireships (hellburners) sent into Calais harbour — Panic-stricken Spanish captains cut their anchor cables in the dark, scattering into the North Sea where English guns attacked them.
[✓][✗]
14.
Sir Francis Drake — Drake’s voyage on the Golden Hind brought back vast Spanish treasure, earning him a royal knighthood from Queen Elizabeth on his ship.
[✓][✗]
15.
Both sides competed fiercely to convert indigenous souls and seize strategic wealth — English Protestants like Raleigh sought to build an anti-Catholic maritime empire to check Spanish Catholic power in the New World.
[✓][✗]
16.
The ‘Protestant Wind’ (severe Atlantic gales and storms) — Armada ships trying to escape north ran aground on rocky Scottish and Irish cliffs; fewer than half the ships limped back to Spain.
[✓][✗]
Lesson 3: Trade to Empire & The EIC
17.
The East India Company (EIC) — Queen Elizabeth granted a commercial monopoly to London merchants to sail into the Indian Ocean and break the Dutch spice monopoly.
[✓][✗]
18.
The joint-stock company — Merchants pooled capital and spread risks; if a ship sank, investors lost only their subscribed shares rather than their entire fortune.
[✓][✗]
19.
The Mughal Empire — Under emperors like Akbar and Shah Jahan, the Mughal Empire commanded 25% of world manufacturing and built architectural wonders like the Taj Mahal.
[✓][✗]
20.
Factories (Factory settlements) — EIC ‘factors’ (commercial agents) stored textiles and spices inside fortified compounds like Fort St George (Madras) and Fort William (Calcutta).
[✓][✗]
21.
Cotton calico, silk, saltpetre, and spices — Light Indian calico and chintz cottons revolutionized European fashion, while saltpetre was critical for British military gunpowder manufacturing.
[✓][✗]
22.
Early merchants acted as humble supplicants paying tribute and seeking permits — Ambassadors like Sir Thomas Roe bowed before the Mughal Emperor Jahangir, seeking trade privileges rather than attempting military rule.
[✓][✗]
23.
Sepoy regiments commanded by British officers — The EIC recruited Indian soldiers (sepoys) and drilled them in modern European flintlock musket tactics, forming a massive corporate private army.
[✓][✗]
24.
The EIC exploited regional rivalries and collected local land taxation (Diwani) — Following the 1757 Battle of Plassey, the EIC seized the tax revenues of Bengal, transforming from a trading firm into a colonial governing power.
[✓][✗]

Lesson 4: James I & The Gunpowder Plot
25.
1603 — The union of the English and Scottish crowns brought the Stuart dynasty to London, uniting two kingdoms under one Protestant monarch.
[✓][✗]
26.
The Gunpowder Plot — Catholic conspirators aimed to assassinate King James, his ministers, and Parliament to install a Catholic puppet monarch on the throne.
[✓][✗]
27.
Robert Catesby — Catesby was a charismatic Warwickshire Catholic gentleman whose father had been persecuted and fined under Elizabethan anti-Catholic laws.
[✓][✗]
28.
Guy Fawkes (John Johnson) — Fawkes had fought as a mercenary for Catholic Spain; he was arrested in the cellar with a lantern, slow matches, and pocket watch.
[✓][✗]
29.
The Monteagle Letter — The letter warned Monteagle to skip the opening of Parliament because “they shall receive a terrible blow”; Monteagle showed it to the King’s ministers.
[✓][✗]
30.
The Popish Recusancy Acts (heavier fines, oaths of allegiance, and loss of rights) — Catholics were barred from practicing law, serving as army officers, voting, or living within ten miles of London unless taking anti-papal oaths.
[✓][✗]
31.
The Thanksgiving Act (Bonfire Night / 5th of November) — The state ordered church bells rung and bonfires lit every 5 November, reinforcing Protestant national identity through anti-Catholic effigies.
[✓][✗]
32.
Protestants feared Catholics owed ultimate allegiance to the Pope in Rome — Because the Pope claimed spiritual authority to excommunicate kings and release subjects from their vows, Catholics were viewed as potential traitors.
[✓][✗]
Lesson 5: The Ideological Battle: Civil War
33.
The Divine Right of Kings — Charles believed monarchs were accountable only to God, meaning any resistance or criticism from Parliament was sinful rebellion.
[✓][✗]
34.
The Eleven Years’ Tyranny (Personal Rule) — Angry at parliamentary opposition, Charles dismissed Parliament and financed his government through ancient feudal taxes without consent.
[✓][✗]
35.
Ship Money — Traditionally levied on coastal ports during war, Charles extended Ship Money to inland counties like Buckinghamshire, provoking outrage.
[✓][✗]
36.
1642 — Charles raised his royal standard at Nottingham in August 1642 after failing to arrest five MPs in the House of Commons by force.
[✓][✗]
37.
Cavaliers (Royalists) — Supporters of the King were mocked as aristocratic, long-haired Cavaliers, while parliamentary supporters were labelled Roundheads.
[✓][✗]
38.
The New Model Army — Promoting officers on military merit rather than noble birth, this religious, disciplined force crushed the King at the Battle of Naseby.
[✓][✗]
39.
The public execution (beheading) of King Charles I — Charles was tried by a special High Court for treason against his own people; his public execution abolished the monarchy.
[✓][✗]
40.
A Republic (The Commonwealth / Protectorate) — Britain was governed without a king, ruled first by Parliament and then by Oliver Cromwell as military Lord Protector.
[✓][✗]
Lesson 6: The Economic Shift: 1660–1750
41.
The Restoration of the Monarchy — Following Cromwell’s death and the collapse of the military protectorate, Parliament invited Charles I’s son to return from exile.
[✓][✗]
42.
The Glorious Revolution — Seven English nobles invited Dutch Prince William of Orange to invade England to preserve Protestantism and parliamentary liberties.
[✓][✗]
43.
The English Bill of Rights — The Bill banned monarchs from suspending laws, levying taxes without Parliament, or keeping a peacetime standing army without consent.
[✓][✗]
44.
The Bank of England — Created to raise money for William III’s wars against France, the Bank pioneered the National Debt and national paper currency.
[✓][✗]
45.
Mercantilism — European powers believed global wealth was fixed; colonies were forced to supply raw materials and buy manufactured goods only from the mother country.
[✓][✗]
46.
The Navigation Acts — These protectionist laws excluded Dutch and foreign merchants from British imperial trade, guaranteeing immense naval and commercial dominance.
[✓][✗]
47.
The Act of Union — Scotland dissolved its Edinburgh parliament in exchange for Scottish access to England’s lucrative global colonial trading empire.
[✓][✗]
48.
Private property and investments were legally secure from royal confiscation — Because Parliament controlled taxes and debts, investors trusted British financial institutions, creating low interest rates and booming trade.
[✓][✗]

Marking Key 49–72
Lesson 7: The Transatlantic Slave Trade
49.
The Triangular Trade — British goods sailed to Africa, enslaved people were shipped to American plantations, and slave-grown sugar and tobacco were shipped to Britain.
[✓][✗]
50.
Guns, gunpowder, brass pans, cloth, and iron bars — British manufacturing centres like Birmingham (guns) and Manchester (textiles) boomed by producing goods tailored for African coastal slave traders.
[✓][✗]
51.
The Middle Passage — Enslaved people were shackled in darkness in the suffocating, diseased holds of slave ships for weeks of terror and death.
[✓][✗]
52.
6 to 12 weeks depending on weather and ocean currents — During the voyage, dysentery (the ‘bloody flux’), smallpox, and dehydration killed an estimated 15% to 20% of all captive Africans.
[✓][✗]
53.
The Brookes of Liverpool diagram — The diagram showed 454 human bodies packed into wooden shelves like cargo, proving the industrial dehumanisation of the trade.
[✓][✗]
54.
Tight-packing (Spoon-fashion) — Captains believed packing more human beings into the hold maximized profits, even though it caused horrific suffocation and disease.
[✓][✗]
55.
Sugar cane — Sugar was Britain’s most valuable import; boiling raw cane into sugar crystals was brutal, deadly forced industrial labour in extreme heat.
[✓][✗]
56.
Over 12 million men, women, and children — Britain alone transported over 3.1 million enslaved Africans, making cities like Bristol and Liverpool the slave-trading capitals of the Atlantic.
[✓][✗]
Lesson 8: Resistance to the Slave Trade
57.
Shipboard revolts and jumping overboard (suicide) — Historians record over 500 violent shipboard insurrections; many Africans chose death in the ocean, believing their spirits would return home.
[✓][✗]
58.
The Maroons — Maroons established independent, fortified mountain towns, fighting British troops to a standstill and signing self-governing peace treaties.

People & Terms
Eight Crucial Protagonists of the Early Modern Era
Sultan Mehmed II • Ottoman Sultan (1432–1481)
Conquered Constantinople in 1453 using modern gunpowder artillery, transforming the Ottoman state into an imperial Eurasian superpower.
Queen Elizabeth I • Queen of England (1558–1603)
Defeated the Spanish Armada (1588), chartered the East India Company (1600), and secured England as a sovereign Protestant maritime power.
King Charles I • King of England, Scotland & Ireland (1625–1649)
Championed the Divine Right of Kings and ruled without Parliament for 11 years; provoked the Civil War and was executed for treason in 1649.
Oliver Cromwell • Lord Protector of the Commonwealth (1599–1658)
Forged the New Model Army, defeated Royalist forces, signed the King’s death warrant, and governed Britain as a puritan military republic.
Queen Nanny of the Maroons • Leader of the Jamaican Maroons (c. 1686–1755)
Escaped enslaved African strategist who led guerrilla warfare against the British army, securing a 1739 treaty granting Maroon land freedom.
Olaudah Equiano • African Writer & Abolitionist (c. 1745–1797)
Survived the Middle Passage and slavery; published his famous 1789 autobiography, mobilizing British public opinion against the trade.
Sir Isaac Newton • Physicist & Mathematician (1642–1727)
Formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation in Principia (1687), anchoring the Enlightenment’s belief in empirical scientific reason.
Sir Robert Walpole • First British Prime Minister (1676–1745)
Consolidated parliamentary government under the Hanoverian monarchs, managing the National Debt and stabilizing British fiscal empire.
Academic Vocabulary with Spoken Pronunciation Guide
Divine Right of Kings [Dih-vyne Ryte]
The doctrine that monarchs receive their authority directly from God and are unaccountable to parliaments or earthly laws.
Joint-Stock Company [Joynt-Stok]
A business owned by multiple shareholders who pool capital and share profits, pioneering global corporate expansion.
Mercantilism [Mur-kan-tih-liz-um]
The economic system of maximizing bullion reserves through protectionist tariffs, colonial monopolies, and the Navigation Acts.
The Middle Passage [Mid-ul Pas-ij]
The second leg of the Triangular Trade; the forced, lethal transatlantic shipment of enslaved African people in cargo holds.
Constitutional Monarchy [Kon-stih-too-shun-ul]
A system where the monarch’s power is strictly limited by written law, the Bill of Rights (1689), and an elected parliament.
Enclosure Acts [En-kloh-zhur]
Parliamentary acts fencing off communal peasant lands into private farms, increasing crop yields while displacing rural laborers.
Manumission [Man-yoo-mish-un]
The formal legal release or purchase of an enslaved person from bondage, as achieved by Olaudah Equiano in 1766.
Sovereignty [Sov-rin-tee]
Supreme and independent power or authority in government, fiercely contested between the Crown and Parliament.

Essay Guide
Your Essay Question:
“To what extent was Britain transformed into a ‘modern’ nation by 1750?”
Historical Pillar Historical Evidence & Specific Mechanism Evaluation: Modern vs Traditional
Political Power & Monarchy Civil War execution of Charles I (1649); 1689 Bill of Rights established parliamentary supremacy and constitutional monarchy. Modern: Rule of law and Prime Minister replaced royal divine right.
Global Trade & Capital Joint-stock finance, Bank of England (1694), East India Company factory posts, and tea/sugar consumer revolution. Modern: Pioneered global corporate capitalism and state credit.
Empire & Enslavement Transatlantic Slave Trade transported 3.1m captive Africans; Caribbean sugar plantations enriched Bristol and Liverpool. Inhuman: National wealth was directly built on forced racial exploitation.
Social & Democratic Rights Fewer than 5% of adult men possessed the vote in 1750; women were disenfranchised; enclosure displaced tenant farmers. Traditional: Aristocratic elites tightly controlled political power and land.
The Historians' Debate: Was 1750 Britain Truly ‘Modern’?
Interpretation A (The "March of Progress" View): Britain was taking giant leaps forward—pioneering constitutional rights, science, global commerce, and the rule of law.
Interpretation B (The "Human Cost & Exploitation" View): Britain’s rise was financed by the human horrors of the Transatlantic Slave Trade and concentrated wealth among an unrepresentative ruling aristocracy.

Chronology Domino Flowchart

Two Millennia of Public Health, Epidemics & Engineering (100 AD – 1875)

Timeline Chain
Causal Chain: Trace how scientific ignorance and laissez-faire government delayed clean water until engineering and epidemiology forced national reform.
c. 100 AD
Roman Conduits & Sewers: Roman engineers build stone conduits, hypocaust bathhouses, and Housesteads fort communal latrines.
122 AD
Hadrian’s Wall Drainage: Housesteads Roman Fort engineered with multi-seat latrines flushed continuously by collected rainwater.
410 AD
Roman Collapse: Legions withdraw from Britain; aqueducts fall into disuse as Anglo-Saxon timber farming replaces Roman municipal towns.
c. 1165
Canterbury Waterworks: Monks at Canterbury Priory engineer lead piping networks to bring clean spring water and flush latrines.
1348
The Black Death: Plague kills 30–50% of England; doctors blame poisonous miasma and balance of humors rather than fleas.
1388
First Sanitation Act: Parliament passes an act forbidding the dumping of dung, animal entrails, and slaughterhouse waste into rivers.
c. 1450
Medieval Gongfermers: Town councils appoint night-soil workers to shovel overflowing cesspits exclusively after dark.
1596
First Water Closet: Sir John Harington invents the first flushing toilet for Elizabeth I; fails to catch on due to lack of pipes.
1665
The Great Plague of London: Over 68,000 die; red crosses painted on locked doors before the 1666 Great Fire purges timber slums.
1831
First Cholera Epidemic: Asiatic cholera strikes Sunderland and London; kills 31,000 in cramped industrial back-to-back slums.
1842
Chadwick’s Sanitary Report: Statistical evidence proves poor sanitation shortens labourer life expectancy below 20 years.
1854
Dr John Snow & Soho: Spot map of Broad Street pump proves cholera is waterborne; pump handle removed, ending outbreak.
1858
The Great Stink: Thames stench halts Parliament; Joseph Bazalgette commissioned to build 1,100 miles of London brick sewers.
1875
Public Health Act: Government abandons laissez-faire, legally compelling all local councils to provide clean water and sewers.

Wild moss — Excavations in York and London reveal soft woodland mosses were bundled and used as disposable personal hygiene wipes.

Lesson 5: The Great Stink & London Sewers
33.
The Great Stink of London — Heat fermented hundreds of thousands of tons of raw sewage in the River Thames, creating an unbearable, nauseating chemical stench.
[✓][✗]
34.
Chloride of lime (calcium hypochlorite) — The smell in the Commons debating chamber was so overpowering that MPs had to hold handkerchiefs to their faces and evacuate committee rooms.
[✓][✗]
35.
MPs directly feared for their own lives and could not work — Believing miasma caused cholera, politicians panicked that the toxic Thames vapour would kill them directly in Westminster.
[✓][✗]
36.
Sir Joseph Bazalgette — Bazalgette designed and oversaw the construction of the greatest civil engineering sanitation marvel of the Victorian era.
[✓][✗]
37.
82 miles of main intercepting sewers and 1,100 miles of street sewers — Using 318 million Portland cement bricks, Bazalgette’s system intercepted waste from old sewers and transported it safely east of the city.
[✓][✗]
38.
Egg-shaped (oval) cross-section — The narrow base concentrated low water flows, keeping water velocity fast enough to flush solid waste and prevent sediment blockages.
[✓][✗]
39.
Downriver past Beckton and Crossness into the Thames estuary — Giant steam pumping stations lifted sewage into massive reservoirs, releasing it on the outgoing ebb tide so it washed out to sea.
[✓][✗]
40.
Cholera struck only the East End where sewers were not yet finished — Central and West London remained completely cholera-free, scientifically proving that modern sewage interception stopped disease.
[✓][✗]
Lesson 6: Dr John Snow & The 1875 Public Health Act
41.
Cholera was waterborne, caused by swallowing contaminated water — Snow argued cholera affected the human gut (stomach and intestines), not the lungs, meaning it entered via drinking water or food.
[✓][✗]
42.
A spot map plotting each cholera death outside street addresses — Snow marked 500 fatal cases as black lines on a street grid, demonstrating that deaths clustered tightly around one specific public pump.
[✓][✗]
43.
The Broad Street pump in Soho — Almost all 616 victims had drunk water from the Broad Street pump, while nearby workers at a brewery who drank only beer were unharmed.
[✓][✗]
44.
Remove the handle from the Broad Street pump — Removing the handle stopped residents from drawing contaminated water, causing the local epidemic to collapse immediately.
[✓][✗]
45.
A cracked cesspit from an infected infant leaking into the well — A mother had washed diapers of a baby ill with cholera into a cesspit only three feet away from the porous brick lining of the well.
[✓][✗]
46.
Robert Koch (Vibrio cholerae) — Koch isolated the comma-shaped bacterium in Egypt and India, providing visual, indisputable laboratory proof of Snow’s waterborne theory.
[✓][✗]
47.
The 1875 Public Health Act — Passed under Prime Minister Disraeli, it replaced voluntary guidelines with strict legal duties: clean water, sewage disposal, and health inspectors.
[✓][✗]
48.
The state accepted legal responsibility for citizen health and sanitation — For the first time, government prioritized human life over landlord property rights, establishing modern Britain’s public health foundation.
[✓][✗]

Key Historical Figures & Academic Vocabulary

6 Crucial Protagonists • Spoken Pronunciation Guide • Core Terminology

People & Terms
The Six Crucial Protagonists in Public Health History
Claudius Galen • Greek/Roman Imperial Physician (c. 129–216 AD)
Developed Theory of the Four Humors (Blood, Phlegm, Black Bile, Yellow Bile) and Miasma; dominated medical thought for 1,500 years.
Sir John Harington • Elizabethan Courtier & Author (1560–1612)
Godson of Queen Elizabeth I; invented Britain’s first flushing water closet (‘The Metamorphosis of Ajax’, 1596) using a cistern valve.
Edwin Chadwick • Social Reformer & Civil Servant (1800–1890)
Authored landmark 1842 Sanitary Report proving squalor caused epidemic disease; championed clean piped water and led the 1848 Board of Health.
Dr John Snow • Pioneering Physician & Epidemiologist (1813–1858)
Proved cholera was waterborne during the 1854 Soho epidemic via his famous spot map; ordered the Broad Street pump handle removed.
Sir Joseph Bazalgette • Chief Engineer, Metropolitan Board of Works (1819–1891)
Designed London’s 1,100-mile underground brick sewer network following the 1858 Great Stink, permanently cleansing the River Thames.
Robert Koch • German Microbiologist (1843–1910)
Discovered the cholera bacterium (Vibrio cholerae) under a microscope in 1883, providing conclusive bacteriological proof of water transmission.
Academic Vocabulary with Spoken Pronunciation Guide
Miasma Theory [My-az-muh] (Greek)
The ancient belief that epidemic diseases like plague and cholera were transmitted by inhaling poisonous, foul-smelling air from rotting matter.
Aqueduct & Conduit [Ak-wuh-dukt / Kon-dwit] (Latin)
Engineered bridge or underground stone pipe channels using gravity to transport millions of gallons of spring water from hills into towns.
Gongfermer (Gong Farmer) [Gong-fur-mer] (Medieval English)
A night-soil labourer hired to shovel out human excrement from domestic cesspits exclusively under cover of darkness.
Laissez-faire [Lay-say-fair] (French)
“Leave it alone.” The Victorian political doctrine that government should not interfere in business, taxation, or private property for social welfare.
Epidemiology [Ep-ih-dee-mee-ol-uh-jee] (Science)
The scientific study of how diseases spread through populations, pioneered by Dr John Snow’s geographical spot map in Soho.
Vibrio cholerae [Vib-ree-oh Kol-er-eye] (Biology)
The comma-shaped microscopic waterborne bacterium that infects the small intestine, causing fatal dehydration and rice-water diarrhoea.

How to Write Your End-of-Unit Essay

Key Stage 3 History • Water & Sanitation Enquiry Guide

Essay Guide
Your Essay Question:
“Why did it take so long to clean up Britain’s towns and cities?”
Causal Factor Historical Evidence & Specific Mechanism Significance
Laissez-faire Government Victorian MPs believed the state had no right to regulate private businesses or impose national rates for sewers (1848 Act was voluntary). Delayed national mandatory sanitary reform until the 1875 Public Health Act.
Scientific Ignorance Entrenched belief in Galen’s Miasma Theory; authorities focused on spraying perfumes and lime rather than filtering drinking water. Prevented doctors from recognizing contaminated water pumps until John Snow in 1854.
Vested Interests & Cost Wealthy ratepayers and private water monopolies fiercely resisted taxes needed to lay underground sewers and connect working slums. Landlords neglected outdoor privies and cesspools to maximize rental profits.
Rapid Urbanisation Factory industrialisation caused population explosion in Leeds, Manchester, and London; building back-to-backs overwhelmed old cesspits. Created the perfect breeding ground for deadly Asiatic Cholera outbreaks.
Historiographical Perspectives: What Drove Reform?
Interpretation A (Self-Interest / The Great Stink): Reform happened only when wealthy MPs smelled the Thames in 1858 and feared for their own lives.
Interpretation B (Empirical Science & Activism): Reform was driven by heroic investigators like Chadwick and Snow using maps and statistics to prove filth caused death.
Sentence Starters & Causal Connective Toolkit
  • Point: One crucial reason why sanitation reform was delayed was the widespread belief in...
  • Evidence: For example, during the cholera epidemics of 1831 and 1848, the government...
  • Explanation: This delayed action because politicians prioritised laissez-faire economy over...
  • Judgement: Ultimately, Britain was only cleaned up when the 1858 Great Stink threatened Parliament directly...

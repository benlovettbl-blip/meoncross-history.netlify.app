Curriculum Roadmap & Chronological Spine

1914 – 1919

Trace the 5-year transformation of global conflict: from voluntary recruitment in 1914 to the bloodbath of the Somme, total home front mobilisation, and the Paris Peace Conference.

1914
Outbreak, BEF Mobilisation & Kitchener’s Call Assassination in Sarajevo; Britain enters war; 500,000 volunteers enlist; Pompey Pals formed in Portsmouth.
1914
First Battle of Ypres: Sepoy Khudadad Khan VC Indian Army divisions arrive in France to stabilize the British frontline against overwhelming German assaults.
1915
Munitions Crisis, DORA & Women in Industry Shell shortages prompt state takeover of factories; Lloyd George mobilises women as "Canary Girls"; DORA expands.
1916
Military Service Act (Conscription) & The Somme Compulsory conscription begins; 57,470 British casualties on Day 1 of the Somme; Haig’s war of attrition.
1917
Passchendaele, Tank Warfare & Global Labour Mud of Third Ypres; 140,000 Chinese labourers and BWIR handle ammunition; first major tank attack at Cambrai.
1918
Spring Offensive, Hundred Days & Armistice German Kaiserschlacht fails; Allied combined-arms Hundred Days Offensive breaks Hindenburg Line; Armistice signed.
1919
Paris Peace Conference & Treaty of Versailles The Big Three draft the peace; Article 231 war guilt and £6.6bn reparations imposed; League of Nations founded.
1922
Communal Memorialisation: Stubbington Green Thatched memorial shelter built over village pump on Stubbington Green, honoring 67 fallen local heroes.
🏛️ Hampshire & Meon Valley Local History Connection:

The Great War is engraved into our local Hampshire landscape: from the Portsmouth Dockyard workers who formed the 14th and 15th (Portsmouth) Battalions—the famous Pompey Pals who suffered catastrophic losses on the Somme—to the Stubbington Village War Memorial on the green commemorating 67 fallen men, including blacksmith Arthur Tribbeck’s sons and the Lowry brothers of Manor Way Grange.

Vocabulary Mastery Vault • The Great War

KS3 Disciplinary & Substantive Concepts • Year 9 History
Mastery

Mastery of these high-yield disciplinary and substantive concepts is essential for achieving Grade 6–9 in KS3 History extended writing:

Total War:

A conflict demanding complete mobilization of an entire society's civilian workforce, industry, agriculture, and military resources, eliminating the distinction between combatant and civilian.

Pals Battalions (1914):

Volunteer military units recruited under Lord Kitchener allowing workmates, football teams, and neighbours to serve together; suffered catastrophic concentrated local losses on the Somme.

War of Attrition:

A military strategy aiming to wear down the enemy's manpower, reserves, and industrial supplies through sustained slaughter until total physical collapse.

Defence of the Realm Act (DORA):

Emergency British legislation passed in August 1914 granting the government sweeping autocratic powers to censor the press, requisition property, and control daily civilian habits.

Conscription & Conscientious Objection:

Compulsory military call-up introduced in 1916; opposed by conscientious objectors on moral or religious grounds, who faced harsh military tribunals and imprisonment.

Canary Girls (Munitionettes):

Over 1 million women who machined artillery shells in national projectile factories; nicknamed for their yellowing skin caused by toxic TNT jaundice.

Article 231 (The War Guilt Clause):

The controversial clause in the 1919 Treaty of Versailles forcing Germany to accept sole moral and legal responsibility for causing all Allied damage, justifying £6.6bn reparations.

Lost Generation & Memorialisation:

The generation of young men decimated by industrialized combat; commemorated through stone monuments, war memorial shelters, and annual remembrance rituals across Britain.

The 4 Golden Rules of Historical Extended Writing:
  1. Direct Answer: Open every paragraph with a clear thematic point that directly addresses the enquiry question.
  2. Specific Evidence: Ground every argument in precise historical facts, figures, names, and contemporary legislation.
  3. Causal Connectives: Never just describe events; explain the mechanism of how and why one event caused or accelerated another.
  4. Evaluative Judgement: Weigh competing arguments against explicit historical criteria to reach a nuanced, independent conclusion.

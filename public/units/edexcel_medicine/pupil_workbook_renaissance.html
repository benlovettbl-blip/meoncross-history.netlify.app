Living Timeline • Part 1: Humanism & The Anatomical Revolution (1440–1628)

Instructions: As you study each enquiry lesson, complete the timeline missions by sketching and annotating in the corresponding Key Topic boxes below.
1440 • Johannes Gutenberg: Movable Metal Type Printing Press Key Topic 2.1

Gutenberg invents movable metal type in Mainz (introduced to England by Caxton in 1476). Shatters Church monopoly on medical manuscript copying. Enables rapid mass-production of identical medical texts and anatomical diagrams without copyist errors.

c. 1500 • The Rise of Medical Humanism Key Topic 2.1

Scholars bypass distorted medieval Church translations and translate original Greek and Latin medical manuscripts directly. Promotes human reason, empirical observation, and critical questioning, fostering an intellectual climate ready to challenge Galen.

1543 • Andreas Vesalius Publishes De Humani Corporis Fabrica Key Topic 2.3

Based on direct human dissection at Padua, Vesalius corrects over 300 errors in Galen's anatomy (human lower jaw is one bone, not two; no pores in the heart septum). Proves Galen only dissected animals. Inspires empirical observation across Europe.

1628 • William Harvey Publishes De Motu Cordis Key Topic 2.4

Calculates heart pumps 540 pints of blood per hour—proving blood must circulate through a closed system rather than being continuously manufactured by the liver. Demonstrates vein valves ensure one-way flow toward the heart.

2/14 Gutenberg gave scholars mass-produced books, and Vesalius gave them accurate anatomy—Galen’s 1,500-year winning streak was officially broken.

Living Timeline • Part 2: The Scientific Method & Bedside Observation (1660–1676)

Instructions: As you study each enquiry lesson, complete the timeline missions by sketching and annotating in the corresponding Key Topic boxes below.
1660 / 1662 • The Royal Society Founded & Granted Royal Charter Key Topic 2.1

Operates under Nullius in Verba ('Take nobody's word for it'); receives Royal Charter from Charles II in 1662. Publishes Philosophical Transactions (1665), Europe's first peer-reviewed journal, institutionalising empirical laboratory testing.

1665 • Robert Hooke Publishes Micrographia (Leeuwenhoek Microbes 1676) Key Topic 2.1

Hooke uses compound microscopes to reveal plant cells and fleas. In 1676, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek observes living 'animalcules' (bacteria). However, because microbes are not linked to disease, the discovery saves zero lives.

1665 • Case Study: The Great Plague Catastrophe in London Key Topic 2.5

Kills 100,000 Londoners (20% of the city). Mayor enforces 28-day household quarantines with watchmen and red crosses. Catastrophic slaughter of 200,000 dogs and cats allows infected black rat fleas to proliferate unchecked.

1676 • Thomas Sydenham Publishes Observationes Medicae Key Topic 2.2

Nicknamed 'The English Hippocrates', Sydenham pioneers clinical bedside observation, classifying diseases into distinct species by symptoms. Prescribes cooling regimes for smallpox and cinchona bark (quinine) for malaria fevers.

Sydenham proved practical observation beat theoretical philosophy every time—even if he still carried leeches in his medical bag. 3/14

Task 2: Core Knowledge Anchor • High-Yield GCSE Concepts
The 'English Hippocrates': Sydenham rejected university book-learning, pulse-taking charts, and astrology, insisting that physicians must sit at the bedside and carefully record patient symptoms over time.
Classifying Diseases as Species: Argued that diseases were separate biological entities with specific characteristics (like plants in a botany book), successfully distinguishing scarlet fever from measles.
Treatments & The Limits of Change: Prescribed cinchona bark (quinine) for malaria and cool regimes (open windows, light blankets) for smallpox; yet continued to rely on traditional bloodletting and purging.

KT2.4: William Harvey & The Circulation of the Blood (1628)

Key Topic 2.4
Task 1: Prior Knowledge Retrieval (Do Now) Recall Target: 4/4

Task 2: Core Knowledge Anchor • High-Yield GCSE Concepts
The Heart as a Mechanical Pump: Influenced by Renaissance mechanical water pumps, Harvey proved the heart is a muscular pump that forces blood into arteries, which returns through veins in a closed, one-way system.
Disproving Galen's Liver Theory: Galen taught blood was made in the liver and consumed like fuel. Harvey mathematically calculated the heart pumped 540 pints an hour (more than body weight), proving blood must recirculate.
Forensic Experiments & Treatment Limits: Used tight ligatures on arms to prove vein valves only allow blood to flow towards the heart; published De Motu Cordis (1628); yet bloodletting continued because doctors lacked drug cures.

Task 2: Core Knowledge Anchor • High-Yield GCSE Concepts
The Great Plague Catastrophe (1665): Swept through overcrowded London, killing roughly 100,000 citizens (20% of the population); bodies collected at night by dead-carts and dumped in mass parish lime pits.
Civic Plague Orders (Change): Mayor enforced strict municipal quarantine: infected houses padlocked for 28 days, painted with a red cross and 'Lord have mercy upon us', guarded by watchmen; 200,000 dogs and cats slaughtered.
Stagnation in Treatment (Continuity): Physicians had no understanding of rat fleas or bacteria; citizens smoked tobacco and carried pomanders against miasma; apothecaries sold fake 'Plague Water'; bleeding and purging persisted.

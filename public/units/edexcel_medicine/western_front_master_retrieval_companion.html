1. Theatre of War & Trenches
Key sectors: Ypres Salient (Hill 60, Passchendaele mud), The Somme (1916 casualty crisis), Arras (chalk caves, underground hospital), Cambrai (tanks, blood depot). Trench layout: front line, support, reserve, communication, saps, and deep dugouts.
2. Illnesses, Weaponry & Trauma
Environmental conditions: trench foot (whale oil, dry socks), trench fever (body lice), shell shock (NYDN). Weaponry: artillery shells (58% wounds), shrapnel bullets, gas gangrene/tetanus soil bacteria, poison gas (chlorine, phosgene, mustard gas).
3. Evacuation Chain & Innovations
Chain of evacuation: RAP → ADS/MDS → CCS → Base Hospitals. Medical corps: RAMC & FANY. Breakthroughs: Thomas Splint (82% survival), Carrel-Dakin, debridement, mobile X-rays, blood storage, Cushing brain surgery, Gillies plastic reconstruction.

8 Core Disciplinary Terms • Pearson Edexcel Specification Glossary Historic Environment Precision
1. Ypres Salient
Vulnerable bulge in the British line surrounded on 3 sides; subjected to converging German artillery and liquid Passchendaele mud.
2. Gas Gangrene
Lethal anaerobic bacterial infection caused by soil bacteria (*Clostridium*) in deep, oxygen-deprived shrapnel muscle wounds.
3. Wound Debridement
Surgical excision of dead, contaminated tissue and foreign uniform cloth from jagged wounds before suturing to halt infection.
4. Carrel-Dakin Method
Continuous chemical irrigation system flushing sterilised sodium hypochlorite solution through rubber tubes into deep wound pockets.
5. Thomas Splint
Rigid traction splint introduced to France in Dec 1915; prevented broken femur ends grinding, lifting survival from 20% to 82%.
6. Sodium Citrate
Anticoagulant chemical (Lewisohn 1915) that stopped blood clotting, enabling indirect transfusion and revolutionising blood storage.
7. Cambrai Blood Depot
World's first blood bank created by Oswald Robertson in Nov 1917, storing Universal Group O blood in iced bottles for 26 days.
8. Triage Protocol
Systematic casualty sorting at the Casualty Clearing Station (CCS): walking wounded, urgent surgery cases, and moribund (fatal).

Enquiries 1–2: Theatre & Environment
Q1 A battlefield bulge surrounded on three sides by the enemy
Left British troops exposed to German artillery fire from higher ground on surrounding ridges.
Q2 It protected the English Channel ports used for supplies and reinforcements
If Ypres fell, the German army could cut off supply links between Britain and the front line.
Q3 57,000 casualties (over 19,000 killed)
The worst single day in British military history, completely overwhelming frontline medical stations.
Q4 An underground hospital in chalk tunnels (Thompson’s Cave)
Contained 700 beds, operating rooms, water, and power, completely safe from shellfire.
Q5 Massed deployment of over 450 British tanks
Broke German lines and created the conditions for the first operational blood bank.
Q6 Frontline, Support, Reserve, and Communication trenches
Allowed troops, food, ammunition, and wounded soldiers to move safely between front and rear.
Q7 To stop shell blast waves and prevent enemy riflemen firing straight down the line
Protected men from exploding shells, but made carrying stretchers around corners very difficult.
Q8 Destroyed roads caused motor ambulances to get stuck, forcing stretcher-bearers to walk for miles
Teams of four stretcher-bearers had to carry heavy stretchers by hand through knee-deep mud.
Q9 Standing for days in cold, waterlogged mud and tight wet boots
Slowed blood flow, causing feet to go numb, blister, swell, and turn gangrenous.
Q10 Rubbing feet with whale oil and changing into dry socks twice daily
Officers held daily foot inspections; soldiers worked in pairs to dry each other’s feet.
Q11 Amputation of toes or the whole foot
Necessary to stop spreading gangrene and fatal blood poisoning once tissue died.
Q12 Trench Fever
Caused high fever, shaking, severe headaches, and aching leg muscles, disabling men for weeks.
Q13 Body lice
Lice thrived in dirty woollen clothes; scratching bites rubbed infected louse droppings into the skin.
Q14 Built division bathhouses and mobile steam delousing vans for uniforms
Hot steam vans baked uniforms to kill lice eggs, while soldiers washed in hot water.
Q15 NYD.N ("Not Yet Diagnosed, Nervous")
Used to avoid panic, prevent pension claims, and discourage men from seeking discharge.
Q16 Uncontrollable shaking, nightmares, loss of speech, and confusion
Caused by constant fear, deafening shellfire noise, and the trauma of explosive bombardments.
Enquiries 3–4: Trauma & Evacuation
Q17 High-explosive artillery shells and shrapnel
Exploding steel shell casings threw jagged fragments that shattered bones and ripped muscle.
Q18 The soil had been heavily manured for centuries, filling it with deadly bacteria
Manured soil contained tetanus and gas gangrene bacteria that multiplied in deep wounds without oxygen.
Q19 Gas Gangrene
Soil bacteria produced poisons that turned muscle black, killing tissue within hours and causing death in a day.
Q20 Anti-tetanus serum (ATS)
Massively reduced deaths from tetanus (lockjaw) caused by soil bacteria entering open wounds.
Q21 The Brodie Helmet
Made of pressed steel with a wide rim to deflect falling shrapnel and dirt.
Q22 Chlorine Gas
A greenish-yellow choking gas that damaged the lungs, causing soldiers to drown in bodily fluids.
Q23 Phosgene Gas
Smelled like mouldy hay and acted slowly, often killing an exposed soldier within 48 hours.
Q24 Mustard Gas
An oily liquid that stayed in the soil for weeks, causing severe skin blisters, blindness, and lung burns.
Q25 To move, sort, and treat wounded soldiers in stages from the battlefield to safety
Ensured casualties were treated quickly without clogging up frontline medical stations.
Q26 Within 200 metres of the frontline trench, in a dugout or ruined cellar
Positioned close to the fighting so wounded men could receive basic first aid within minutes.
Q27 Basic first aid (bandages, pain relief, splints); no surgery could be done
Patched up walking wounded to fight, or sent stretcher cases back to dressing stations.
Q28 The Dressing Station (Advanced or Main Dressing Station)
Run by the Field Ambulance; gave fresh dressings, tetanus jabs, and hot drinks.
Q29 7 to 12 miles behind the front line, near railways and outside shell range
Positioned near railheads so stabilized wounded could be sent by train to coastal hospitals.
Q30 Triage: sorting wounded into walking cases, urgent surgery, and dying
Focused limited surgeons and operating tables on men with the best chance of survival.
Q31 Near French and Belgian ports (Calais, Boulogne, Le Havre, Rouen)
Close to the sea so long-term casualties could be loaded directly onto hospital ships for Britain.
Q32 Driving motor ambulance convoys under shellfire between medical stations
FANY drivers were the first women to drive British military vehicles on the Western Front.
Enquiries 5–6: Surgery & Innovations
Q33 Cutting away all dead, damaged, and infected tissue around the wound
Removed the dead flesh that dangerous gas gangrene bacteria needed to grow.
Q34 The Carrel-Dakin Method
Flushed a mild antiseptic bleach solution through rubber tubes directly into deep wounds.
Q35 The chemical solution spoiled quickly and had to be freshly mixed every 6 hours
Hard to prepare during large battles when thousands of casualties arrived at once.
Q36 Only 20% survived (an 80% death rate)
Bone ends ground together in bumpy wagons, tearing large blood vessels and causing fatal shock.
Q37 Robert Jones (nephew of Hugh Owen Thomas)
Set up training centres for medics; survival rates for femur fractures jumped from 20% to 82%.
Q38 Held the broken leg in rigid tension, stopping bone ends from cutting blood vessels
Kept the broken leg still during rough journeys, preventing fatal internal bleeding and shock.
Q39 Built six mobile X-ray vans that drove directly to Casualty Clearing Stations
Allowed surgeons to locate bullets and shrapnel inside soldiers before starting surgery.
Q40 They showed metal bullets and bone, but could not detect dirty cloth scraps
Uniform scraps carried manure bacteria deep into wounds but were invisible on X-ray plates.
Q41 Discovered different human blood groups (A, B, and O)
Showed why earlier transfusions often caused blood to clump together and kill the patient.
Q42 Blood Group O
Essential for battle casualties because Group O blood could be given safely without testing.
Q43 Sodium Citrate
Stopped blood clotting without poisoning the wounded patient receiving the transfusion.
Q44 Citrate glucose solution
Kept red blood cells alive in refrigerated storage, making blood banks possible.
Q45 Oswald Hope Robertson (an American army doctor)
Collected Group O blood before the battle, storing 22 bottles in ice to treat casualties in shock.
Q46 Harvey Cushing
Used local anaesthetic to stop brain swelling and operated close to the front at CCS units.
Q47 Queen’s Hospital in Sidcup, Kent
Designed by Harold Gillies for facial surgery; carried out over 11,000 plastic operations.
Q48 The "Tube Pedicle" (walking skin graft)
Rolled skin from the chest into a tube to keep blood flowing before stitching it onto the face.

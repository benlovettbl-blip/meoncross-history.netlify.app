8 Core Disciplinary Terms • Pearson Edexcel Specification Glossary Historic Environment Precision
1. Ypres Salient Bulge in the line surrounded by Germans on 3 sides; subjected to converging artillery fire and waterlogged Passchendaele mud.
2. Gas Gangrene Anaerobic bacterial infection caused by soil bacteria (*Clostridium*) in deep, oxygen-deprived muscle wounds; produced foul gas.
3. Debridement Surgical cutting away of dead, infected tissue from jagged shrapnel wounds before stitching, preventing gangrenous spread.
4. Carrel-Dakin Method Continuous chemical irrigation using sterilised rubber tubes flushing sodium hypochlorite solution into deep wound pockets.
5. Thomas Splint Rigid metal traction splint introduced in 1915; prevented broken femur ends grinding together, lifting survival from 20% to 82%.
6. Sodium Citrate Anticoagulant chemical discovered by Richard Lewisohn in 1915, preventing blood clotting and enabling indirect blood storage.
7. Blood Depot (Cambrai) World's first blood bank created by Oswald Robertson in 1917, storing Universal Group O blood in iced bottles for rapid transfusions.
8. Triage Protocol Systematic sorting of casualties at CCS into three groups: walking wounded, urgent surgery cases, and moribund (fatal).

Western Front Chronology & Medical Milestones (1914–1918)
Oct–Nov 1914: First Battle of Ypres BEF stops German advance to Channel ports; salient established; trench warfare begins.
Autumn 1914: Anti-Tetanus Serum Issued Routine prophylactic injections administered to all wounded men, preventing fatal lockjaw.
Apr–May 1915: Second Battle of Ypres Germans introduce chlorine poison gas; urine-soaked cotton pads used before gas masks.
April 1915: Mining at Hill 60 British tunnel deep under German positions in Ypres salient and detonate 5 massive mines.
July 1915: Hypo Helmet Gas Masks First manufactured gas helmets issued; superseded in 1916 by the Small Box Respirator.
Autumn 1915: Brodie Helmets Issued Steel helmets with wide brims replace cloth caps, reducing fatal head injuries by 80%.
Dec 1915: Thomas Splint Introduced Robert Jones introduces traction splint to France; femur survival rate leaps from 20% to 82%.
1 July 1916: First Day of the Somme British suffer 57,000 casualties in one day; medical evacuation facilities overwhelmed.
April 1917: Battle of Arras & Cave Hospital Underground hospital inside chalk quarries (Thompson’s Cave) treats 700 beds under shelter.
Jul–Nov 1917: Third Battle of Ypres (Passchendaele) Constant rain and shelling turn clay into liquid swamp; men and mules drown in mud.
Nov 1917: Battle of Cambrai Blood Depot Oswald Hope Robertson creates world’s first blood bank using stored universal Group O blood.
1917: Queen’s Hospital, Sidcup Harold Gillies opens specialist hospital for facial reconstructive surgery and tube pedicle grafts.
Spring 1918: German Spring Offensive Mobile warfare resumes; forward Casualty Clearing Stations forced to evacuate rapidly.
11 Nov 1918: The Armistice Hostilities cease; over 113,000 RAMC personnel and 240,000 amputees complete wartime service.
The 4-Stage Chain of Evacuation Architecture
1. Regimental Aid Post (RAP)
Location: Within 200m of front line • Personnel: Regimental Medical Officer & Stretcher-Bearers
Immediate emergency first aid (bandages, tourniquets, morphia). Patched up walking wounded to fight; stabilised serious casualties. No surgery performed.
2. Field Ambulance & Dressing Stations (ADS / MDS)
Location: ½ mile to 1 mile behind front • Personnel: RAMC Field Ambulance units, orderlies, nurses
Emergency triage and anti-tetanus injections. Located in abandoned farms or tents. Serious casualties loaded onto horse or motor ambulances for transport.
3. Casualty Clearing Station (CCS)
Location: 7 to 12 miles back (near railway lines) • Personnel: Specialist surgical teams, nurses (QAIMNS), mobile X-rays
The primary life-saving surgical unit. Triage into walking wounded, urgent surgery cases, and moribund. Emergency operations for gas gangrene and compound fractures.
4. Base Hospitals
Location: French coastal ports (Calais, Boulogne, Le Havre) • Personnel: Civilian specialists, surgical units, specialist wards
Long-term patient recovery, specialist treatment for head wounds, burns, and gas. Casualties evacuated by hospital ship to "Blighty" (Britain) for rehabilitation.
Key Exam Principle: The Chain of Evacuation was organized by triage urgency. Rapid forward surgery at the CCS was vital because gas gangrene killed within 24 hours. The Thomas Splint kept femoral fractures stable during transport, cutting deaths from 80% to 20%.

2. Shrapnel drove muddy, bacteria-soaked uniform cloth deep into the body [✓] [✗]

3. The soil had been heavily manured for centuries, filling it with deadly bacteria [✓] [✗]

4. Gas Gangrene [✓] [✗]
Soil bacteria produced poisons that turned muscle black, killing tissue within hours and causing death in a day.
5. Anti-tetanus serum (ATS) [✓] [✗]

6. Soldiers wore only soft cloth caps while looking over trench parapets [✓] [✗]

7. The Brodie Helmet [✓] [✗]
Made of pressed steel with a wide rim to deflect falling shrapnel and dirt.
8. Chlorine Gas [✓] [✗]
A greenish-yellow choking gas that damaged the lungs, causing soldiers to drown in bodily fluids.
9. Held cloth pads soaked in urine over their noses and mouths [✓] [✗]
The ammonia in urine neutralized the acidic chlorine gas, buying precious minutes.
10. Phosgene Gas [✓] [✗]
Smelled like mouldy hay and acted slowly, often killing an exposed soldier within 48 hours.
11. Mustard Gas [✓] [✗]
An oily liquid that stayed in the soil for weeks, causing severe skin blisters, blindness, and lung burns.
12. The British Army rapidly issued effective gas masks (box respirators) [✓] [✗]
The Small Box Respirator (issued 1916) filtered out deadly gases, keeping death rates low.

Lesson 4: The Chain of Evacuation
1. To move, sort, and treat wounded soldiers in stages from the battlefield to safety [✓] [✗]
Ensured casualties were treated quickly without clogging up frontline medical stations.
2. Within 200 metres of the frontline trench, in a dugout or ruined cellar [✓] [✗]
Positioned close to the fighting so wounded men could receive basic first aid within minutes.
3. Basic first aid (bandages, pain relief, splints); no surgery could be done [✓] [✗]
Patched up walking wounded to fight, or sent stretcher cases back to dressing stations.
4. The Dressing Station (Advanced or Main Dressing Station) [✓] [✗]

1. Wounds were already filled with mud and manure bacteria the moment a bullet hit [✓] [✗]

2. Cutting away all dead, damaged, and infected tissue around the wound [✓] [✗]

Primary Historical Sources for Western Front Enquiries Edexcel Specification Types
1. National Army Records & War Diaries Maintained daily by British Army units. Provides factual dates, battalion movements, casualty tallies, and battlefield conditions. Objective military records, though may understate defeats or panic.
2. Medical Officers' Day Books & RAMC Reports Detailed clinical accounts written by surgeons and Regimental Medical Officers at RAPs and CCS units. Extremely accurate regarding wound types, treatments, and shortages of supplies.
3. Hospital Admission Registers Log books recording patient names, ranks, wound classifications, dates admitted, and discharge/death status. Ideal for statistical enquiries into fracture survival or gas mortality rates.
4. Personal Memoirs & Soldiers' Letters / Diaries First-hand emotional accounts from soldiers, stretcher-bearers, and nurses. Rich in personal experience of pain, mud, and morale, but written from individual, subjective perspectives.
5. Official & Contemporary Photographs British official war photographers (e.g. Ernest Brooks). Shows physical trench layout, deep mud, and evacuation transport. May be staged for public morale or censored by authorities.
6. Red Cross & FANY Voluntary Archives Reports and logs kept by volunteer nurses and ambulance drivers. Highly useful for investigating the role of women, motor ambulance transport, and mobile bath units.

5. Essential Western Front Disciplinary & Clinical Distinctions Examiner Precision
Antiseptic vs. Aseptic: Antiseptic kills germs in wounds (carbolic acid). Aseptic prevents germs entering operating theatres. Aseptic failed in 1914 because fertile soil bacteria were already embedded in shrapnel wounds before hospital admission.
Debridement vs. Carrel-Dakin: Debridement is the mechanical cutting away of dead, infected tissue. Carrel-Dakin uses sterilized rubber tubes to flush sodium hypochlorite continuously into deep wound pockets to prevent anaerobic infection.
Direct Transfusion vs. Blood Depot: 1915 direct transfusion required donor-to-patient tubing (Rous & Turner). 1917 Cambrai used sodium citrate & glucose in refrigerated bottles (Oswald Robertson's blood bank) for rapid treatment of shock.
RAMC vs. FANY Corps: RAMC (Royal Army Medical Corps) provided official military doctors, surgeons, orderlies, and stretcher-bearers. FANY (First Aid Nursing Yeomanry) provided voluntary female motor ambulance drivers and frontline aid.
Shrapnel vs. High-Explosive Shells: Shrapnel shells exploded in mid-air spraying lead pellets and driving contaminated uniform cloth into flesh. High-explosive shells exploded on impact with massive blast concussions, causing compound fractures and traumatic amputations.
Casualty Clearing Station vs. Base Hospital: CCS was the primary forward surgical unit (10–12 miles back) performing emergency triage and amputations. Base Hospitals (coastal ports) provided specialist long-term recovery and transport to "Blighty".

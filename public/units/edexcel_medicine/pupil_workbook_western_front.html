The Western Front Evacuation Chain

RAMC & FANY Lifeline: From No Man’s Land to Base Hospital
The 6 Sequential Stations of the Evacuation Chain
1. STRETCHER BEARERS
Frontline & No Man’s Land
• 16 men per battalion of 1,000.
• 4 to 6 bearers per stretcher in mud.
• Apply first field dressing under fire.
2. REGIMENTAL AID POST (RAP)
200–300 yards behind front line
• Located in communication trench/cellar.
• RMO gives morphine & tetanus serum.
• Cannot perform major surgery.
3. DRESSING STATIONS (ADS/MDS)
1 to 3 miles behind front line
• Run by RAMC Field Ambulance.
• Treat shock with sweet tea & blankets.
• Sorts casualties for motor ambulance.
4. CASUALTY CLEARING STATION
7 to 12 miles back (rail/canal)
• Surgical core: operating theatres & X-rays.
• Female nurses (QAIMNS).
• Triage: Walking, Immediate, Moribund.
5. BASE HOSPITALS
Coastal ports (Boulogne/Calais)
• Large general/stationary hospitals.
• Extended recovery & specialized care.
• Linked by hospital trains & canal barges.
6. "BLIGHTY" (BRITAIN)
Hospital ships across Channel
• Queen’s Hospital Sidcup (Plastic surgery).
• Roehampton (Amputee limb fitting).
• Netley Hospital (Military psychiatry).
RAMC TRIAGE CLASSIFICATION AT CCS
• WHITE TAG (Walking Wounded): Minor shrapnel cuts, superficial gas irritation. Cleaned, patched, and returned to the line within days.
• RED TAG (Immediate Surgery): Compound fractures, abdominal hemorrhages, chest trauma with a viable chance of survival if operated on at once.
• GREEN/NO TAG (The Moribund): Hopeless wounds (severe brain destruction, massive blood loss). Made comfortable with morphine until death.
TRANSPORT METHODS: HORSE, MOTOR & CANAL
• Horse Ambulances (1914): Slow, violent jolting over shell craters caused hemorrhagic shock and re-fractured bones.
• Motor Ambulances & FANY (1915+): Faster transport; voluntary women drivers braved artillery to evacuate men from dressing stations.
• RAMC Canal Barges: Smooth, vibration-free water transit along French canals, ideal for post-operative brain and abdominal trauma.
Forensic Patient Journey: Private Thomas Henderson (2nd Royal Scots) Ypres Sector • 14 September 1917
08:15 Frontline: Struck by high-explosive shell shrapnel in trench. Shattered right femur (compound fracture); severe arterial bleed.
08:45 Stretcher Retrieval: 4 stretcher bearers carry him through flooded saps to the Regimental Aid Post dugout.
09:10 RAP First Aid: RMO fits Thomas Splint with windlass traction, injects 500 units anti-tetanus serum and 1/3 grain morphine.
11:30 ADS to CCS: Transported by FANY motor ambulance van 8 miles back to No. 46 Casualty Clearing Station.
13:00 CCS Triage: Classified Red Tag. Mobile X-ray locates 3 metal fragments. Surgeon debrides necrotic flesh; Carrel-Dakin tubes inserted.
Day 4 Base Hospital: Evacuated by heated RAMC canal barge to Boulogne; loaded on hospital ship for recovery in Blighty.
Rule 1 of the Evacuation Chain: If the stretcher bearer drops you, do not complain—he has just dodged a 5.9-inch howitzer shell. 3/16

Key Historical Vocabulary & Forensic Definitions
• Frontline Trench: The foremost defensive trench situated closest to enemy lines, featuring a firestep, sandbag parapet, and barbed wire entanglements, where infantry mounted guard.
• Communication Trench: Deep, connecting trenches dug at right angles to parallel lines, allowing fresh troops, ammunition, food, and stretcher-borne casualties to move under cover.
• Traverse: A sharp 90-degree bend or earthwork buttress constructed into trench lines to prevent enemy riflemen firing down the ditch and contain artillery shell bursts.
• Ypres Salient: A highly vulnerable, low-lying British military bulge around the medieval town of Ypres, surrounded on three sides by German forces holding higher commanding ridges.

Key Historical Vocabulary & Forensic Definitions
• Trench Foot: A painful, necrotic condition of the lower feet caused by prolonged exposure to cold, wet mud and poor circulation, which could degenerate into gangrene requiring amputation.
• Whale Oil: A thick, pungent animal grease supplied in large barrels to frontline British troops, rubbed into bare feet daily to create a water-resistant protective barrier.
• Trench Fever: A debilitating bacterial infection (Pyrexia of Unknown Origin) caused by Bartonella quintana and transmitted by body lice faeces, producing severe shin pains and high fevers.
• Body Lice: Small wingless parasitic insects that nested in the warm seams of woollen service uniforms, biting human skin and spreading trench fever across the Western Front.

Key Historical Vocabulary & Forensic Definitions
• High-Explosive Shell: Heavy artillery ammunition filled with TNT that detonated on impact, shattering its steel casing into thousands of jagged, high-velocity shrapnel fragments.
• Gas Gangrene: A ferocious, life-threatening infection caused by anaerobic bacteria (Clostridium welchii) from manured soil that produced gas bubbles and rotted living muscle tissue.
• Tetanus: A lethal bacterial disease (Clostridium tetani) from soil causing severe muscle spasms and lockjaw, successfully prevented by routine injections of tetanus antitoxin.
• Brodie Helmet: A pressed-steel combat helmet patented in 1915 by John Brodie, featuring a wide brim to protect soldiers’ heads from downward-falling artillery shrapnel.

Key Historical Vocabulary & Forensic Definitions
• Chain of Evacuation: The organized, multi-stage medical evacuation network designed to transport wounded soldiers from the frontline firestep back to specialist hospitals in Britain.
• Regimental Aid Post (RAP): The foremost medical dugout located within 200–300 yards of the frontline, where the medical officer administered field dressings, morphine, and tetanus antitoxin.
• Casualty Clearing Station (CCS): The critical surgical field hospital situated 7–12 miles behind the front line beside rail networks, equipped with sterile operating theatres, X-rays, and female nurses.
• Triage: The systematic sorting of casualties into three categories upon arrival at a CCS (walking wounded, immediate surgery, and moribund) to prioritize life-saving operations.

Key Historical Vocabulary & Forensic Definitions
• Compound Fracture: A severe bone fracture where jagged broken bone ends pierce through muscle and skin, creating an open wound highly vulnerable to lethal bacterial infection.
• Thomas Splint: A rigid metal frame designed by Hugh Owen Thomas and championed by Robert Jones, applying traction to broken thigh bones and reducing femur mortality from 80% to 20%.
• Traction: The steady mechanical pulling force applied to a fractured limb to counteract violent muscle spasms, pull bone ends into alignment, and prevent internal hemorrhaging.
• Wound Debridement: The surgical cutting away (excision) of all dead, devitalized tissue and foreign matter around a wound to starve anaerobic bacteria of the material they need to cause gangrene.

Provenance: Official photograph produced for the RAMC Training and Photographic Archive, Western Front, c.1916. Wellcome Collection, London (Shelfmark: RAMC 512/3).
Context: An official instructional photograph compiled for the Royal Army Medical Corps c.1916, illustrating the precise method of applying the Thomas Splint to a patient with a compound fracture of the femur. The padded ring fits firmly against the pelvis while traction is maintained on the foot, pulling the limb straight to prevent movement of broken bone fragments.

Key Historical Vocabulary & Forensic Definitions
• Sodium Citrate: A chemical anticoagulant added to fresh blood in 1914 by Hustin and Agote that stopped blood from clotting outside the body by binding calcium ions.
• Blood Depot: The world’s first blood bank established by Captain Oswald Robertson at the Battle of Cambrai (1917), storing citrated blood in ice chests for up to 28 days.
• Harvey Cushing: Pioneering American neurosurgeon who operated under local anesthetic and used electromagnets to extract shrapnel from brain tissue, reducing head wound mortality to 29%.
• Harold Gillies: New Zealand-born surgeon who founded Queen’s Hospital, Sidcup, pioneering modern plastic surgery and the tubed pedicle skin flap for disfigured soldiers.

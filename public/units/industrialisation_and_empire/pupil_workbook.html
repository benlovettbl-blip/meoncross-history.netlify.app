Primary Visual Evidence • Industrial Sublime Philip James de Loutherbourg, RA (1801)

In 1801, Philip James de Loutherbourg captured the Madeley Wood (Bedlam) ironworks in Coalbrookdale, Shropshire. Depicting the blazing coke blast furnaces illuminating the nighttime sky, the painting stands as the definitive visual monument of Britain’s dawn as the world’s first industrial superpower:

1. Blazing Coke Blast Furnaces:
Violent orange and crimson flames erupt from open furnace tops, symbolising the substitution of timber by coal and coke smelting.
2. Choking Plumes & Nocturnal Sky:
Dense billowing black smoke obscures the moonlit sky, capturing the dramatic Sublime power and environmental reality of heavy industry.
3. Cast Engine Boilers & Slag:
Foreground heaps of newly cast cylindrical steam engine boilers, pipes, and cooling slag illustrate mass metallurgical production.
4. Horse Carter & Muscle Power:
A carter guiding workhorses hauling goods highlights the historic threshold between organic animal labor and mechanized steam power.

Metallurgy

Disciplinary Significance: Loutherbourg’s masterpiece captures the Industrial Sublime—a fusion of awe and terror—symbolizing the dawn of an Anthropocene era in which human technological mastery eclipsed natural biological limits.

Does Loutherbourg depict Coalbrookdale as an apocalyptic vision of hell and human suffering, or as a heroic celebration of British technological mastery and industrial progress?

1784 • Henry Cort Patented Puddling at Funtley Ironworks Enquiry 1

Fareham ironmaster Henry Cort invents the reverberatory puddling furnace and grooved rolling mill along the River Meon, enabling Britain to produce tough naval wrought iron from domestic coal and ending reliance on Baltic imports.

1787 • Portsmouth Royal Dockyard Naval Trials Enquiry 1

Admiralty master smiths at Portsmouth Dockyard conduct rigorous metallurgical tests on Funtley iron, proving it fully equal to Swedish Orgrounds iron for ship anchors, mast hoops, and naval fittings for the Royal Navy.

1819 • The Peterloo Massacre at St Peter's Field, Manchester Enquiry 4

Manchester magistrates order Yeomanry cavalry to charge 60,000 peaceful cotton workers demanding universal suffrage, killing 18 and injuring 650; Parliament responds with the repressive Six Acts.

1830 • The Hampshire "Captain Swing" Riots Across the Meon Valley Enquiry 4

Starving farm labourers in the Meon Valley, Selborne, and Fareham smash threshing machines and burn ricks under the mythical name "Captain Swing"; the Winchester Assizes sentences 101 men to penal transportation to Australia.

Living Timeline • Part 2: Factory Reform, Slum Sanitation & The Ballot (1832–1872)

Living Timeline Dual-Coding Protocol: Complete each milestone sketchpad using dual-coding (combining visual storytelling symbols, causal arrows, and dates) to master the chronological spine across the centre-fold.
1832 • The Sadler Committee Eyewitness Report on Child Labour Enquiry 2

Michael Sadler’s parliamentary committee publishes horrifying eyewitness testimony of 14-hour days, physical beatings, and crippling injuries suffered by child piecers in northern textile mills, forcing the passage of the 1833 Factory Act.

1832 • Passage of the Great Reform Act Enfranchising Manchester Enquiry 5

Terrified of revolution and a run on the Bank of England, Parliament abolishes 56 rotten boroughs and grants MPs to Manchester, Birmingham, and Leeds, though the £10 franchise deliberately excludes the working class.

1858 • The Great Stink of the Thames & Bazalgette's Sewers Enquiry 3

A boiling heatwave turns the sewage-filled Thames into an unbearable stench that invades Parliament; MPs immediately fund Joseph Bazalgette’s 82-mile underground brick sewer network, permanently ending waterborne cholera.

1872 • The Secret Ballot Act First Deployed at Pontefract Enquiry 5

William Gladstone’s government passes the Ballot Act, abolishing corrupt open hustings and requiring private voting in wooden booths, permanently eliminating landlord coercion and employer intimidation of working voters.

Between 1784 and 1872, British power was transformed by industrial metallurgy, political protest, and democratic expansion. 9/16

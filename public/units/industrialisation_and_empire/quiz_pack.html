Enquiry 1: Iron & Steam: Henry Cort (Q1–Q8)
Q1: 1769
Watt’s 1769 patent dramatically increased fuel efficiency and transformed coal into continuous rotative power, liberating factories from river valleys.
Q2: Molten metal flowed into sand moulds resembling suckling pigs; raw coal contaminated it with sulphur, leaving it brittle as glass
Sulphur-rich pit coal made British pig iron brittle and prone to shattering under high steam pressure or naval artillery shock.
Q3: Funtley (Fontley), near Fareham
Cort leased Funtley Mill in 1775 to exploit River Meon water power and direct tidal water access down Fareham Creek into Portsmouth Dockyard.
Q4: An arched brick ceiling deflected pure radiant heat onto the iron hearth, keeping smoke and sulphur separate from the metal
The reverberatory design acted like a giant radiant oven, burning away carbon impurities while preventing sulphur contamination from the burning coal.
Q5: They continuously squeezed out liquid slag and rolled uniform bars in seconds rather than requiring hours of slow manual hammering
Hydraulic grooved rollers mechanized the consolidation of wrought iron, standardising naval dimensions and multiplying output fifteen-fold.
Q6: Master smiths cold-twisted bars into corkscrews, bent rods double, and forged anchor rings with 20-pound sledgehammers without cracking
Admiralty trials proved Funtley iron equaled Sweden’s finest Orgrounds iron, shattering Britain’s dangerous reliance on Baltic imports.
Q7: Portsmouth was the world’s largest naval consumer of wrought iron, providing an immediate military market, naval trial facilities, and tidal transport
Without the Royal Navy’s urgent demand for reliable ship bolts and anchors, Cort would have lacked the capital, testing facilities, or market to develop puddling.
Q8: They revealed that enslaved Black metallurgists operated grooved rollers years earlier, proving the Industrial Revolution was tied to Atlantic colonial networks
Dr Jenny Bulstrode showed that Jamaican ironworks run by enslaved Africans used grooved rollers before Cort, challenging the traditional "lone English genius" narrative.
Enquiry 2: Child Labour, Slums & Health (Q9–Q16)
Q9: Scavengers and piecers
Scavengers crawled beneath active, whirring machinery to sweep up loose cotton fibres, while piecers tied broken threads at constant risk of mutilation.
Q10: The Sadler Committee Report (1832)
Michael Sadler’s report shocked Victorian Britain by documenting horrific factory cruelty, paving the way for the Factory Act of 1833.
Q11: Cholera
Cholera caused sudden, fatal dehydration through diarrhoea and vomiting, killing tens of thousands in unsewered, overcrowded industrial slums.
Q12: Deadly combinations of open cesspools, contaminated well water, unventilated back-to-back cellars, typhus, and exhausting 14-hour factory work
Rapid, unregulated urban migration created catastrophic sanitary conditions, where infants and labourers succumbed to waterborne and respiratory diseases.
Q13: Miasma theory ("bad air")
Most Victorian doctors believed decomposing matter poisoned the atmosphere, ignoring contaminated drinking water supplies until John Snow’s 1854 discovery.
Q14: He mapped cholera deaths clustering around the Broad Street water pump and proved removing the pump handle halted the epidemic
Snow proved that cholera was an ingested waterborne contagion caused by cesspool sewage leaking directly into public drinking wells.
Q15: The Great Stink of London
When intense heat turned the sewage-choked River Thames outside Parliament intolerably foul, politicians fled committee rooms and funded intercepting sewers.
Q16: He argued that preventable filth made breadwinners sick, creating widows and orphans who drained local Poor Law tax rates, making sanitation economically rational
Chadwick convinced reluctant laissez-faire ratepayers that investing in clean piped water and arterial sewers would actually lower municipal poor relief costs.
Enquiry 3: Swing Riots to Chartism (Q17–Q24)
Q17: "Captain Swing"
The fictitious "Captain Swing" embodied the collective fury of starving agricultural labourers fighting wage cuts and mechanised unemployment.
Q18: Threshing machines
Threshing machines replaced winter hand-flailing, robbing farm labourers of their only dependable winter wages and plunging families into destitution.
Q19: It sentenced 101 men to penal transportation to Australia, imprisoned hundreds, and publicly hanged 19-year-old labourer Henry Cook
The government made a ruthless example of Hampshire workers, deporting skilled breadwinners and hanging Cook for knocking off a magistrate’s hat.
Q20: The Great Reform Act 1832
The 1832 Act enfranchised urban industrial towns like Birmingham and Manchester, but retained high property qualifications that excluded working men.
Q21: The People’s Charter
The Charter set out Six Points (universal male suffrage, secret ballot, no property qualification, annual parliaments, equal constituencies, salaries for MPs).
Q22: William Lovett advocated peaceful "Moral Force" through education and petitions; O’Connor threatened "Physical Force" and revolutionary general strikes
Moral Force Chartists sought constitutional legitimacy through moral persuasion, while Physical Force Chartists prepared pike drills and insurrection.
Q23: Over 5,000 armed Welsh coal miners marched on the Westgate Hotel to liberate Chartist prisoners; soldiers opened fire, killing 22 demonstrators
The Newport Rising was the last major armed insurrection on British soil, resulting in high-treason convictions and cementing the state’s fear of revolution.
Q24: Five of the Six Points (all except annual parliaments) were progressively adopted into British law between 1867 and 1918, building modern democracy
Chartism educated millions in political organising, creating the trade union and democratic foundations that eventually enfranchised the entire adult population.

Enquiry 4: Warrior & 1857 Rebellion (Q25–Q32)
Q25: The East India Company (EIC)
Founded in 1600, the EIC evolved from a commercial spice trader into an armed sovereign corporate empire collecting taxes from over 150 million Indians.
Q26: The new Enfield rifle paper cartridges were rumored to be greased with animal tallow made from sacred cow and forbidden pig fat
Biting the greased cartridges violated the religious laws of Hindu and Muslim soldiers, confirming fears that Britain intended forced Christian conversion.
Q27: The Doctrine of Lapse
Enforced aggressively by Governor-General Dalhousie, the policy annexed independent kingdoms (like Jhansi, Satara, and Awadh) when a ruler died without a biological son.
Q28: Rani Lakshmibai
Refusing to surrender her annexed state, Rani Lakshmibai became the supreme icon of armed anti-colonial resistance during the 1857 Rebellion.
Q29: HMS Warrior
Carrying 4.5-inch wrought iron armour plates and heavy steam engines, HMS Warrior instantaneously rendered every wooden battlefleet in the world obsolete.
Q30: It flooded Indian markets with cheap industrial textiles, destroying ancient domestic handloom industries and impoverishing millions of weavers
Britain dismantled Indian manufacturing monopolies, converting India into a raw cotton supplier and a captive consumer of British factory goods.
Q31: The East India Company was completely abolished and direct imperial rule was transferred to the British Crown under the British Raj
Parliament stripped the private corporation of sovereign power, placing India under a Secretary of State and declaring Queen Victoria monarch of India.
Q32: HMS Warrior showcased industrial metallurgical triumph and global Pax Britannica, while the 1857 Rebellion exposed violent colonial military extraction
Victorian Britain celebrated technological genius and naval protection, but that supremacy was maintained through brutal suppression and corporate exploitation.
Enquiry 5: The Victorian Verdict (Q33–Q40)
Q33: The Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations
Championed by Prince Albert, the 1851 exhibition showcased 100,000 exhibits to 6 million visitors, proclaiming Britain the "Workshop of the World".
Q34: Charles Booth
Booth’s colour-coded Life and Labour of the People in London proved mathematically that massive poverty was caused by low wages and illness, not moral failings.
Q35: Seebohm Rowntree
Rowntree established that 28% of York’s population lived below the basic nutritional poverty line, providing statistical ammunition for social reform.
Q36: Real wages increased, manufactured goods became cheap and abundant, railways democratised travel, and life expectancy rose in the late Victorian era
Optimists point to long-term gains: technological literacy, rising real wages by the 1850s, statutory welfare reforms, and expanding democratic rights.
Q37: Catastrophic declines in urban life expectancy, destruction of artisan independence, horrific child exploitation, and psychological alienation in factories
Pessimists argue that for at least two generations, working-class Britons sacrificed their health, family lives, and independence to enrich capitalist industrialists.
Q38: Parliament enacted Factory Acts limiting working hours, banned underground child mine labour (1842), and enforced public health sanitation (1875)
Faced with working-class rebellion and cholera, Victorian governments gradually abandoned extreme laissez-faire in favour of the modern regulatory state.
Q39: It required Cort’s wrought iron rails, Watt’s rotary steam power, and coal fuel, connecting provincial industrial towns to global ocean ports
Railways unified Britain’s domestic market, slashed transport costs by 90%, allowed rapid fresh food transit to cities, and accelerated national postal communication.
Q40: It permanently ended humanity’s dependence on animal muscle and organic energy, unleashing fossil-fuel industrialisation that remade politics, class, and global geopolitics
The shift from organic biology to fossilised thermal energy permanently altered human social organisation, living standards, environmental ecology, and imperial power.

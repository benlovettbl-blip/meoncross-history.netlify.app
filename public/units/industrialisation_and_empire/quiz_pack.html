Enquiry 1: Iron & Steam: Henry Cort (Q1–Q8)
Q1: 1769
Watt’s 1769 patent dramatically increased fuel efficiency and transformed coal into continuous rotative power, liberating factories from river valleys.
Q2: Molten metal flowed into sand moulds resembling suckling pigs; raw coal contaminated it with sulphur, leaving it brittle as glass
Sulphur-rich pit coal made British pig iron brittle and prone to shattering under high steam pressure or naval artillery shock.
Q3: Funtley (Fontley), near Fareham
Cort leased Funtley Mill in 1775 to exploit River Meon water power and direct tidal water access down Fareham Creek into Portsmouth Dockyard.
Q4: An arched brick ceiling deflected pure radiant heat onto the iron hearth, keeping smoke and sulphur separate from the metal
The reverberatory design acted like a giant radiant oven, burning away carbon impurities while preventing sulphur contamination from the burning coal.
Q5: They continuously squeezed out liquid slag and rolled uniform bars in seconds rather than requiring hours of slow manual hammering
Hydraulic grooved rollers mechanized the consolidation of wrought iron, standardising naval dimensions and multiplying output fifteen-fold.
Q6: Master smiths cold-twisted bars into corkscrews, bent rods double, and forged anchor rings with 20-pound sledgehammers without cracking
Admiralty trials proved Funtley iron equaled Sweden’s finest Orgrounds iron, shattering Britain’s dangerous reliance on Baltic imports.
Q7: Portsmouth was the world’s largest naval consumer of wrought iron, providing an immediate military market, naval trial facilities, and tidal transport
Without the Royal Navy’s urgent demand for reliable ship bolts and anchors, Cort would have lacked the capital, testing facilities, or market to develop puddling.
Q8: They revealed that enslaved Black metallurgists operated grooved rollers years earlier, proving the Industrial Revolution was tied to Atlantic colonial networks
Dr Jenny Bulstrode showed that Jamaican ironworks run by enslaved Africans used grooved rollers before Cort, challenging the traditional "lone English genius" narrative.
Enquiry 2: Factory System & Child Labour (Q9–Q16)
Q9: Scavengers and piecers
Scavengers crawled beneath active, whirring machinery to sweep up loose cotton fibres, while piecers tied broken threads at constant risk of mutilation.
Q10: Byssinosis (known as "mill fever" or brown lung disease)
Fine cotton fibres choked children’s lungs, causing chronic coughing, permanent respiratory damage, and premature death among textile operatives.
Q11: The Sadler Committee Report (1832)
Michael Sadler’s parliamentary report shocked Victorian Britain by documenting horrific mill cruelty, paving the way for the Factory Act of 1833.
Q12: The Factory Act of 1833
The 1833 Act established the principle of state regulation, limiting children aged 9–13 to eight hours of daily work and appointing four inspectors nationwide.
Q13: "Strapping" with heavy leather belts
Overlookers, whose own pay depended on output targets, ruthlessly beat drowsy children to prevent machine slowdowns and enforce relentless factory discipline.
Q14: The Mines Act 1842
Lord Ashley’s inquiry exposed half-naked young trappers and hurriers chained to coal carts in dark, flooded mines, prompting immediate parliamentary prohibition.
Q15: Laissez-faire capitalism
Industrialists argued that Parliament had no right to interfere with "freedom of contract" or trade profits, insisting state regulations would ruin British commerce.
Q16: Workers surrendered personal autonomy to the relentless mechanical pace of steam engines and the strict schedule of the factory clock
In domestic workshops, artisans controlled their own pace; factories imposed punitive fines, locked gates, and continuous machine toil for 12 to 14 hours daily.
Enquiry 3: Slums, Cholera & Sanitation (Q17–Q24)
Q17: Cholera
Cholera caused sudden, fatal dehydration through diarrhoea and vomiting, killing tens of thousands in unsewered, overcrowded industrial slums.
Q18: Deadly combinations of open cesspools, contaminated well water, unventilated back-to-back cellars, typhus, and exhausting factory toil
Rapid, unregulated urban migration created catastrophic sanitary conditions, where infants and labourers succumbed to waterborne and respiratory diseases.
Q19: Miasma theory ("bad air")
Most Victorian doctors believed decomposing matter poisoned the atmosphere, ignoring contaminated drinking water supplies until John Snow’s 1854 discovery.
Q20: He mapped cholera deaths clustering around the Broad Street water pump and proved removing the pump handle halted the epidemic
Snow proved that cholera was an ingested waterborne contagion caused by cesspool sewage leaking directly into public drinking wells.
Q21: He argued that preventable filth made breadwinners sick, creating widows and orphans who drained local Poor Law tax rates, making sanitation economically rational
Chadwick convinced reluctant laissez-faire ratepayers that investing in clean piped water and arterial sewers would actually lower municipal poor relief costs.
Q22: The Great Stink of London
When intense heat turned the sewage-choked River Thames outside Parliament intolerably foul, politicians fled committee rooms and funded intercepting sewers.
Q23: Sir Joseph Bazalgette
Bazalgette’s brick-arched intercepting sewers carried London’s waste eastward to the Thames estuary, permanently eliminating cholera from the British capital.
Q24: It made clean water supply, sewer connection, and street lighting compulsory for all local councils, ending extreme laissez-faire in municipal governance
The 1875 Act established the modern public health framework, recognizing that safeguarding citizen health was a mandatory duty of civil government.

Enquiry 4: Peterloo, Swing & Tolpuddle (Q25–Q32)
Q25: The Peterloo Massacre
Magistrates panicked and sent sabre-wielding yeomanry into a peaceful crowd at St Peter’s Field listening to radical orator Henry Hunt demand votes for working men.
Q26: "Captain Swing"
The fictitious "Captain Swing" embodied the collective fury of starving agricultural labourers fighting wage cuts and mechanised unemployment.
Q27: Threshing machines
Threshing machines replaced winter hand-flailing, robbing farm labourers of their only dependable winter wages and plunging families into destitution.
Q28: It sentenced 101 men to penal transportation to Australia, imprisoned hundreds, and publicly hanged 19-year-old labourer Henry Cook
The government made a ruthless example of Hampshire workers, deporting skilled breadwinners and hanging Cook for knocking off a magistrate’s hat.
Q29: The Tolpuddle Martyrs
Led by George Loveless, the labourers were prosecuted under an obsolete 1797 mutiny law against "unlawful oaths", triggering massive trade union protests.
Q30: A depopulated rural voting district (like Newtown on the Isle of Wight) that sent two MPs to Parliament while industrial cities like Manchester had none
Wealthy landowners bought and sold pocket boroughs to control Parliament, excluding millions of urban factory workers from democratic representation.
Q31: It enfranchised industrial cities and middle-class property owners, but retained a £10 property qualification that deliberately excluded working men
Working men felt cheated by the 1832 settlement, realizing that the middle class had secured power while leaving the working majority disenfranchised.
Q32: Isolated riots and local strikes were crushed by military force; only securing parliamentary votes could change laws and protect workers’ rights
The harsh lessons of Peterloo, Winchester Assizes, and Tolpuddle proved that industrial workers required constitutional power, directly inspiring Chartism.
Enquiry 5: Chartism & Democracy (Q33–Q40)
Q33: The People’s Charter
The Charter set out Six Points (universal male suffrage, secret ballot, no property qualification, annual parliaments, equal constituencies, salaries for MPs).
Q34: Annual elections made MPs accountable to voters, while salaries allowed working-class men without private fortunes to serve in Parliament
Without state salaries, only wealthy landowners and factory magnates could afford to serve as MPs, preserving elite monopoly over lawmaking.
Q35: William Lovett advocated peaceful "Moral Force" through education and petitions; O’Connor threatened "Physical Force" and revolutionary general strikes
Moral Force Chartists sought constitutional legitimacy through moral persuasion, while Physical Force Chartists prepared pike drills and insurrection.
Q36: Over 5,000 armed Welsh coal miners marched on the Westgate Hotel to liberate Chartist prisoners; soldiers opened fire, killing 22 demonstrators
The Newport Rising was the last major armed insurrection on British soil, resulting in high-treason convictions and cementing the state’s fear of revolution.
Q37: The Plug Plot Riots (1842 General Strike)
Sparked by wage cuts, half a million factory workers and miners across Lancashire and Yorkshire struck for both higher pay and the People’s Charter.
Q38: The Secret Ballot Act 1872
Prior to 1872, voting was conducted in public on open hustings; the secret ballot allowed workers to vote their conscience without fear of eviction or sacking.
Q39: The Second Reform Act (1867) and Third Reform Act (1884)
The 1867 Act gave votes to urban male householders, doubling the electorate to 2 million; the 1884 Act extended equal voting rights to rural labourers.
Q40: Five of the Six Points (all except annual parliaments) were progressively adopted into British law between 1867 and 1918, creating modern democracy
Chartism educated millions in political organising, creating the trade union and democratic foundations that eventually enfranchised the entire adult population.

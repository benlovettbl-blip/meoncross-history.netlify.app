Question 3: Historiographical Interpretations [12 marks • 15 mins]

HISTORIOGRAPHICAL ANALYSIS
Interpretation 1 • Historian Peter Fryer, Staying Power: The History of Black People in Britain (1984)
“The Windrush pioneers arrived with high hopes, believing they were returning to their ancestral Mother Country as proud British subjects. Instead, they were met with cold weather, squalid housing, and an open colour bar. Landlords slammed doors in their faces, and employers relegated skilled tradesmen to the most menial jobs. Yet through mutual-aid 'Pardner' schemes, church fellowships, and sheer resilience, they built vibrant communities that enriched the cultural life of the nation.”
Interpretation 2 • Sociologist Sheila Patterson, Dark Strangers: A Study of West Indians in London (1963)
“Post-war British society was not intentionally hostile, but rather deeply insular and bewildered by sudden cultural diversity. The initial friction in housing and employment was primarily a clash of customs, dialects, and social expectations rather than systemic racial prejudice. Given time, full employment, and gradual assimilation into British working-class neighbourhoods, Commonwealth migrants would be absorbed harmoniously into the fabric of British life.”
Question 3(b): Study Interpretations 1 and 2. How do they differ about the reception faced by the Windrush Generation in post-war Britain? [4 marks]

1897 • Formation of the NUWSS & Constitutional Suffrage Enquiry 1

Millicent Fawcett unites regional suffrage societies into the National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies (NUWSS). Campaigning with peaceful petitions, parliamentary lobbying, and mass marches, they prove female political maturity.

1903–1913 • Formation of the WSPU & The "Cat and Mouse Act" Enquiry 1

Emmeline Pankhurst founds the Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU) under "Deeds, not words". Suffragettes adopt militant civil disobedience, window-smashing, hunger strikes, and force-feeding under the 1913 Cat and Mouse Act.

Feb 1918 • Representation of the People Act 1918 Enquiry 1

Following the decisive mobilization of over 1.5 million women in munitions factories ("Canary Girls"), transport, and the Land Army during World War I, Parliament grants women over thirty meeting property criteria the right to vote.

Dec 1942 • The Beveridge Report & Slaying the "Five Giants" Enquiry 2

Sir William Beveridge publishes his revolutionary blueprint on Social Insurance, selling over 630,000 copies. He pledges to conquer the Five Giants—Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, and Idleness—"from the cradle to the grave".

Living Timeline • Part 2: The NHS, Windrush, Civil Rights & Nuclear Age (1948–1982)

Living Timeline Dual-Coding Protocol: Complete each milestone sketchpad using dual-coding (combining visual storytelling symbols, causal arrows, and dates) to master the chronological spine across the centre-fold.
5 July 1948 • Foundation of the National Health Service (NHS) Enquiry 2

Minister of Health Aneurin Bevan opens the NHS at Park Hospital, Davyhulme. Overcoming fierce BMA resistance by compromising with consultants, Bevan establishes healthcare funded by taxation and completely free at the point of delivery.

22 June 1948 • Arrival of HMT Empire Windrush at Tilbury Enquiry 3

The Empire Windrush docks carrying 492 Caribbean passengers invited to help reconstruct post-war Britain. Arriving as citizens under the British Nationality Act 1948, they confront the colour bar, building mutual-aid Pardner networks.

1963 • The Bristol Bus Boycott & Race Relations Acts Enquiry 4

Paul Stephenson and Roy Hackett lead a four-month passenger boycott against the Bristol Omnibus Company's refusal to hire Black or Asian bus crews. Capitulation on 28 August 1963 paves the way for the Race Relations Acts of 1965 and 1968.

1958–1982 • CND, Aldermaston Marches & Greenham Common Enquiry 5

The Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament (CND) launches the annual Aldermaston Marches, using Gerald Holtom's peace symbol. Nuclear anxiety inspires civil defence bunkers like York, while 30,000 women "Embrace the Base" at Greenham Common in 1982.

Between 1900 and the late 20th century, British democracy was transformed by civic protest, the welfare settlement, and multi-ethnic community resilience. 9/16

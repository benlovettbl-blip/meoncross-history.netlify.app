Enquiry 01: Outbreak & Gas Masks Q1–4
Q1: 3 September 1939 (at 11:15am) (Chamberlain broadcast from 10 Downing Street after Hitler ignored Britain's ultimatum to withdraw from Poland.)
Q2: 38 million gas masks (Fearing poison gas bombs remembered from the First World War, the government issued gas masks to every citizen.)
Q3: To prevent German bomber pilots from seeing streetlights or illuminated landmarks at night (Every window had to be covered with heavy black curtains so enemy bombers flying in darkness could not navigate.)
Q4: Children carried the cardboard box on their shoulder everywhere and practiced wearing respirators in regular drills (Pupils could not enter school or board buses without their box, practicing reading and writing while wearing rubber masks.)
Enquiry 02: The Evacuation Experience Q5–8
Q5: Operation Pied Piper (In four days, 1.5 million schoolchildren, teachers, and mothers with babies were evacuated to rural reception areas.)
Q6: So billeting officers and volunteers could immediately identify bewildered children at busy railway stations (Labels recorded each child's name, home address, school, and destination station to keep classes and families organized.)
Q7: The Billeting Officer (Billeting officers inspected village cottages and assigned children to foster families, often in local village halls.)
Q8: Children discovered green fields and farm animals, but suffered deep homesickness separated from parents (Many city children had never seen cows or orchards, but missed their mothers and fathers with only letters to stay in touch.)
Enquiry 03: The Kindertransport Rescue Q9–12
Q9: The November Pogrom (Kristallnacht) (After widespread violent Nazi attacks against Jewish homes and synagogues, Britain permitted unaccompanied child refugees.)
Q10: Nearly 10,000 children (approx. 9,354) (Rescue trains brought children from Germany, Austria, and Czechoslovakia to the Netherlands and by ferry to Harwich.)
Q11: Only one small suitcase (Nazi authorities strictly limited children to one bag with clothes and keepsakes; no money or valuables were allowed.)
Q12: To allow British volunteers and foster families to identify the correct child upon arrival in London (Most children spoke only German or Czech; their numbered neck tags matched records held at Liverpool Street Station.)
Enquiry 04: The Blitz & Shelters Q13–16
Q13: 57 consecutive nights (Between September 1940 and May 1941, German bombers attacked London and industrial ports to crush civilian morale.)
Q14: The Anderson shelter (Over 3 million Anderson shelters were distributed free to low-income families to bury in back gardens.)
Q15: The Morrison shelter (Designed by Herbert Morrison, this steel cage protected families in homes without gardens from falling debris.)
Q16: On Underground tube station platforms (Civilians camped along deep tube platforms where dozens of feet of earth protected them from explosive bomb blasts.)
Enquiry 05: Blackout & ARP Wardens Q17–20
Q17: Air Raid Precautions (ARP wardens were civilian volunteers who patrolled streets, directed people to shelters, and gave first aid.)
Q18: "Put that light out!" (Wardens patrolled streets on foot and bicycle checking for light leaks that could guide enemy bombers.)
Q19: Curbs, lampposts, tree trunks, and car bumpers (Because streetlights and normal car headlights were banned, white paint prevented accidents in total darkness.)
Q20: A rising and falling wailing siren (warning), followed by a continuous single-pitch tone (All Clear) (Sirens were mounted on police stations and town halls to give people enough time to enter shelters.)
Enquiry 06: Rationing & Dig for Victory Q21–24
Q21: German U-boats sank merchant cargo ships in the Atlantic, causing dangerous food shortages (Britain imported 55 million tonnes of food yearly; rationing guaranteed equal, fair shares for every civilian.)
Q22: The Ration Book (Coupons were registered with local shopkeepers who stamped or clipped them when buying meat, butter, and sugar.)
Q23: "Dig for Victory" (The government urged families to grow potatoes and cabbages, producing over 3 million tonnes of domestic food.)
Q24: Because waste squandered supplies that Merchant Navy sailors had risked their lives to bring across the ocean (Wasting food was treated as helping the enemy; kitchen scraps were collected in pig swill bins to feed livestock.)
Enquiry 07: Women at War Q25–28
Q25: "Land Girls" (Land Girls worked 50-hour weeks milking cows, ploughing fields, driving tractors, and harvesting food across Britain.)
Q26: The "Lumber Jills" (Women’s Timber Corps) (Over 6,000 women operated heavy crosscut saws and worked in sawmills to produce timber for pit props and construction.)
Q27: Assembling aircraft in factories, driving ambulances, operating radar, or codebreaking at Bletchley Park (Women entered heavy industry, engineering, and the auxiliary armed services (ATS, WAAF, WRNS) in record numbers.)
Q28: It proved women could master skilled, demanding jobs equally with men, accelerating the move toward equality (Earning independent wages and running vital national infrastructure challenged outdated domestic stereotypes.)
Enquiry 08: Pre-War Life & Lost Rights Q29–32
Q29: Over 9 million Jewish people (Jewish families lived in 21 European countries, attending ordinary schools, playing sports, and running businesses.)
Q30: A Schultüte (school cone) (The Schultüte celebrated entering school and was cherished by German children of all backgrounds before Nazi rule.)
Q31: November 1938 (Following the November Pogrom, Nazi decrees expelled all Jewish children from state schools into segregation.)
Q32: Jewish children were banned from sports clubs, swimming pools, public parks, riding bicycles, and owning pets (Step-by-step exclusion isolated Jewish children from friends and public life long before deportations began.)
Enquiry 09: Bletchley Park Codebreakers Q33–36
Q33: The Enigma machine (Enigma used rotating wheels and a plugboard to scramble text into billions of mathematical code combinations.)
Q34: Bletchley Park (Station X) (Over 10,000 codebreakers, mathematicians, and service personnel worked in wooden huts intercepting enemy radio signals.)
Q35: Alan Turing (Turing and his team designed electromechanical Bombe machines that tested rotor positions in minutes.)
Q36: About 75% were women, and their work was kept secret for over 30 years (until the 1970s) (Women operated complex Bombe machines and decoded messages, strictly obeying the Official Secrets Act.)
Enquiry 010: Rescuers, VE Day & Legacy Q37–40
Q37: Nicholas Winton (later Sir Nicholas Winton) (Winton cancelled a Swiss holiday in December 1938, opened an office in Prague, and arranged British foster homes.)
Q38: A person who takes active moral action to help or defend others in danger rather than remaining a bystander (Upstanders like Winton, Trevor Chadwick, and volunteer foster parents actively stepped forward to rescue innocent children.)
Q39: Victory in Europe Day, celebrated on 8 May 1945 (Following Germany's surrender, a national holiday was declared with joyful street parties and community feasts.)
Q40: To honour those who defended freedom, protect democracy, and learn from past atrocities to prevent prejudice (Remembrance ensures younger generations understand that freedom and human dignity must be protected.)

Core KS2 Historical Terms
ARP: Air Raid Precautions volunteers enforcing blackout.
Kindertransport: Rescue of 10,000 child refugees.
Anderson Shelter: Steel shelter buried in gardens.
Rationing: Fair sharing of food during shortages.
Enigma: German cipher machine cracked at Bletchley.
Upstander: Person taking action to help those in peril.

[1.1] (Source A) In November 1558, twenty-five-year-old Elizabeth Tudor ascended the throne following the death of Mary I. She inherited an impoverished realm burdened by an astronomical £300,000 Crown debt, largely incurred through costly foreign wars in France.

Seeking immediate stability, Elizabeth appointed her most trusted advisor, Sir William Cecil, as Principal Secretary on accession day, establishing a partnership that directed English governance for four decades.

Q2. Why did Elizabeth’s appointment of moderate, experienced advisers like William Cecil prove critical in stabilising her royal government in 1558?
[2.1] (Source B) Tudor monarchical governance was rooted in the concept of Divine Right, but the Crown possessed no standing army or professional police force. Sovereign power was instead maintained through an executive 19-man Privy Council comprising senior noblemen and statesmen meeting daily to guide defense, finance, and diplomacy.

[2.2] To bind the landed aristocracy and ambitious courtiers to her rule, Elizabeth deployed a sophisticated system of royal patronage, distributing titles, offices, leases, and lucrative commercial monopolies. By balancing rival political factions at court—most notably between the cautious William Cecil and her favourite, Lord Robert Dudley—the Queen prevented any single noble clique from dominating royal decision-making.
[3.1] Elizabeth’s right to rule was challenged by deep-seated sixteenth-century patriarchal prejudice and religious doubts over her legitimacy. Roman Catholic canon law regarded Henry VIII’s marriage to Anne Boleyn as illegal and bigamous, because Pope Clement VII had refused to annul Henry’s first marriage to Catherine of Aragon. Furthermore, following Anne’s execution in 1536, Parliament passed the Second Succession Act, legally declaring Elizabeth illegitimate.

[3.2] As an unmarried female ruler—a Queen Regnant—contemporary European society considered her inherently incapable of commanding military forces or governing without a husband. Consequently, both Parliament and the Privy Council exerted relentless pressure upon Elizabeth to marry and produce a Protestant heir, a dilemma she navigated by declaring herself married to the English realm.
[4.1] Beyond Whitehall, the Crown depended entirely on local authorities to execute royal policy across England's counties. County administration was overseen by Lords Lieutenant, wealthy magnates responsible for raising local militias during emergencies, and unpaid Justices of the Peace (JPs) drawn from the country gentry. These JPs enforced statutory law, collected taxes, repaired roads, and punished petty crime at quarterly sessions.

[4.2] Meanwhile, Parliament was an occasional advisory body called only when the monarch required extraordinary taxation (subsidies) or statute legislation. While MPs gathered only nine times during Elizabeth’s forty-five-year reign, parliamentary consent was constitutionally mandatory to authorize Crown taxes, granting the Commons significant financial leverage.
[5.1] In foreign affairs, England stood isolated and militarily encircled by Catholic powers. In January 1558, French forces had captured Calais, England’s last continental possession held since 1347, stripping the realm of its vital trade gateway and strategic Channel outpost.

[5.2] The danger was compounded on the northern border by the historic Auld Alliance between France and Scotland. Scotland was ruled by French Catholic regent Mary of Guise, who stationed French garrisons on English borders, while her daughter Mary, Queen of Scots, married to the French Dauphin, openly quartered the English royal arms on her heraldic crest, asserting herself as the lawful monarch of England.

The northern and western shires—dominated by conservative Catholic noble families—remained deeply committed to traditional Latin worship and papal authority. Conversely, London and the southeastern ports had embraced radical Protestant Reformation doctrines, fuelled by merchant trade with Europe.

[2.1] (Source A) Passed by Parliament in May 1559, the Act of Supremacy re-established England’s formal break with the Papacy in Rome. In a deliberate political compromise to appease Catholics who held the Pope as the only earthly head of the Church, Elizabeth rejected Henry VIII’s title of 'Supreme Head' and assumed the title of Supreme Governor of the Church of England.

[4.1] (Source B) In the summer of 1559, William Cecil issued 57 Royal Injunctions to provide precise administrative instructions for the day-to-day enforcement of the religious settlement.

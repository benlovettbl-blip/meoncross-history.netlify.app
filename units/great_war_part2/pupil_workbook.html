The Great War: 1914–1919

Industrial Slaughter, Global Sacrifice, Total War & The Flawed Peace
Visual Decoding • 4 Core Features John Singer Sargent (1919)

Commissioned by the British War Memorials Committee, Sargent depicted the aftermath of a mustard gas barrage on the Western Front in August 1918:

1. The Blind Leading:
Blindfolded soldiers walk toward triage, hands resting on comrades’ shoulders in mutual survival.
2. Carpet of Casualties:
Dozens of gassed infantrymen lie packed on duckboards, clutching water bottles in agony.
3. Chemical Modernity:
Mustard gas blisters and blinds indiscriminately, subverting classical heroic warfare.
4. Distant Football:
In the distance under a pale sun, troops play football, highlighting the surreal routine of total war.
Mustard Gas
Industrial horror on Western Front.
Dressing Station
Overwhelmed medical triage lines.
Comradeship
Human solidarity amidst slaughter.
Disciplinary Significance: Sargent’s monumental 6-metre canvas captures the human cost of modern industrial slaughter, contrasting classical heroic friezes with the agonizing realities of chemical warfare.
Disciplinary Hinge Question: Evaluation
Does Sargent depict the Great War as a tragic triumph of human endurance and comradeship, or as an unsparing indictment of senseless industrial slaughter?

Enquiry Writing Task [8 marks • 10 mins]

HISTORIOGRAPHICAL ANALYSIS
Interpretation 1 • Lord Curzon, British War Cabinet Minister, Speech at Royal Albert Hall (1917)
“The Indian Army and the troops from our dominions have rushed forward with magnificent devotion to defend the King-Emperor. Side by side with their British brothers-in-arms, they have shed their blood on the plains of Flanders, Gallipoli, and Egypt. This imperial brotherhood proves that the British Empire is held together not by force, but by mutual love, loyalty, and common sacrifice.”
Interpretation 2 • David Olusoga, Modern British Historian, The World’s War (2014)
“Over four million non-white men were mobilised as combatants and labourers by Britain. Yet their treatment was defined by systemic racism. Black and Asian soldiers were barred from becoming officers, paid a fraction of British wages, and subjected to brutal floggings. After 1918, the Imperial War Graves Commission deliberately refused to give African dead individual headstones. Their sacrifice was whitewashed from British national memory.”
Task A: Explain how Interpretations 1 and 2 differ about the experiences of colonial troops. [4 marks]

PART C

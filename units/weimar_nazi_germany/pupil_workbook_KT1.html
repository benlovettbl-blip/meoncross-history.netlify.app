9 NOV 1918 • Abdication of Kaiser Wilhelm II & Proclamation of Republic Key Topic 1.1

Facing military collapse and naval mutiny at Kiel, Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicates and flees to Holland. Social Democrat leader Friedrich Ebert takes power as Chancellor, establishing Germany’s first parliamentary democracy.

28 JUN 1919 • Signing of the Treaty of Versailles (The Diktat) Key Topic 1.1

Weimar representatives sign the Versailles treaty under threat of immediate Allied invasion. Germany loses 13% of its European territory, its colonies, 100,000-man army limit, £6.6bn reparations, and accepts sole blame under Article 231.

JAN 1923 • French Occupation of the Ruhr & Passive Resistance Key Topic 1.2

When Germany defaults on timber and coal reparations, 60,000 French and Belgian troops occupy the Ruhr industrial heartland. Ebert calls for passive resistance, printing paper money to pay striking workers.

Living Timeline • Part 2: Chronological Anchors (Key Topic 1)

Instructions: Complete the dual-coding sketches to permanently anchor these chronological turning points in long-term memory.
NOV 1923 • Hyperinflation Peak & Introduction of Rentenmark Key Topic 1.2

The German mark collapses to 4.2 trillion marks per US dollar; life savings of the middle class are wiped out. Newly appointed Chancellor Gustav Stresemann burns the old currency and introduces the stable, land-backed Rentenmark.

AUG 1924 • The Dawes Plan Secures US Wall Street Loans Key Topic 1.3

Stresemann agrees to the Dawes Plan: annual reparations payments are reduced and US banks inject 800 million gold marks in foreign loans, kicking off Germany’s economic golden era.

OCT 1925 • The Locarno Pact & European Reintegration Key Topic 1.3

Germany voluntarily accepts its western borders with France and Belgium. The diplomatic breakthrough secures Germany’s entry into the League of Nations in 1926 and wins Stresemann the Nobel Peace Prize.

Archival & Conceptual Blueprint • Structure of the Weimar Constitution & Versailles Losses

1. Anatomy of the Weimar Constitution (August 1919): Democratic Architecture vs Built-in Flaws
👑 The President (Head of State): Elected every 7 years by all German adults. Appointed the Chancellor, controlled the armed forces, and could dissolve the Reichstag. Exercised Article 48 emergency powers.
🏛️ The Chancellor & Cabinet: Appointed by the President. Needed majority support in the Reichstag to pass everyday laws. Led government ministries and formulated state policy.
⚖️ The Reichstag (Parliament): Elected every 4 years by Proportional Representation. Passed laws, approved national taxation, and debated national issues. Over 20 separate parties sat in parliament.
🛡️ The Reichsrat & Fundamental Rights: Reichsrat represented 18 German regions (Länder). Bill of Rights guaranteed freedom of speech, freedom of religion, equality of women, and right to form trade unions.
2. Territorial, Military & Financial Penalties of the Treaty of Versailles (1919)
TERRITORIAL LOSSES 13% of European territory lost. Alsace-Lorraine returned to France; Polish Corridor given to Poland (cutting off East Prussia); Eupen-Malmedy to Belgium; Danzig made Free City.
MILITARY DISARMAMENT Army strictly limited to 100,000 long-term volunteers. Conscription banned. Navy capped at 6 battleships, zero submarines. Air force completely banned. Rhineland demilitarised.
REPARATIONS & WAR GUILT Article 231 (War Guilt Clause) forced Germany to accept sole blame for WW1. Inter-Allied Reparations Commission set total indemnity at £6.6 billion (132 billion gold marks) in 1921.

Master Knowledge Organiser • Key Topic 1: The Weimar Republic, 1918–29

1. Core Disciplinary Vocabulary & Conceptual Definitions
• Proportional Representation: Electoral system where parties gain seats precisely proportional to votes cast, causing endless unstable coalitions.
• Article 48: Emergency constitutional power allowing the President to bypass the Reichstag and rule by decree without democratic consent.
• Dolchstoßlegende: The toxic right-wing myth that the German army was undefeated in the field but stabbed in the back by democratic politicians.
• Diktat: A dictated peace; German term for Versailles because Germany was locked out of negotiations and forced to sign under threat of invasion.
• Hyperinflation: Catastrophic currency collapse in 1923 where paper marks became worthless and prices rose out of control.
• Rentenmark: Temporary stable currency introduced by Stresemann in Nov 1923, backed by German land and industrial mortgages.
• Dawes Plan (1924): Agreement scaling annual German reparations and injecting 800 million gold marks in American loans to restart industry.
• Bauhaus: Revolutionary modernist design school founded by Walter Gropius, prioritizing clean functionality over ornate decoration.
2. Essential Chronology
• 9 Nov 1918: Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicates; Republic declared
• 11 Nov 1918: Armistice signed in Compiègne forest
• Jan 1919: Spartacist Communist Uprising crushed in Berlin
• 28 Jun 1919: Treaty of Versailles signed by Weimar envoys
• Aug 1919: Weimar Constitution officially ratified
• Mar 1920: Kapp Putsch defeated by Berlin general strike
• Jan 1923: French and Belgian troops occupy the Ruhr
• Nov 1923: Hyperinflation peak; Stresemann issues Rentenmark
• Aug 1924: Dawes Plan approved; Wall Street loans flow
• Oct 1925: Locarno Treaties signed; western borders secured
3. Key Historical Figures Matrix
• Friedrich Ebert: SPD leader and first President of the Weimar Republic (1919–25); navigated early revolutionary crises.
• Gustav Stresemann: Chancellor (1923) and Foreign Minister (1923–29); ended hyperinflation, secured Dawes Plan and Locarno.
• Rosa Luxemburg: Co-founder of the Spartacus League; brilliant Marxist theorist murdered by Freikorps in Jan 1919.
• Wolfgang Kapp: Right-wing nationalist civil servant who led the failed military putsch against Berlin in March 1920.
• Walter Gropius: Visionary architect who founded the Bauhaus design movement in Weimar and Dessau.
• Marlene Dietrich: Iconic German actress who symbolized the glamour and liberated culture of the Golden Twenties.

Grade 9 Exam Technique Masterclass • Pearson Edexcel Paper 3 Rubrics

1. The 4/4 Mark Formula: Inference from Source A [4 Marks • 5 Mins] TARGET: 4 / 4
Mark Scheme Law: Two separate inferences required. Each inference must state: (1) what you can deduce beyond surface description + (2) a verbatim quote or precise detail from Source A supporting your deduction. Never quote without explaining what it means!
2. The 12/12 Mark Formula: Explain Why [12 Marks • 18 Mins] TARGET: 11–12 / 12 (LEVEL 4)
Three Linked PEEL Paragraphs: Use both provided stimulus bullet points + bring in ONE distinct self-selected knowledge point. Every paragraph must feature causal links: "Consequently, As a direct consequence, This meant that..." Connect your factors: explain how economic crisis enabled political radicalism!
3. The 8/8 Mark Formula: How Useful are Sources B & C [8 Marks • 12 Mins] TARGET: 7–8 / 8 (LEVEL 3)
COP Formula (Content, Origin, Purpose): Evaluate Source B then Source C. Interrogate content using own knowledge; evaluate provenance (who wrote it, when, why, and who was the intended audience). Never dismiss a source as "biased"; explain how bias makes it useful as evidence of propaganda or fear!
4. The 16/16 Mark Formula: Evaluative Essay [16 Marks + 4 SPaG • 25 Mins] TARGET: 15–16 / 16 (LEVEL 4)
Criteria-Led Judgment Architecture:
• Opening: State your sustained thesis and establish your evaluative criteria (e.g. legal illusion vs daily social reality).
• Section 1: Support the targeted Interpretation using its quotes and your own historical evidence.
• Section 2: Evaluate the competing Interpretation using counter-evidence from your own knowledge.
• Conclusion: Deliver a justified verdict explaining why one historian’s emphasis is more persuasive.
14/16 "Grade 9 Interpretations: Historians don't disagree by accident; their evidence pools and focus differ."

Instructions: As you study each enquiry lesson, illustrate the milestone and record your key notes in the open space below.
c. 1250 • The Church Monopoly: Galen & Monastic Scriptoria Milestone 1

Hippocrates’ Four Humours and Galen’s Opposites dominate medical thinking. Because Galen believed every organ was designed by a single Creator, the Church adopts his texts as sacred doctrine, controlling all manuscript copying in monastic scriptoria.

1277 • The Imprisonment of Roger Bacon (Suppression of Experimentation) Milestone 2

Franciscan friar Roger Bacon is imprisoned by Church leaders for advocating firsthand observation and scientific experiments rather than unquestioned acceptance of ancient books. This harsh punishment warns scholars that challenging Galen is heresy.

c. 1300 • The Physician’s Toolkit: Uroscopy in the Matula & Zodiac Man Milestone 3

Physicians carry pocket Vademecum handbooks containing 20-shade urine charts and astrology wheels. Examining urine in a glass matula becomes the primary diagnostic method; doctors check the Zodiac Man to determine when to perform bloodletting.

c. 1320 • Humoural Therapeutics: Phlebotomy, Purging & Theriac Milestone 4

Treatments focus entirely on restoring humoural balance. Phlebotomy (bloodletting with fleams, cupping, or leeches) and purging (emetics and clysters) physically deplete patients. Apothecaries prepare Theriac (60+ ingredients including viper flesh) as a universal remedy.

Living Timeline • Part 2: Epidemic Catastrophe & Monastic Care (1345–c1500)

PAGE 3
Instructions: As you study each enquiry lesson, illustrate the milestone and record your key notes in the open space below.
1345 • The Great Planetary Conjunction in Aquarius Milestone 5

In March 1345, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn align in Aquarius. University of Paris physicians state this celestial conjunction drew up poisonous vapours from the earth, corrupting the air with deadly miasma, preparing Europe for the Black Death.

1348 • The Black Death Reaches Melcombe Regis, Dorset Milestone 6

In June 1348, the pestilence arrives on trade ships carrying black rats and fleas. The dual epidemic—bubonic plague (flea bites, agonizing buboes) and pneumonic plague (airborne coughing droplets)—kills 30–50% of England’s population within 18 months.

1349 • King Edward III’s Cleanliness Order & East Smithfield Mass Pits Milestone 7

Alarmed by rotting waste and stench, Edward III orders London streets cleared of dung to remove miasma. Churchyards overflow; emergency mass burial trenches are dug outside city walls at East Smithfield to bury hundreds of corpses daily.

c. 1400 • Monastic Hospitals: The Golden Age of ‘Care Not Cure’ Milestone 8

Over 1,200 hospitals run by monks and nuns operate in England (e.g. St Bartholomew’s, 1123). Patients receive clean bedding, warmth, food, and continuous prayer facing a chapel altar. Zero surgery or medical cure is attempted; lepers and plague victims are strictly barred.

Stimulus: Galen’s Theory of Opposites The diagnostic matula (uroscopy) (You must also use information of your own)
Structure Strip • 3-Paragraph Analytical Plan
1. LOGIC OF THE FOUR HUMOURS Explain how Hippocrates’ balance of blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile linked fluids to seasons and elements, providing a universal, sensible framework.
2. ACTIONABLE TREATMENT RULES Explain how Galen’s Theory of Opposites gave practical rules (curing excess cold/moist phlegm with hot/dry remedies), making the theory actionable for doctors.
3. UNIVERSAL DIAGNOSTIC TOOLS Explain how uroscopy (examining urine in a matula against a 20-shade wheel) and the Zodiac Man gave doctors diagnostic authority without physical examination.

Explain why humoural treatments such as bloodletting and purging remained the standard medical response to illness throughout the Middle Ages (c1250–c1500). [12 marks]
Stimulus: Phlebotomy (bloodletting) The Regimen Sanitatis (You must also use information of your own)
Structure Strip • 3-Paragraph Analytical Plan
1. HUMOURAL DRAINING Explain how doctors believed excess blood caused fevers and inflammation; describe methods (fleams into veins, cupping, live leeches) and why patients felt psychosomatic relief.
2. PURGING THE DIGESTIVE TRACT Explain how emetics (inducing vomiting) and laxatives/enemas (clysters) were used to violently clear corrupted humours from the stomach and intestines.
3. HERBAL THERIACS & REGIMEN Explain how apothecaries compounded antidotes like Theriac (60+ ingredients) and how physicians advised rich patients to follow the Regimen Sanitatis (diet, exercise, rest).

Question 5/6: Evaluative Essay [16+4 marks • 25 mins]

PAGE 13
‘The main reason why people failed to prevent the spread of the Black Death in 1348–49 was belief in supernatural causes.’ How far do you agree? Explain your answer. [16+4 marks]
Stimulus: Punishment from God Miasma (bad air) (You must also use information of your own)
Structure Strip • 3-Paragraph Analytical Plan
1. SUPERNATURAL CAUSES (AGREE) Explain how people believed God sent the plague to punish sin, prompting mass church gatherings, pilgrimages, and flagellants whipping themselves—accelerating contagion.
2. MIASMA & FLEAS (DISAGREE) Explain how belief in bad air led to futile measures (street fires, carrying sweet posies), while cramped timber housing allowed black rats and fleas to multiply unchecked.
3. CIVIC WEAKNESS (EVALUATION) Weigh the factors: neither physicians nor civic officials understood bacteria; Edward III’s 1349 street-cleaning order was powerless against rat fleas and coughing droplets.
Analytical Connectives: On the one hand, belief in supernatural causes was a primary reason because... • For instance, flagellants... • On the other hand, misdiagnosis of miasma and living conditions were vital because... • Crucially, authorities lacked understanding of Yersinia pestis... • In conclusion, while religious fatalism dictated reactions, the fundamental barrier was...
